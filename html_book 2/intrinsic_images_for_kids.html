Intrinsic images, and the "online $R_t$" question

Story first, no formulas. Then the one trap that decides the grade, then the trick the hint is pointing at, and only at the end the math. Three demos you can press along the way.

Moed A · 19/6/2022 · Question 3 · 13 points · INTRINSIC IMAGES

Suggest an online algorithm for computing the intrinsic reflectance image $R_t$ from a sequence $I_1, \dots, I_t$. The algorithm can store as many as 256 input images (or their equivalent in terms of memory footprint). Hint: the intensity values of a pixel in $I_1, \dots, I_t$ are in the range $[0,255]$.

1 · The story

The painted wall and the moving clouds

There is a wall with a picture painted on it. The paint never changes. It is the same at breakfast and at sunset.

All day, clouds drift past the sun. Sometimes a shadow lies on the left of the wall, sometimes on the right, sometimes there is no shadow at all. The light keeps changing.

You take a photo every minute for a whole day. Now: can you draw the picture on the wall, with no shadows at all — a photo the camera never actually took?

That shadow-free picture of the pure paint is the reflectance image $R$. The map of shadows and brightness is the illumination image $L$. Every photo you took is just those two multiplied together: paint × light.

Why one photo is not enough

A dark patch in one photo — is it dark paint, or white paint in shadow? A single photo cannot tell. One equation, two unknowns. Hopeless.

But a whole sequence can. Because the paint is the part that stays, and the shadows are the part that moves. Whatever stays put across the whole day is paint. Whatever comes and goes is light. That single sentence is the entire chapter.

So how do you decide "what stays"?

Take one single spot on the wall and write down its brightness in every photo. You get a long list of numbers. Most of the time that spot is in plain sunlight, so most of the numbers are nearly the same. Once in a while a cloud passes and the number drops hard.

You want the value that "stays". So:

  • Average? Bad. A few very dark shadow readings drag the average down. The answer gets bruised by every cloud that ever passed.
  • Minimum? Worse. It picks the darkest shadow ever seen — the answer is the worst moment of the day.
  • Middle value (median)? Perfect. Sort the numbers and take the one in the middle. A few crazy values at the ends do not move the middle at all. The middle is where the crowd is, and the crowd is the sunlit paint.

2 · The trap that decides the grade

The corner that is always dark

Imagine a corner of the wall that a big tree keeps shaded for most of the day. Its readings are dark in most photos. Take the middle value there and you get… dark.

So the "median of the photos" trick declares: "that corner is painted dark grey." Wrong. It is white paint that happens to sit in shade. The shadow got baked into the paint.

This is exactly the P and Q pixels in Weiss's Figure 4, and it is the single most common way to lose points on this question.

The fix: stop looking at brightness, look at changes

Instead of asking "how bright is this spot?", ask "how different is this spot from its neighbour?"

Why that helps, in two lines:

  • A shadow is big and smooth. Inside the shadow, every pixel is darkened by roughly the same amount, so neighbour-minus-neighbour is zero. A shadow only shows up at its border — a thin line, and that line moves with the cloud.
  • The paint's own edges — where the picture changes colour — are always in the same place, in every single photo.

So in the "differences" world: the paint's edges are permanent, and the shadow's edges are rare visitors that appear at a different place every time. Take the middle value of the differences and the visitors are voted out, while the permanent edges survive. That is the sparseness assumption, in plain words: a shadow's edges are rare; a shadow's darkness is not.

Then you rebuild the picture from its edges — walk across the wall adding up the differences, like reconstructing a staircase from the height of each step.

Say this sentence in the exam and you have the 13 points: the median is taken on the filtered (derivative) frames, not on the raw frames.

3 · What "online" means, and where this breaks

Online = photos arrive one at a time, you must output an answer after every photo, and you are not allowed to keep them all. Live video, not a folder on disk.

Walk through the recipe and ask, for each step, "could I do this with just the new photo in my hand?"

StepWhat it doesOnline?
1take the log of the new photo✔ needs only this photo
2compute the neighbour-differences of it✔ needs only this photo
3median over all photos so far✘ needs all of them
4rebuild the image from the edges✔ fixed recipe, no history
5undo the log → output $R_t$✔

Exactly one step is the problem: the median. The usual way to find a middle value is to keep all the numbers and sort them — which means memory that grows forever. After 10,000 frames you are storing 10,000 images. That is the whole question, and nothing else.

Answer strategy Do not rewrite Weiss. Say "everything is already online except the median", then spend your page fixing the median. That framing alone reads as a confident answer.

4 · Reading the hint

Counting votes without keeping the ballots

You need the middle value of a huge pile of numbers, but you are not allowed to keep the numbers.

Here is the thing: those numbers are not arbitrary. Pixel values are 0 to 255 — only 256 different values exist. So instead of writing down every number, keep 256 counters: "how many photos said 0, how many said 1, … how many said 255."

That is a histogram, and it never grows. One million photos, still 256 counters.

Now find the middle value without any sorting: walk the counters from left to right, adding them up, and stop the moment the running total passes half the number of photos. That counter is the median. Done.

The hint was the answer

"values are in $[0,255]$" → only 256 possible values → count them instead of storing them.
"you may store 256 images" → 256 counters per pixel — one image's worth of memory per counter. The exam is telling you the shape of the answer in both halves of the sentence.

You have already seen this exact trick — twice "Add up the counts and stop when you pass the threshold" is the cumulative array. In Q2 of this same exam (the loaded die) you build $C_i$ and stop at the first $C_j > r$. In Condensation's select step you build $C^{(n)}$ and stop at the first $C^{(j)} > r$. Here you build the same cumulative counts and stop at the first bin reaching $t/2$. One primitive, three questions. Say so in the exam — it costs one sentence and it shows you see the course as a whole.

5 · Only now, the math

Every symbol is a thing you already saw

"Paint × light" is a product, and products are annoying. Take logs and it becomes a sum:

$$ I(x,y,t) = R(x,y)\,L(x,y,t) \quad \xrightarrow{\ \log\ } \quad i = r + l $$

Filter each log-frame with derivative filters $f_n$ (Weiss uses two: horizontal and vertical). Because convolution is linear, the filtered frame splits the same way:

$$ o_n(x,y,t) \;=\; f_n * i \;=\; \underbrace{f_n * r}_{\text{permanent — the paint's edges}} \;+\; \underbrace{f_n * l}_{\text{sparse — the shadow's moving border}} $$

Claim 1 (the assumption): the filtered illumination $f_n * l$ is Laplacian-distributed — i.e. usually ≈ 0 with occasional spikes. Under that assumption the maximum-likelihood estimate of the filtered reflectance is the per-pixel temporal median:

$$ \hat r_n(x,y) \;=\; \mathop{\mathrm{median}}\limits_t\; o_n(x,y,t) \tag{2} $$

Why the median falls out: a Laplacian likelihood $e^{-\beta|\cdot|}$ multiplied over all frames becomes one exponential of a sum of absolute errors, and the number minimising total absolute distance to a set of values is their median. Had you assumed a Gaussian you would be minimising squared errors and would get the mean — which, as demo 1 shows, the shadows drag around.

Then rebuild the image from its edges. You know the paint's derivatives, you want the paint. That is an over-constrained linear system, solved once and for all by a fixed pseudo-inverse:

$$ f_n * \hat r = \hat r_n \tag{5} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \hat r = g * \Big( \sum_n f_n^{\,r} * \hat r_n \Big) \tag{6} $$
$$ \text{with } f_n^{\,r}(x,y) = f_n(-x,-y) \quad\text{and}\quad g * \Big(\sum_n f_n^{\,r} * f_n\Big) = \delta \tag{7} $$

$g$ is a fixed deconvolution filter — it depends only on which filters you picked, never on the photos, so you compute it once, offline, forever. In 1-D with $f = [-1, 1]$ this "reconstruction" is just a running sum across the row, which is literally what demo 2 does.

Finally $R = e^{\hat r}$, and each frame's lighting comes free: $\hat l(x,y,t) = i(x,y,t) - \hat r(x,y)$.

6 · The online algorithm, written properly

Keep, per pixel and per filter, a 256-bin histogram $H_k$ of the (quantised) filter responses seen so far. Nothing else. For each arriving frame $I_t$:

  1. Log. $i_t = \log I_t$.
  2. Filter. $o_n = f_n * i_t$ for each derivative filter $f_n$.
  3. Count. Quantise $o_n(x,y)$ to a bin index $k$ and do $H_k(x,y) \mathrel{+}= 1$. This is $O(1)$ per pixel and it is the only thing you store.
  4. Running median. $\hat r_n(x,y) \;=\;$ the smallest bin $k$ whose cumulative count $\sum_{j \le k} H_j(x,y)$ reaches $t/2$. (Keep a pointer at the current median bin and shift it as counts change — then this is $O(1)$ too, instead of rescanning 256 bins.)
  5. Reconstruct. $\hat r = g * \sum_n f_n^{\,r} * \hat r_n$ with the precomputed $g$; output $R_t = e^{\hat r}$.
  6. Free bonus. $L_t = I_t / R_t$, the illumination for this frame.

7 · The four wrinkles that separate 13/13 from 9/13

1 · Median of filtered values, not of raw pixels If you histogram $I_t$ itself you have written background subtraction, not intrinsic images, and the always-shaded corner comes out as dark paint (section 2). Histogram the derivative responses of the log frames. Write that sentence explicitly — the grader is looking for it.
2 · So the values are not literally in [0,255] — you must quantise $o_n$ is a difference of logs: real-valued, roughly in $[-\log 256, +\log 256] \approx [-5.5, 5.5]$. The hint tells you the input alphabet is finite, which bounds the response range and justifies fixed bins — it does not mean the responses are already integers. One sentence — "bin that fixed range uniformly into 256 levels" — closes the gap.
3 · Budget arithmetic Two filters × 256 bins = 512 count-images, which overshoots a strict reading of "256 images". Handle it in one line: either lean on "or their equivalent in memory footprint" (a counter need not be as wide as a pixel), or use 128 bins per filter → exactly 256 images' worth, at negligible precision cost. Noticing this earns the point; ignoring it can cost one.
4 · Reflectance is recovered up to a global scale You rebuilt $\hat r$ from its derivatives, and derivatives kill constants — so $\hat r$ is only known up to an additive constant, i.e. $R$ up to a multiplicative one. Fix it with a convention (e.g. scale so the brightest reflectance is 1). Demo 2 does exactly that. Mentioning it shows you understand what equation (6) can and cannot give you.
The other reading of the question, and why it is weaker You could read "store 256 images" as keep a sliding window of the last 256 frames and take the window median. That is online and fits the budget. But it throws away everything older than 256 frames, and it makes the $[0,255]$ hint pointless — why mention the intensity range at all? Mention it in one line as a fallback if you like, then commit to the histogram.

8 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

One page, in order

Setup. $I = RL$, so $i = \log I = r + l$. Weiss: filter with derivative filters, $o_n = f_n * i$; assume $f_n * l$ is sparse; then $\hat r_n = \operatorname{median}_t o_n$ (eq. 2), and $\hat r = g * \sum_n f_n^{\,r} * \hat r_n$ (eq. 6–7) with $g$ precomputed once; $R = e^{\hat r}$.

What blocks online use. Log, filtering and reconstruction are all per-frame. Only the temporal median needs the whole history — stored naively that is $O(t)$ memory.

Fix — the hint. Pixel values are in $[0,255]$, so the log-derivative responses lie in a bounded, known range. Quantise it into 256 levels and keep, per pixel and per filter, a 256-bin histogram of responses. That is the "256 images" of memory, and it is independent of $t$.

Per frame: log → filter → increment one bin per pixel ($O(1)$) → read the running median as the smallest bin whose cumulative count reaches $t/2$ (the same inverse-CDF/cumulative trick as Q2 and as Condensation's select step) → reconstruct with the fixed $g$ → output $R_t$. Lighting follows as $L_t = I_t / R_t$.

Properties. Constant memory ($256 \times \#$filters per pixel), $O(1)$ per pixel per frame, an output at every frame. Note the median must be taken on the filtered frames, not the raw ones, or permanently-shaded regions get baked into the reflectance; and $\hat r$ is recovered up to an additive constant, fixed by a normalisation convention.

9 · Exam drill

Q. Why can't you recover $R$ from a single image?

$I = RL$ is one equation with two unknowns at every pixel — infinitely many factorisations fit. The problem is ill-posed. A sequence breaks the tie by adding a structural assumption: $R$ is constant over time while $L$ changes, and the changes in $L$ have sparse derivatives.

Q. Why the median and not the mean?

Because of the noise model. A Laplacian ($e^{-\beta|\cdot|}$) likelihood turns into a sum of absolute errors, minimised by the median; a Gaussian turns into a sum of squared errors, minimised by the mean. Shadow-edge responses are rare, large outliers — the mean is dragged by them, the median is not. Laplacian is the honest model for natural image derivatives.

Q. Why median of the filtered images rather than the raw ones?

A raw temporal median is the ML estimate only if $l$ itself were mostly zero — i.e. the scene were mostly pitch dark. False. What is typically true is that the derivatives of $l$ are mostly zero: shadows are large and smooth, so they only produce responses at their moving boundary. So a pixel that is permanently darker (Weiss's Q vs P) contributes no derivative and comes out with the same reflectance as its bright neighbour — which is the correct answer.

Q. What exactly makes the naive algorithm non-online?

Only the median. Finding a middle value normally means keeping every sample, so memory grows as $O(t)$ and you cannot emit an answer until the sequence ends. Log, filtering, and reconstruction are all per-frame operations with fixed cost.

Q. How does a histogram give a median with no sorting?

The histogram is the sorted data, stored as counts per value instead of a list. Sweep the bins left to right accumulating counts; the first bin at which the cumulative count reaches $t/2$ is by definition the value with half the samples below it — the median. Cost is $O(\#\text{bins})$, or $O(1)$ amortised if you keep a pointer at the median bin and shift it as counts arrive.

Q. How much memory, exactly, and why is it independent of $t$?

$256 \times (\#\text{filters})$ counters per pixel — with Weiss's two derivative filters, 512 counters per pixel (or 256 if you use 128 bins each). A new frame only increments counters; it never adds any. So the footprint is fixed at startup no matter how long the video runs, which is exactly the definition of a streaming algorithm.

Q. Where does $g$ come from, and does it need recomputing every frame?

$g$ solves $g * (\sum_n f_n^{\,r} * f_n) = \delta$ — it is the deconvolution that inverts the combined blur of the filter bank. It depends only on the chosen filters, never on the data, so it is computed once offline and reused for every frame and every sequence. In practice it is one Fourier-domain (Poisson-style) solve.

Q. If the same shadow never moves for the entire sequence, what happens?

Its boundary produces a derivative response in every frame, so it is no longer sparse — the median keeps it, and the shadow edge is reconstructed as if it were painted on. This is the honest limit of the method: it separates paint from light only to the extent that the light actually changes. Same reason a single image is hopeless, just slower.
