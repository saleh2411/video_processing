Companion page to Chapter 2 · Moed B 2022, question 1 · 13 points

Horn–Schunck, but the car also has a laser

A camera gives you brightness everywhere. A LiDAR gives you distance, but only at a few scattered dots. The question: how do you pour those dots into Horn–Schunck so the flow gets better? Story first, then the two places depth can enter the energy, then the full derivation, then a live solver you can watch bleed and un-bleed across the car's edge.

Moed B · 1/8/2022 · Question 1 · 13 points · OPTIC FLOW

A vehicle is equipped with a grayscale camera and a LiDAR sensor. The LiDAR sensor provides depth value to a sparse subset of the pixels in the image (i.e., not every pixel has a corresponding depth value). See example in Figure 1.

Modify the Horn–Schunck algorithm to take as input two grayscale images $I_1$ and $I_2$ and their corresponding sparse depth maps $D_1$ and $D_2$, and produce the optic flow between them.

1 · The story

Two kids watching cars from a bridge

Imagine you are looking at a photo of a street and someone asks: "which way did everything move between this photo and the next one?"

The camera kid answers by looking at brightness. He finds a dark spot in photo 1 and hunts for the same dark spot in photo 2. This works great on a stripey T-shirt or a tree — lots of detail. But on a big flat grey wall he is helpless: every point looks like every other point, so he has no idea how far it slid. That is the aperture problem.

Horn–Schunck fixes it by adding a rule: "neighbours move together." The kid guesses the wall's motion by copying the motion of the wall's edges. The guess spreads inward like paint. That spreading is the smoothness term.

The laser kid has a different tool. She can't see colours at all, but every so often she can shoot a laser and ask "how far away is this exact spot?" She only gets a few hundred dots, not the whole picture. She knows two useful things:

  • A dot that was 8 metres away in photo 1 is still about 8 metres away in photo 2 — it just moved somewhere else on the screen. So she can chase her dots, and each chased dot is one more hard fact about motion, even on the flat grey wall.
  • Where distance jumps suddenly — 8 metres here, 30 metres one pixel to the right — she is standing at the edge of an object. And objects move as one, but two different objects don't. So the camera kid should not spread his paint across that line.

So the laser helps twice: it adds facts where the picture has none, and it tells the paint where to stop.

The whole answer, in one sentence Keep the Horn–Schunck energy, add a second data term that is switched on only at pixels where LiDAR exists (depth-constancy, exactly like brightness-constancy), and make the smoothness weight anisotropic so it collapses across depth discontinuities. Then re-derive the Euler–Lagrange equations; the per-pixel update becomes a $2\times2$ linear solve instead of the classic scalar formula.

2 · The material this question is built on

Recap — Horn–Schunck (Chapter 2)

Brightness constancy says a moving point keeps its grey value:

$$ I(x,y,t) = I(x+u\,\delta t,\; y+v\,\delta t,\; t+\delta t) $$

First-order Taylor expansion of the right side and cancelling gives the optic flow constraint equation — one equation, two unknowns per pixel:

$$ I_x u + I_y v + I_t = 0 \tag{1} $$

Under-determined, so Horn–Schunck adds a global smoothness prior and minimises

$$ E_{\rm HS}(u,v) = \iint \underbrace{\big(I_x u + I_y v + I_t\big)^2}_{\text{data}} \;+\; \alpha^2 \underbrace{\big(\|\nabla u\|^2 + \|\nabla v\|^2\big)}_{\text{smoothness}} \; dx\,dy \tag{2} $$

whose Euler–Lagrange equations, with $\nabla^2 u \approx \bar u - u$ (the local weighted average minus the centre), give the famous Jacobi iteration

$$ u^{k+1} = \bar u^{k} - I_x\frac{I_x\bar u^k + I_y \bar v^k + I_t}{\alpha^2 + I_x^2 + I_y^2}, \qquad v^{k+1} = \bar v^{k} - I_y\frac{I_x\bar u^k + I_y \bar v^k + I_t}{\alpha^2 + I_x^2 + I_y^2} \tag{3} $$
Two properties of HS you must exploit here (i) It is a variational fill-in method: pixels with no usable data still get a flow, inherited from their neighbours through the smoothness term. That is exactly why sparse input is not a problem — you can switch a data term on at 2% of the pixels and HS will diffuse it everywhere.
(ii) Its smoothness term is isotropic and global, so it happily smears motion across object boundaries. That is HS's classic weakness, and depth discontinuities are precisely a map of where the boundaries are.

3 · Where depth can enter — the two doors

Design choices
DoorIdeaCosts / assumptions
A · extra data term Depth is just another channel that is conserved under motion, so write a second linearised constraint $D_x u + D_y v + D_t = 0$ and switch it on only where LiDAR exists. Assumes depth-constancy: true for motion parallel to the image plane, approximate otherwise (an approaching car changes depth). Requires two neighbouring LiDAR samples to form $D_x, D_y$.
B · anisotropic smoothness Down-weight the smoothness between two neighbouring pixels whose depths differ, so flow does not diffuse across a depth step. Assumption-free (uses depth only as a boundary cue, never as a motion measurement). Needs depth interpolated/propagated to the pixels between samples.
C · rigid-motion prior (bonus) If the scene is static and only the car moves with known ego-motion $(R,\mathbf{t})$, depth predicts the flow outright; use it as a soft prior $\|(u,v)-(u_0,v_0)\|^2$. Needs ego-motion and a static-world assumption; breaks on other moving vehicles. Mention as an extension, don't make it the core answer.

A full-credit answer uses A + B and states the caveat of A.

4 · The modified energy

Notation

Let $m(\mathbf{x}) \in \{0,1\}$ be the LiDAR validity mask: $m=1$ where a depth sample exists in $D_1$ and its neighbours on the scan pattern exist (so that $D_x, D_y$ are computable), $m=0$ otherwise. Let $\tilde D$ be depth propagated to every pixel (nearest-sample / bilateral fill) — used only as an edge cue, never as a measurement. Define the pairwise smoothness weight

$$ w_{pq} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\big(\tilde D(p) - \tilde D(q)\big)^2}{2\sigma_D^2}\right) \in (0,1] \tag{4} $$

$w_{pq}\!\approx\!1$ inside an object (depths agree), $w_{pq}\!\approx\!0$ across a depth cliff. Now the energy:

$$ E(u,v) = \iint \big(I_x u + I_y v + I_t\big)^2 \;+\; \lambda\, m(\mathbf{x})\big(D_x u + D_y v + D_t\big)^2 \;+\; \alpha^2 \, w \big(\|\nabla u\|^2 + \|\nabla v\|^2\big)\; d\mathbf{x} \tag{5} $$

with $D_t = D_2 - D_1$ measured at the sample, and $\lambda$ balancing "one metre of depth error" against "one grey level of brightness error" (it also absorbs the units, since $D$ is in metres and $I$ in grey levels — in practice normalise both channels or set $\lambda \sim \sigma_I^2/\sigma_D^2$).

Why the depth term is not redundant

Term 1 constrains $(u,v)$ only along $\nabla I$ — the aperture problem again. Term 2 constrains $(u,v)$ along $\nabla D$. On a textureless but slanted or stepped surface (a plain van door, the flat road) $\nabla I \approx 0$ but $\nabla D \neq 0$, so the depth term supplies exactly the information the brightness term is missing. Where both gradients exist and are non-parallel, the two constraint lines intersect and the flow is determined pointwise, with no help from the smoothness prior at all:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} I_x & I_y \\ D_x & D_y \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix} = -\begin{pmatrix} I_t\\ D_t\end{pmatrix} \quad\text{is invertible iff}\quad I_xD_y - I_yD_x \neq 0 $$

5 · Deriving the update rule

Write the discrete smoothness term over 4-neighbourhoods, $\alpha^2\sum_{q\in N(p)} w_{pq}\big[(u_p-u_q)^2 + (v_p-v_q)^2\big]$, and set $\partial E/\partial u_p = \partial E/\partial v_p = 0$. With

$$ S_p = \sum_{q\in N(p)} w_{pq}, \qquad \bar u_p = \frac{1}{S_p}\sum_{q\in N(p)} w_{pq}\, u_q, \qquad \bar v_p = \frac{1}{S_p}\sum_{q\in N(p)} w_{pq}\, v_q $$

(the depth-weighted local average — flow is now averaged only over neighbours at a similar distance), the stationarity conditions are the $2\times2$ system

$$ \begin{pmatrix} I_x^2 + \lambda m D_x^2 + \alpha^2 S_p & I_xI_y + \lambda m D_xD_y \\[2pt] I_xI_y + \lambda m D_xD_y & I_y^2 + \lambda m D_y^2 + \alpha^2 S_p \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} u_p \\ v_p\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha^2 S_p \bar u_p - I_xI_t - \lambda m D_xD_t \\[2pt] \alpha^2 S_p \bar v_p - I_yI_t - \lambda m D_yD_t \end{pmatrix} \tag{6} $$

Solve it in closed form by Cramer's rule at every pixel, sweep, repeat. Note the sanity checks:

  • $\lambda = 0$ and $w \equiv 1$ ⇒ $S_p = 4$, and (6) collapses back to the classic HS update (3).
  • $m = 0$ (no LiDAR here) ⇒ the depth rows vanish and the pixel is updated by pure HS — the sparsity is handled automatically, no interpolation of $D$ needed for the data term.
  • The matrix is $M^{\!\top}\!M + \alpha^2S_p\,\mathbb{I}$ with $M$ the two constraint rows, so it is symmetric positive definite for $\alpha>0$ — always invertible, so the iteration is well posed.
  1. Pre-process. Optionally median-filter $D_1,D_2$ to kill LiDAR outliers; build the validity mask $m$; build $\tilde D$ by nearest-sample propagation (or a joint-bilateral / guided filter using $I_1$) for the smoothness weights only.
  2. Build a Gaussian pyramid of $I_1,I_2$ (and downsample $D$ by taking the nearest valid sample, never averaging across a depth edge). Vehicle motion is large; the linearisation in (1) needs sub-pixel displacements.
  3. At each level: warp $I_2$ (and $D_2$) toward $I_1$ with the current flow, compute $I_x,I_y,I_t$ and, at LiDAR samples, $D_x,D_y,D_t$; compute $w_{pq}$ from (4).
  4. Iterate (6) to convergence (Jacobi / Gauss–Seidel / SOR).
  5. Propagate the flow to the next finer level (upsample $\times2$, multiply by 2) and repeat. Optionally add a robust penalty (Charbonnier $\sqrt{s^2+\varepsilon^2}$) on both data terms so a single bad LiDAR return cannot poison the field.
The caveat you must state for full marks Depth is not conserved the way brightness is. $D_2(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{w}) = D_1(\mathbf{x})$ holds only for motion parallel to the image plane. For a car approaching at $\dot Z$ over $\delta t$, the correct constraint is $D_x u + D_y v + D_t = \dot Z\,\delta t \neq 0$. Two honest fixes: (a) keep $\dot Z$ as a third unknown per surface / per segment (this turns the method into scene flow, $(u,v,\dot Z)$, and needs one more constraint), or (b) give the depth term a robust penalty and a small $\lambda$ so it acts as a soft hint. Door B (the smoothness weight) does not suffer from this at all — it never assumes depth is conserved.

7 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

One page, as the exam asks

Idea. Keep the Horn–Schunck variational framework and use the sparse depth twice: as an additional data term active only at LiDAR pixels, and as an edge cue that modulates the smoothness term. HS already fills in flow where data is missing, so sparsity is not an obstacle.

Energy. With $m(\mathbf{x})=1$ at valid LiDAR pixels and $0$ elsewhere, $\tilde D$ = depth propagated to all pixels (used only for weights), and $w_{pq}=\exp\!\big(-(\tilde D_p-\tilde D_q)^2/2\sigma_D^2\big)$:

$$ E(u,v)=\iint (I_xu+I_yv+I_t)^2 + \lambda\, m\,(D_xu+D_yv+D_t)^2 + \alpha^2 w\big(\|\nabla u\|^2+\|\nabla v\|^2\big)\,d\mathbf{x} $$

The second term is the depth analogue of brightness constancy ($D_2(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{w})\approx D_1(\mathbf{x})$, Taylor-expanded); $D_x,D_y$ are formed from neighbouring samples on the scan pattern, $D_t=D_2-D_1$ at the sample. The third term stops flow from diffusing across depth discontinuities, i.e. across object boundaries.

Solution. Euler–Lagrange with $S_p=\sum_q w_{pq}$ and depth-weighted averages $\bar u_p,\bar v_p$ gives, per pixel, the SPD $2\times2$ system

$$ \begin{pmatrix} I_x^2+\lambda mD_x^2+\alpha^2S_p & I_xI_y+\lambda mD_xD_y\\ I_xI_y+\lambda mD_xD_y & I_y^2+\lambda mD_y^2+\alpha^2S_p\end{pmatrix}\!\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha^2S_p\bar u_p - I_xI_t-\lambda mD_xD_t\\ \alpha^2S_p\bar v_p - I_yI_t-\lambda mD_yD_t\end{pmatrix} $$

solved by Cramer's rule and iterated (Jacobi/Gauss–Seidel) to convergence, inside a coarse-to-fine pyramid with warping so the Taylor linearisation stays valid for large vehicle motion. Setting $\lambda=0,\,w\equiv1$ recovers standard Horn–Schunck exactly.

Why it helps. The brightness term constrains $(u,v)$ only along $\nabla I$; the depth term constrains it along $\nabla D$. On textureless but geometrically structured surfaces ($\nabla I\approx0$, $\nabla D\neq0$) the depth term breaks the aperture problem, and where both gradients exist and are non-parallel the flow is determined pointwise. Meanwhile the modulated smoothness removes HS's characteristic over-smoothing at motion boundaries, which for a driving scene coincide with depth boundaries.

Limitation. Depth is not strictly conserved: for motion with a $Z$ component the constraint becomes $D_xu+D_yv+D_t=\dot Z\delta t$. Handle it with a robust (Charbonnier/$L_1$) penalty and moderate $\lambda$, or extend to scene flow $(u,v,\dot Z)$. The smoothness-weight use of depth is free of this assumption. Also median-filter the depth first, since one bad LiDAR return would otherwise act as a hard constraint.

8 · Exam drill

Q. Why not just interpolate the sparse depth to a dense map and be done with it?

You can, and you must for the smoothness weights — but not for the data term. Interpolation invents depth values; feeding invented values into a hard measurement term propagates the interpolation error into the flow, and worst of all it invents them exactly where you have no evidence: across the object boundaries, which is the one place they must not be smooth. The mask $m$ costs nothing and keeps measurements and guesses cleanly separated. For the weight $w_{pq}$ a wrong guess only softens or hardens a regulariser, which is benign.

Q. How do you compute $D_x$ and $D_y$ if depth is sparse?

Use the scan geometry. LiDAR returns arrive along scan-lines, so within a line the horizontal spacing $s$ is known and $D_x \approx (D(x+s)-D(x-s))/2s$; vertically use the neighbouring scan-line, spacing $t$: $D_y \approx (D(y+t)-D(y-t))/2t$. Set $m=1$ only where all four neighbours exist and are within a depth threshold of the centre (otherwise you are differencing across an occlusion boundary, where the derivative is meaningless). An alternative is to fit a local plane to the samples in a small window by least squares and read $D_x,D_y$ off the plane — more robust, and it gives you the surface normal for free.

Q. What is the right value of $\lambda$? The units don't match.

They don't: $I$ is in grey levels, $D$ in metres, so $\lambda$ has units of $\text{grey}^2/\text{m}^2$. Normalise both residuals by their noise: $\lambda = \sigma_I^2/\sigma_D^2$, where $\sigma_I$ is the image noise and $\sigma_D$ the LiDAR ranging noise. This is the standard maximum-likelihood weighting — each term becomes a squared $z$-score. In practice you can also just rescale $D$ to $[0,255]$ (or use disparity $1/D$, which is better behaved: disparity noise is roughly uniform, whereas depth noise grows with range) and set $\lambda\approx1$.

Q. Would disparity be better than depth?

Usually yes, on two counts. First, disparity $d = fB/Z$ is what actually moves linearly with image coordinates in a rectified setup, and its noise is roughly homogeneous, so the squared penalty is statistically fair across the whole range — with raw depth, a 1 m error at 5 m and at 80 m get the same penalty even though the far one is within the sensor's noise. Second, $1/Z$ is bounded, so the sky / no-return case degrades gracefully to $0$ instead of $\infty$.

Q. Why does the $2\times2$ matrix always invert? Compare with Lucas–Kanade.

It is $M^{\!\top}M + \alpha^2S_p\mathbb{I}$ with $M = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}I_x&I_y\\ \sqrt{\lambda m}D_x & \sqrt{\lambda m}D_y\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. $M^{\!\top}M$ is positive semi-definite and $\alpha^2 S_p > 0$ shifts every eigenvalue up, so it is strictly positive definite. This is the same trick that makes HS well posed everywhere while Lucas–Kanade needs $\sum\nabla I\nabla I^{\!\top}$ (the Harris/structure matrix) to be full rank — LK simply refuses to answer at a corner-free pixel, HS answers with the prior. Note that the LiDAR term also directly improves LK: adding depth rows to the LK stacked system raises the rank of the structure matrix, so "good features to track" would include flat-but-stepped regions.

Q. Can the depth data term hurt?

Yes, in three situations. (1) Radial motion — depth genuinely changes, so the constraint is biased; that's the $\dot Z$ caveat. (2) Occlusion boundaries — a LiDAR sample near a silhouette can hit the far surface in frame 1 and the near surface in frame 2, producing a huge fake $D_t$; mask those out or use a robust penalty. (3) Calibration/time offset — camera and LiDAR are not co-located and not perfectly synchronised, so a depth "pixel" is really a projected 3-D point; if the extrinsics or the rolling-shutter/scan timing are off by a little, you are constraining the wrong pixel. All three are handled by keeping $\lambda$ moderate and the penalty robust.

Q. Does the depth term have its own aperture problem?

Yes, and it is worth saying so — it is the same one-equation-two-unknowns issue. $D_xu+D_yv+D_t=0$ constrains only the component of $(u,v)$ along $\nabla D$. On a planar surface $\nabla D$ is constant, so every LiDAR sample on that plane gives the same constraint direction and the orthogonal component is still undetermined — depth alone cannot solve a flat slanted wall any more than brightness alone can solve a flat grey one. What rescues you is that the two apertures are different: $\nabla I$ and $\nabla D$ are generally non-parallel, so together they span the plane (that is the determinant condition $I_xD_y-I_yD_x\neq0$). And on a curved surface $\nabla D$ itself rotates from pixel to pixel, so a small neighbourhood of depth samples already determines the flow on its own. Demo 1 is built exactly this way — the car body is curved, which is why switching on the depth term alone visibly fixes its interior.

Q. Sketch the ego-motion (door C) variant.

For a static scene and a camera moving with rotation $\boldsymbol\omega$ and translation $\mathbf{t}$, the flow at a pixel with depth $Z$ is the classic motion-field equation $$u = \frac{-t_x f + x t_z}{Z} + \omega_x\frac{xy}{f} - \omega_y\Big(f+\frac{x^2}{f}\Big) + \omega_z y,$$ and symmetrically for $v$. So depth gives a full predicted flow $(u_0,v_0)$ at every LiDAR pixel. Add $\mu\, m\,\|(u,v)-(u_0,v_0)\|^2$ to the energy. This is far stronger than the depth-constancy term (two constraints per pixel rather than one), but it is only valid on static parts of the scene, so combine it with a robust penalty — pixels on other moving cars will simply reject the prior, and their residual conveniently doubles as a moving-object detector.

Q. Why is the coarse-to-fine pyramid non-negotiable here?

Because equation (1) came from a first-order Taylor expansion, which is only valid when the displacement is small relative to the image structure — roughly a pixel. A car at 50 km/h with a 30 Hz camera moves tens of pixels per frame. Downsampling by $2^L$ divides the displacement by $2^L$ until the linearisation holds, then each level warps the second image by the current estimate so the residual motion the next level sees stays small. Careful with the depth channel: downsample $D$ by nearest valid sample, never by averaging, or you will manufacture depth values that lie between the car and the building behind it.
