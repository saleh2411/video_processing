Companion page to Chapters 9 & 10 · Moed A, question 6

One knob: does distance care about the picture?

A scary-looking integral with exactly one adjustable number in it. Once you can read that integrand out loud, the question answers itself in ten seconds. Story, a demo that reproduces the exam figure, then the math.

Moed A · 19/6/2022 · Question 6 · 12 points · GEODESIC DISTANCE

Let $I(\mathbf{x}) : \Psi \to \mathbb{R}^d$ be an image with support $\Psi \subset \mathbb{R}^2$. Given a binary mask $M$ associated to a "seed" (or "object") region $\Omega = \{\mathbf{x} \in \Psi : M(\mathbf{x}) = 0\}$, the unsigned Geodesic Distance $D_l(\mathbf{x}; M, \nabla I)$ is given by

$$ D_l(\mathbf{x}; M, \nabla I) = \min_{\{\mathbf{x}'\,|\,M(\mathbf{x}')=0\}} d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}'), \quad\text{with}\tag{1} $$
$$ d(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}) = \inf_{\Gamma \in \mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}} \int_0^{l(\Gamma)} \sqrt{1 + \gamma^2\big(\nabla I(s)\cdot\Gamma'(s)\big)^2}\, ds \tag{2} $$

where $\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}$ is the set of all differentiable paths between $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, and $\Gamma'(s)$ is the unit tangent to the path. This question is about the parameter $\gamma$. Figure 4(a) shows the input image, (b) the mask with object region $\Omega$. Determine for (c) and (d) whether they use $\gamma = 0$ or $\gamma > 0$ (darker means shorter distance). Explain your answer.

1 · The story

Walking home across the park

You want to know how far every spot in the park is from the fountain. You measure "far" by how tired you are when you arrive.

Version A — the park is completely flat and empty. Then tiredness only depends on how many steps you took. Walk 100 metres, get 100 metres tired. Distance is pure walking length, and it makes no difference which way you went or what you walked over.

Version B — the park is full of hedges. Now walking along a hedge is easy, but pushing through one is exhausting. Two spots the same number of steps from the fountain can be wildly different in tiredness, depending on how many hedges are in the way.

$\gamma$ is the knob that says how tall the hedges are.

  • $\gamma = 0$ → the hedges are flat. Nothing to push through. Distance = plain walking length. The distance map is blind to the picture.
  • $\gamma > 0$ → the hedges are real. Crossing one costs extra. The distance map can see the picture.

And in an image, a "hedge" is an edge — a place where the brightness changes.

So the question becomes a single yes/no Look at the distance map and ask: can I see the picture's own texture in it?
Texture invisible, just a smooth halo → $\gamma = 0$.
The swirls and edges print through into the map → $\gamma > 0$.

2 · Reading the integrand out loud

You do not need to solve it, only to read it

The whole formula is "add up a cost as you walk along the path". The cost per step is:

$$ \sqrt{\,\underbrace{1}_{\text{one step is one step}} + \underbrace{\gamma^2\big(\nabla I \cdot \Gamma'\big)^2}_{\text{extra charge for crossing an edge}}\,} $$

Take the two cases:

$\gamma = 0$: the second term vanishes, the integrand becomes $\sqrt{1} = 1$, and

$$ d(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}) = \inf_\Gamma \int_0^{l(\Gamma)} 1 \, ds = \inf_\Gamma\, l(\Gamma) $$

— the plain length of the shortest path, i.e. ordinary Euclidean distance (within $\Psi$). The image $I$ has literally disappeared from the formula. The map depends only on the shape of $\Omega$, so you get a smooth halo growing outward from the silhouette, and the background could be anything at all without changing a single value.

$\gamma > 0$: now the term $\gamma^2(\nabla I \cdot \Gamma')^2$ is alive. Note carefully what it measures: $\nabla I \cdot \Gamma'$ is the image gradient projected onto the direction you are walking. So it is the rate at which brightness changes along your path:

  • Walking across an edge → $\Gamma'$ is parallel to $\nabla I$ → the dot product is large → expensive.
  • Walking along an edge (an isophote — a line of constant brightness) → $\Gamma'$ is perpendicular to $\nabla I$ → the dot product is zero → costs the same as flat ground.
  • Inside a homogeneous region → $\nabla I \approx 0$ → also free.

So distance grows fast wherever a path is forced to cross image structure, and the resulting map shows that structure. That's why the swirls become visible.

4 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

Short question — but explain, don't just answer

What $\gamma$ controls. Read the integrand of (2): $\sqrt{1 + \gamma^2(\nabla I\cdot\Gamma')^2}$. The constant $1$ charges for arclength; the second term charges extra for moving along the image gradient, i.e. for crossing edges. $\gamma$ sets how much the image influences distance.

Case $\gamma = 0$. The integrand reduces to $\sqrt{1} = 1$, so $d(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}) = \inf_\Gamma \int ds = \inf_\Gamma l(\Gamma)$ — plain arclength, i.e. Euclidean distance from the seed region. $I$ has vanished from the expression entirely, so the map depends only on the geometry of $\Omega$ and is completely blind to image content: a smooth halo, with background texture leaving no trace.

Case $\gamma > 0$. The term $\gamma^2(\nabla I\cdot\Gamma')^2$ is active. Since $\nabla I \cdot \Gamma'$ is the gradient projected on the direction of travel, paths crossing an edge are penalised, while paths running along an isophote ($\Gamma' \perp \nabla I$) or through a homogeneous region ($\nabla I \approx 0$) cost the same as flat ground. Distance therefore grows quickly across image structure, and that structure becomes visible in the map.

Determination. (c) shows a smooth halo in which the background swirls are invisible → it is image-blind → $\gamma = 0$. (d) shows the swirls and edges printed into the distance field, with the halo distorted around them → it is image-aware → $\gamma > 0$.

Rule of thumb. Map ignores texture ⇒ $\gamma = 0$; texture prints through ⇒ $\gamma > 0$.

5 · Exam drill

Q. Why is the dot product $\nabla I \cdot \Gamma'$ and not just $|\nabla I|$?

Because the cost should depend on how you travel through the structure, not merely on whether structure is present. $\nabla I\cdot\Gamma'$ is the gradient projected onto the direction of motion — the rate at which brightness changes along the path. Walking along an edge changes nothing and is free; walking across it is expensive. Using $|\nabla I|$ instead would charge you for standing near an edge even while running parallel to it, and you would lose the ability to travel cheaply along object contours.

Q. What are the units / why the $\sqrt{1 + \cdots}$ form rather than $1 + \gamma|\nabla I\cdot\Gamma'|$?

The square-root-of-sum-of-squares form is the arclength of the path lifted onto the image surface: if you think of $I$ as a height map, walking in the plane while the height changes traces a longer 3-D path, of length $\sqrt{dx^2 + \gamma^2 dI^2}$. So $\gamma$ literally scales how tall the landscape is. It also guarantees the cost is never below $1$ per unit length, so the metric is well-behaved and reduces cleanly to Euclidean at $\gamma=0$.

Q. What happens as $\gamma \to \infty$?

The arclength term becomes negligible next to the gradient term, so distance is dominated purely by total brightness variation along the path. Paths will take enormous geometric detours to avoid crossing any edge, and the distance map effectively measures "how many edges must I cross", partitioning the image into homogeneous basins. Useful for aggressive segmentation, but it becomes unstable in noisy regions since noise creates spurious gradients.

Q. In equation (1), why is $\Omega$ defined by $M(\mathbf{x}) = 0$, and what is $D_l$ on $\Omega$?

$\Omega$ is the seed set — the region you measure distance from. Equation (1) is the point-to-set distance: the minimum over all seed points of the path distance to that point. For $\mathbf{x} \in \Omega$ the minimum is attained at $\mathbf{x}$ itself with a zero-length path, so $D_l = 0$ there — which is why the object silhouette is solid black in panels (c) and (d).

Q. How is this actually computed?

Discretise the image as a graph — pixels are nodes, neighbouring pixels are edges, and the edge weight is the integrand evaluated for that step, $\sqrt{1 + \gamma^2 (\nabla I\cdot \mathbf{e})^2}$ for step direction $\mathbf{e}$. Then the infimum over paths is a shortest-path problem, solved by Dijkstra in $O(N\log N)$ from the seed set (multi-source), or by fast sweeping if you want a GPU-friendly raster-scan alternative. Both are in Chapter 9.

Q. Where else does this exact machinery appear in the course?

Chapter 9 colorization (scribbles → geodesic distance maps → blend the colours) and Chapter 10 matting (trimap → geodesic distance to foreground/background → opacity). Moed A Q7 applies the same idea to a 3D point cloud. The recurring recipe: define a cost that is cheap inside regions and expensive across boundaries, then take shortest paths from seeds.

Q. Both (c) and (d) are black in the same place. Why does that not help you decide?

Because that black region is $\Omega$ itself, where $D_l = 0$ by definition for any $\gamma$. The seed region tells you nothing about the parameter. The discriminating evidence is entirely in the background: whether the image's own structure has left a trace in the distance field.
