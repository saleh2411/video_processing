Companion page to Chapter 5 · Moed B 2022, question 3 · 13 points

A sentence is a video. Words are frames.

This question looks like it is about NLP. It is not. It is checking whether you noticed that the texture-synthesis chapter never actually cared that its input was pixels — it only ever needed a sequence of vectors in a Euclidean space. Word2Vec hands you exactly that. So: state the analogy, then port both algorithms — Video Textures (jump around the original) and the linear state-space Dynamic Texture (learn $A$, drive it with noise) — and say what the decode step is.

Moed B · 1/8/2022 · Question 3 · 13 points · DYNAMIC TEXTURE

Word2Vec is a neural network that maps English words to vectors in some Euclidean space. Use Word2Vec to synthesize text. That is, your algorithm should take as input a short text (i.e., a sentence or a paragraph) and generate as output a long text (i.e., a page of text).

1 · The story

The kid with one short song and a very long car ride

You have a 10-second video of a candle flame and you need 10 minutes of candle flame for a movie. You can't film more. What do you do?

You loop it — but a plain loop has a visible jolt at the seam, where the last frame doesn't match the first. So instead you play the clip, and every so often you secretly jump to a different moment in the same clip — but only to a moment that looks almost exactly like where you are now. Nobody sees the jump, because the two frames were nearly identical. Do that forever and you get infinite candle. That is Video Textures.

Now the same trick with a song. You know one verse. You sing it, and when you reach a word — say "and" — you notice the verse has another "and" later on. You silently jump there and keep singing from that point. The listener hears a new, longer song made only of pieces of the old one, and never hears a break, because you only ever jumped between places that sounded the same.

The only thing you needed was a way to say "these two moments look alike" — a distance. For frames it was pixel difference. For words, Word2Vec gives you one: similar words sit close together in its space, so $\|\mathbf{w}_{\rm cat} - \mathbf{w}_{\rm dog}\|$ is small and $\|\mathbf{w}_{\rm cat} - \mathbf{w}_{\rm bureaucracy}\|$ is big. That single fact is what makes the whole texture-synthesis chapter apply to text.

There is also a second, cleverer trick in the chapter. Instead of jumping around the original, you watch how the flame moves and learn a little rule — "whatever it is doing now, next moment it will do roughly this, plus a bit of randomness". Then you can turn the crank forever and generate flame that was never filmed. That is the linear dynamic texture, and it also works on words: learn how meaning drifts from one word to the next, drift some more, and read off the nearest real word at each step.

2 · The material this question is built on

Route 1 — Video Textures (Schödl et al.): re-order the original

Compute the all-pairs distance matrix between frames, $D_{ij} = \|I_i - I_j\|^2$. To decide whether, after showing frame $i$, you may continue at frame $j$, you must compare what would have come next with what you are about to show: the successor of $i$ is $i{+}1$, so the cost of the jump $i \to j$ is $D_{i+1,\,j}$. Turn costs into probabilities with a Boltzmann law,

$$ P_{ij} \;\propto\; \exp\!\left(-\frac{D_{i+1,\,j}}{\sigma^2}\right), \qquad \sum_j P_{ij} = 1 \tag{1} $$

and then simply take a random walk on this Markov chain. Three refinements from the chapter:

  • Preserve dynamics with a temporal filter. Matching single frames is not enough — a ball at the top of its arc going up looks like a ball at the top going down. Filter the matrix with binomial weights $a_k$ over a window, $D'_{ij} = \sum_{k=-m}^{m-1} a_k D_{i+k,\,j+k}$, so that a jump requires the whole neighbourhood in time to match, not just one frame.
  • Prune all but the best few transitions (and drop those below a threshold) so the walk never takes a visibly bad jump.
  • Avoid dead ends with a future cost, computed by value iteration ($Q$-learning): $D''_{ij} = (D'_{ij})^p + \beta \min_k D''_{jk}$ — don't jump into a corner you cannot get out of.

Porting it to text, term by term

$I_t \mapsto \mathbf{y}_t$ (the Word2Vec vector of token $t$). The temporal filter becomes an $n$-gram context window: requiring $D'_{ij}$ small means "the few words before and after match too", which is precisely what keeps the generated text locally grammatical. Everything else is unchanged.

Route 2 — the linear dynamic texture (Doretto/Soatto, the chapter's state space)

Model the sequence as the output of a linear dynamical system driven by noise:

$$ \mathbf{x}_{t+1} = A\,\mathbf{x}_t + B\,\mathbf{v}_t, \qquad \mathbf{y}_t = C\,\mathbf{x}_t \;(+\,\mathbf{w}_t), \qquad \mathbf{v}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}) \tag{2} $$

Identification is the SVD recipe from the chapter. Stack the observations as columns, $Y = [\mathbf{y}_1 \cdots \mathbf{y}_N] \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times N}$, take the rank-$n$ SVD $Y \approx U_n \Sigma_n V_n^{\!\top}$, and read off

$$ \hat C = U_n, \qquad \hat X = [\mathbf{x}_1\cdots\mathbf{x}_N] = \Sigma_n V_n^{\!\top}, \qquad \hat A = X_{2:N}\,X_{1:N-1}^{+} \tag{3} $$
$$ \hat B: \quad V \;=\; X_{2:N} - \hat A X_{1:N-1}, \qquad \tfrac{1}{N-1}VV^{\!\top} = \hat B\hat B^{\!\top} \;\;(\text{by SVD/Cholesky}) \tag{4} $$

Then synthesise as long a sequence as you like by iterating (2) from a seed state. Note the pleasing symmetry with Chapter 4: the Kalman filter infers $\mathbf{x}$ from $\mathbf{y}$; the dynamic texture runs the same model forwards to invent $\mathbf{y}$.

The one genuinely new step that text needs

A synthesised $\mathbf{y}_t$ is an arbitrary point of $\mathbb{R}^d$. A video can display an arbitrary pixel vector — but you cannot print an arbitrary word vector. So you must decode / quantise back to the vocabulary $V$:

$$ \hat w_t \;=\; \arg\max_{w\in V}\; \frac{\mathbf{y}_t \cdot \mathbf{e}(w)}{\|\mathbf{y}_t\|\,\|\mathbf{e}(w)\|} \qquad\text{(cosine nearest neighbour, ANN/kd-tree for speed)} \tag{5} $$
And immediately re-project — otherwise it drifts off the manifold Word vectors occupy a thin, curved sliver of $\mathbb{R}^d$. A linear system driven by Gaussian noise wanders off that sliver, and after a few dozen steps you are decoding points that are far from every real word — the nearest neighbour becomes arbitrary and the output turns to mush (exactly the "blurring / dying out" failure of a badly identified dynamic texture). Fix it by feeding the quantised vector back into the state instead of the raw one: $\mathbf{x}_t \leftarrow \hat C^{\!\top}\mathbf{e}(\hat w_t)$ before the next step. This is snap-back, and it is the text analogue of clamping pixel values to $[0,255]$.

What to notice Push $\sigma$ to its minimum: the walk stops jumping and simply replays the input, then stops — a plain loop. Push it to maximum: it jumps constantly and the output is word salad, because a "cheap" jump is no longer actually cheap. The useful range is in between — exactly the same trade-off as the $\sigma$ in Video Textures. Raising the context window $m$ makes jumps far rarer but much better, because now a jump has to match the surrounding words too.

4 · The algorithm, written out

  1. Tokenise and embed. Split the input text into tokens $w_1,\dots,w_N$ (keep punctuation as tokens — it carries the rhythm, like a scene cut in a video). Map each to $\mathbf{y}_t = \mathbf{e}(w_t) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ with Word2Vec ($d\approx300$). Optionally $\ell_2$ normalise, so Euclidean distance and cosine distance agree.
  2. Build the distance matrix. $D_{ij} = \|\mathbf{y}_i - \mathbf{y}_j\|^2$, $N\times N$.
  3. Preserve dynamics. Filter over a context window with binomial weights: $D'_{ij} = \sum_{k=-m}^{m-1} a_k\, D_{i+k,\,j+k}$. This is the $n$-gram constraint in disguise.
  4. Add future cost (optional but recommended). Value-iterate $D''_{ij} \leftarrow (D'_{ij})^p + \beta\min_k D''_{jk}$ until convergence, so the walk never enters a region of the text it cannot leave.
  5. Probabilities. $P_{ij} \propto \exp(-D''_{i+1,j}/\sigma^2)$; prune to the best few $j$ per row, zero out anything above a threshold, renormalise each row.
  6. Synthesise. Start at the position given by the input's last token (or a random one). Emit $w_i$; sample $j \sim P_{i,\cdot}$; set $i \leftarrow j$; repeat until you have a page.
  7. (Route 2, in place of 2–6.) Identify $(\hat A,\hat B,\hat C)$ by the SVD recipe (3)–(4), seed $\mathbf{x}_1$ from the input's last state, iterate $\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = \hat A\mathbf{x}_t + \hat B\mathbf{v}_t$, output $\mathbf{y}_t = \hat C\mathbf{x}_t$, decode with (5), and snap back $\mathbf{x}_t \leftarrow \hat C^{\!\top}\mathbf{e}(\hat w_t)$.
  8. Detokenise. Join with spaces, capitalise after sentence-final punctuation. Done — a page of text built entirely from a paragraph.

State the honest limitation — it earns marks Word2Vec is a context-free lookup: one fixed vector per word type, with no notion of syntax, agreement or long-range structure. Both routes therefore produce text that is locally plausible and globally meaningless — precisely like a synthesised video texture, which is locally convincing flame and globally has no plot. The context window ($m$) is what buys local grammar, and it is the direct analogue of the temporal filter that Video Textures needed for exactly the same reason. If real coherence is wanted, the honest answer is that you need contextual embeddings / a sequence model (an RNN or a transformer), i.e. replace the linear $A$ with a learned nonlinear map — the same upgrade path that takes you from linear dynamic textures to modern video generation.

5 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

One page, as the exam asks

Observation. The texture-synthesis machinery never uses the fact that a frame is an image — it only needs a sequence of vectors in a metric space and a distance between them. Word2Vec turns a text into exactly that: token $t \mapsto \mathbf{y}_t = \mathbf{e}(w_t)\in\mathbb{R}^d$, with semantically similar words close. So a sentence is a video whose frames are word vectors, and both synthesis algorithms port directly.

Method A — Video Textures on words (main answer). (1) Tokenise the input and embed: $\mathbf{y}_1..\mathbf{y}_N$. (2) $D_{ij} = \|\mathbf{y}_i-\mathbf{y}_j\|^2$. (3) Preserve dynamics with a windowed filter $D'_{ij}=\sum_k a_k D_{i+k,j+k}$ (the $n$-gram/context constraint; without it, jumps are locally right and grammatically wrong). (4) Transition probabilities $P_{ij}\propto\exp(-D'_{i+1,j}/\sigma^2)$ — the cost of jumping from $i$ to $j$ is how well $j$ substitutes for the actual successor $i{+}1$. Prune to the best few per row and renormalise; optionally add the $Q$-learning future cost $D''_{ij}=(D'_{ij})^p+\beta\min_k D''_{jk}$ to avoid dead ends. (5) Random-walk the chain, emitting $w_i$ at each visit, until a page is produced. The output is made of real words in locally-attested orders, with invisible seams, and is unbounded in length.

Method B — linear dynamic texture on words. Fit the state-space model $\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=A\mathbf{x}_t+B\mathbf{v}_t,\ \mathbf{y}_t=C\mathbf{x}_t$ to the embedded sequence by the SVD identification of the chapter: $Y \approx U_n\Sigma_nV_n^{\!\top}$, $\hat C=U_n$, $\hat X=\Sigma_nV_n^{\!\top}$, $\hat A = X_{2:N}X_{1:N-1}^{+}$, and $\hat B$ from the covariance of the residual $V = X_{2:N}-\hat AX_{1:N-1}$. Seed $\mathbf{x}_1$ from the input's final state and iterate the model as long as you like. Because a synthesised $\mathbf{y}_t$ is not a word, add a decoding step: $\hat w_t=\arg\min_{w\in V}\|\mathbf{y}_t-\mathbf{e}(w)\|$ (cosine NN, kd-tree/ANN), and re-project $\mathbf{x}_t \leftarrow \hat C^{\!\top}\mathbf{e}(\hat w_t)$ each step so the trajectory stays on the word manifold. Scale $\hat A$'s spectral radius to just under 1 for a stationary, non-decaying texture.

Trade-off and limitation. A produces readable text but only recombines the input; B can emit words outside the input but drifts. Both are locally plausible and globally meaningless, exactly as a synthesised video texture is locally convincing and has no plot — because Word2Vec is context-free and $A$ is linear. The context window in A (and the state dimension $n$ in B) controls how much local structure survives.

6 · Exam drill

Q. Why is the jump cost $D_{i+1,j}$ and not $D_{i,j}$?

Because you are choosing what to show next. Having just shown $i$, the viewer expects $i{+}1$. If you show $j$ instead, the discontinuity they perceive is the difference between what they expected ($i{+}1$) and what they got ($j$). So the seam is $\|\mathbf{y}_{i+1}-\mathbf{y}_j\|^2 = D_{i+1,j}$. Using $D_{ij}$ would compare the current frame with the next one, which measures nothing useful — the trivial "jump" $j = i$ would look free while actually freezing the video.

Q. Why does single-word matching fail, and what exactly does the window fix?

Same reason a ball at the apex of its arc is ambiguous in Video Textures: identical appearance, opposite dynamics. In text, "the" appears in dozens of positions with identical embedding, so a single-word match makes all of them equally legal jump targets, and you can leap from "the cat sat" into "the bureaucracy of". The window forces $\mathbf{y}_{i+k}\approx\mathbf{y}_{j+k}$ for several $k$ at once, which means the jump is only allowed where a whole phrase matches. That is literally an $n$-gram model, obtained from a video-processing filter.

Q. Euclidean or cosine distance on the embeddings?

Cosine, essentially always — Word2Vec's semantics live in the directions, while the norm mostly encodes word frequency (rare words get long vectors). Using raw Euclidean distance makes frequent function words look artificially close to each other and far from everything, which corrupts the whole matrix. The standard fix is to $\ell_2$-normalise every vector first; then $\|\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\|^2 = 2(1-\cos\theta)$, so Euclidean and cosine order pairs identically and you can keep every formula in the chapter unchanged.

Q. What is $\sigma$ doing, and how should you set it?

$\sigma$ converts costs to probabilities and is a temperature. $\sigma \to 0$: only the zero-cost transition survives, i.e. $i \to i{+}1$ — you replay the input verbatim and stop at the end. $\sigma \to \infty$: all transitions equally likely — uniform random word salad. Set it relative to the typical non-trivial cost, e.g. $\sigma$ = a small multiple of the average of the smallest few off-diagonal entries per row. Identical role, identical tuning heuristic, as in Video Textures.

Q. In route 2, what does the spectral radius of $A$ control?

Everything about the long-run behaviour. If $\rho(A)<1$ the deterministic part decays and the process is driven purely by noise around a fixed point — the decoded output collapses to one word repeated (the text version of a video texture that fades to a still frame). If $\rho(A)>1$ the state blows up and the decoded word is whichever vocabulary vector happens to lie furthest out in that direction — again, one word forever. You want $\rho(A)$ just below 1, so the system is marginally stable and the noise keeps exciting it. In practice you identify $\hat A$ and then rescale its eigenvalues to $\min(|\lambda|, 0.98)$ — the same stabilisation done for video dynamic textures.

Q. How do you seed the generator from the given short text?

Route 1: start the walk at the index of the input's last token, so the generated page continues the input rather than restarting it (and the input itself is your entire "video", so it doubles as the training set). Route 2: run the identified system's state estimate over the input — i.e. project, $\mathbf{x}_t = \hat C^{\!\top}\mathbf{y}_t$ — and take $\mathbf{x}_N$ as the seed. Both are the analogue of starting a video texture at the last frame of the clip.

Q. Can you combine the two routes?

Yes, and it is the best answer if you have room. Use route 2's linear system to propose a direction in embedding space (where the meaning is drifting), but restrict the actual choice of the next token to positions in the input text, scored by route 1's transition probability — i.e. sample $j$ from $P_{ij}\cdot\exp(-\|\mathbf{y}_j - \hat A\mathbf{x}_t\|^2/\tau)$. You get route 1's readability with route 2's ability to keep moving somewhere new instead of orbiting the input. This is the same hybrid idea as constraining a video texture's random walk with a user-specified motion target.

Q. Complexity and scaling?

Route 1: $O(N^2 d)$ to build $D$ and $O(N^2 m)$ to filter it, where $N$ is the length of the input text, not the output — so for a paragraph ($N\sim100$) it is trivial, and generation is $O(1)$ per emitted word. Route 2: one thin SVD of a $d\times N$ matrix, $O(dN\min(d,N))$, then per word $O(n^2)$ for the state update plus a nearest-neighbour query over the vocabulary — do that with a kd-tree or approximate NN in $O(\log|V|)$ rather than $O(|V|d)$, since $|V|$ can be millions.
