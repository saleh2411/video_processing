Companion page to Chapter 11 · Moed B 2022, question 5 · 13 points

Keep the best k, not the best one

PatchMatch works because a good match, found anywhere by luck, gets passed along to its neighbours. That mechanism does not care how many matches each pixel is holding. So the extension is almost mechanical: replace the single stored offset with a bounded max-heap of size $k$, and change "is this better than my match?" into "is this better than my worst match?". The interesting parts are the three details that break if you are careless: duplicates, diversity, and where random search should be centred.

Moed B · 1/8/2022 · Question 5 · 13 points · PATCHMATCH

PatchMatch returns, for every patch in the source image, its nearest neighbor in the target image. We can term this as 1-NN PatchMatch.

Suggest how to extend it to $k$-NN PatchMatch that returns, for each patch in the source image, a list of the top $k$ Nearest Neighbors in the target image ($k$ is a parameter supplied by the user).

1 · The story

The kid looking for their friend in a huge crowd

You have a photo of a face and a stadium full of people, and you must find who looks most like the photo. Checking everyone takes forever.

PatchMatch's trick #1 — luck is cheap when you buy a lot of it. Everyone in your own stadium picks a random stranger in the other stadium. Most of these guesses are rubbish. But there are so many guesses that somebody got lucky.

Trick #2 — good news travels. If the person on your left found a great match, then the person standing next to their match is probably a great match for you — because you and your neighbour look similar, and so do people standing next to each other over there. So each person just asks their left and top neighbour: "who did you find? let me try the seat next to them." One lucky guess spreads across the whole stadium in a single sweep.

Trick #3 — then look around a bit. Once you have a decent match, search near it — first in a big circle, then half that, then half again. Fine-tune.

Now the question: you don't want the single best look-alike, you want the top 5. What changes? Almost nothing! Instead of remembering one name, you carry a small notebook with 5 lines, kept sorted worst-at-the-top. When somebody suggests a candidate you compare it against the worst line; if it beats it, you cross that line out and write the new name. And your neighbours don't hand you one suggestion any more — they hand you all 5 of theirs.

Two traps, both obvious once you name them:

  • Don't write the same person 5 times. Your neighbour's 5 suggestions might all be the same seat. Check before inserting.
  • Don't write 5 people sitting in the same row. Seats 40, 41, 42, 43, 44 look almost identical, so all five will score well — and you learn nothing. Require the entries to be spread out.
The whole answer, in one sentence Replace the per-pixel single offset with a bounded max-heap of $k$ (offset, distance) pairs keyed by distance; propagate all $k$ of a neighbour's offsets; run random search around each (or a randomly chosen) heap entry; and guard insertion with a duplicate test and a minimum-offset-separation test. Cost per iteration goes from $O(n)$ to $O(nk\log k)$, memory from $O(n)$ to $O(nk)$.

2 · Recap — the 1-NN algorithm you are modifying

PatchMatch (Barnes et al., SIGGRAPH 2009)

Goal: the nearest-neighbour field $f:\, S \to \mathbb{R}^2$ mapping each patch centre $p$ in the source to an offset $f(p)$ such that the patch at $p+f(p)$ in the target minimises a patch distance $D$ (usually SSD over a $w\times w$ window). Brute force is $O(|S|\,|T|\,w^2)$ — hopeless. PatchMatch gets a good approximation in a few sweeps using three stages:

  1. Initialisation — $f(p)$ random (or from a coarser level / a rough prior).
  2. Propagation — on even iterations scan top-left → bottom-right and test the candidates $f(p-(1,0))$ and $f(p-(0,1))$ at $p$; on odd iterations reverse the scan order and use $f(p+(1,0)),f(p+(0,1))$. Rests on the coherence assumption: neighbouring patches usually have neighbouring matches.
  3. Random search — test $u_i = f(p) + w\alpha^i \mathbf{R}_i$ for $i=0,1,2,\dots$ with $\mathbf{R}_i$ uniform in $[-1,1]^2$, $\alpha=\tfrac12$, until $w\alpha^i<1$. Exponentially decreasing radius = a coarse-to-fine local search that also lets a pixel escape a bad local minimum.

Complexity: $O(|S|\,w^2\log|T|)$ per iteration; 4–5 iterations usually suffice.

3 · The extension, stage by stage

The three details that actually matter

(a) Duplicates

Propagation shifts a neighbour's offsets by one pixel, and random search perturbs existing ones — both routinely regenerate an offset already in the heap. Without a check, a heap of $k=5$ happily fills with five copies of one match and the answer is worthless. Keep a hash set (or a small linear scan, since $k$ is tiny) of the offsets currently in $H_p$ and reject exact repeats in $O(1)$.

(b) Diversity — the deeper version of the same problem

Offsets $u$ and $u+(1,0)$ are not duplicates, but the patches they point to overlap in $w{-}1$ of their $w$ columns, so they are near-identical matches. Left alone, the top $k$ collapse into one blob: you get the best match and its four immediate neighbours instead of $k$ genuinely different places in the target. Enforce a minimum separation

$$ \forall\, u' \in H_p:\quad \|u - u'\|_\infty \;\ge\; \delta \qquad (\delta \approx w \text{ is a good default}) \tag{1} $$

A softer variant, better when the target has smooth repetitions: allow the insertion but treat entries within $\delta$ as competing for the same slot — i.e. replace the near-by entry if the new one is better, otherwise reject. This is non-maximum suppression over the offset space, and it is what makes the output usable for symmetry detection, denoising and clone detection.

(c) Enrichment — the accelerator the k-NN paper adds

With $k$ candidates in hand you can generate new ones for free by composition, no image distance evaluated to propose them:

  • Forward enrichment. If $a \in H_p$ and $b \in H_a$ (the heap of the pixel that $a$ points at), then $b\circ a$ is a plausible candidate for $p$: "a good match of my good match". Generates $k^2$ proposals per pixel.
  • Inverse enrichment. If $a \in H_p$, then $p$ is a plausible candidate for $a$ — push your own offset backwards to the pixel you matched. Fills in pixels that propagation has not reached.

Enrichment spreads information through the match graph rather than through the image grid, so it converges much faster than propagation alone when the good matches are scattered rather than contiguous — exactly the regime $k$-NN is used in.

The tempting wrong answer "Just run 1-NN PatchMatch $k$ times with different random seeds and collect the results." It seems reasonable and it is strictly worse, for three reasons. (1) No guarantee of the top $k$ — nothing stops all $k$ runs converging to the same best match, which is in fact the likely outcome, since propagation is what makes PatchMatch work and it drives every run toward the same strong minimum. (2) Wasted work — $k$ independent runs cost $k$ full passes but share no information; the heap version pays $O(k)$ per pixel and shares everything. (3) No control of diversity — you cannot enforce (1) across independent runs without a merge step afterwards, which is the heap you were trying to avoid. Demo 1 measures exactly this gap; mention the idea in the exam, then reject it with the first reason.

4 · Pseudocode

for each p in S:                         # INITIALISE
    H[p] = empty max-heap (capacity k)
    repeat k times: try_insert(p, random_offset())

for it in 1..T:                          # ITERATE
    order = raster if it odd else reverse-raster
    d1,d2 = (-1,0),(0,-1) if it odd else (+1,0),(0,+1)

    for p in order:                                        # PROPAGATION
        for q in (p+d1, p+d2):
            if q inside S:
                for (dist,u) in H[q]:        # all k of them, best first
                    try_insert(p, u)         # note: u, not u+(p-q); the offset is what propagates

        for (dist,u) in H[p]:                               # RANDOM SEARCH
            w = max(W_T, H_T); i = 0
            while w * alpha**i >= 1:
                try_insert(p, u + round(w*alpha**i * uniform([-1,1]^2)))
                i += 1

    for p in S:                                            # ENRICHMENT (optional)
        for (dist,a) in H[p]:
            for (dist2,b) in H[p+a]: try_insert(p, a+b)     # forward
            try_insert(p+a, -a)                             # inverse


def try_insert(p, u):
    if p+u outside T: return
    if u in offsetset[p]: return                    # (a) exact duplicate
    if any(||u - u'||_inf < delta for u' in H[p]): # (b) diversity / NMS
        u0 = the offending entry
        if D(p, u) >= D(p, u0): return
        remove u0 from H[p]
    d = D(p, u)                                     # SSD over the w x w patch
    if |H[p]| < k:            push (d,u)
    elif d < H[p].root.dist:  pop root; push (d,u)  # O(log k)

6 · Why you would want this

What $k$ neighbours buy you
ApplicationWhy 1-NN is not enough
Non-local means / BM3D denoisingYou average over many similar patches to cancel noise. With one neighbour there is nothing to average; the variance reduction is $\propto 1/k$.
Symmetry & repetition detectionA repeated element by definition has several equally good matches. The set of offsets is the answer — its structure (a lattice, a reflection) is the symmetry.
Clone / copy-paste forgery detection (Moed A Q5)Self-matching needs $k>1$ because the trivial match (a patch to itself, distance 0) always wins slot 1; the evidence lives in slot 2 and beyond.
Image completion & retargetingVoting from $k$ candidates instead of copying one avoids the seams and the "winner-takes-all" artefacts, and gives you a confidence measure (how much do the top $k$ disagree?).
Label transfer / correspondenceA ranked shortlist lets a global optimiser (MRF, graph cut) pick a spatially coherent choice per pixel instead of being stuck with the local argmin.

7 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

One page, as the exam asks

Key observation. PatchMatch's three stages — random initialisation, propagation, random search — are all "propose a candidate offset, keep it if it is good". Nothing in them depends on how many matches a pixel stores. So $k$-NN PatchMatch is obtained by changing the per-pixel state and the acceptance test, leaving the algorithm's structure intact.

Data structure. Each source pixel $p$ keeps a bounded max-heap $H_p$ of at most $k$ pairs $(D(p,u), u)$ ordered by distance, so the root is the worst of the current $k$, plus a hash set of the offsets it holds. Insertion: if $|H_p|

Modified stages. (i) Initialise with $k$ distinct random offsets per pixel. (ii) Propagate: from each scan-order neighbour $q$, offer all $k$ offsets of $H_q$ to $p$ (they are re-evaluated at $p$). (iii) Random search: around each entry of $H_p$ (or one entry chosen at random per iteration), test $u + w\alpha^i\mathbf{R}_i$ with the usual exponentially shrinking radius until it drops below a pixel. Alternate scan direction each iteration exactly as before, 4–5 iterations.

Two guards on insertion. (a) Duplicates: reject an offset already present (propagation and random search regenerate existing offsets constantly). (b) Diversity: reject — or replace, if better — any candidate with $\|u-u'\|_\infty < \delta$ for some $u'\in H_p$, with $\delta$ of the order of the patch width. Without (b), overlapping patches around a single strong match fill all $k$ slots and the result carries no more information than 1-NN.

Optional accelerator (enrichment). Use the match graph to propose candidates for free: forward, $b\circ a$ for $a\in H_p,\,b\in H_{p+a}$ ("a good match of my good match"), and inverse, offering $-a$ to pixel $p+a$. This propagates information between distant but similar regions, which plain grid propagation cannot do, and markedly speeds up convergence for large $k$.

Cost. Memory $O(nk)$; per iteration $O(nk\,w^2\log|T|)$ patch evaluations — linear in $k$, versus $O(n|T|w^2)$ for exact top-$k$. Keep the standard early-termination of the SSD, now against the heap root.

Why not just run 1-NN $k$ times? Independent runs are not independent in outcome: propagation drives them to the same dominant minimum, so you may get the same match $k$ times and have no guarantee of covering the true top $k$; you also pay $k$ full passes without sharing information, and diversity cannot be enforced across runs without a merge step — i.e. without the heap.

8 · Exam drill

Q. Why a max-heap when you want the smallest distances?

Because the operation you perform millions of times is "is this candidate better than the worst thing I am keeping?" — and to answer that in $O(1)$ you need the worst element at the root. The heap is a bounded top-$k$ container: the max-heap of size $k$ over distances holds the $k$ smallest, with the largest of them immediately visible for eviction. A min-heap would put the best at the root, which is the one element you never need to touch during the algorithm; you would have to scan all $k$ entries for every rejection. (At the end you sort the $k$ entries once for output.) With $k$ small — 4 to 16 in practice — a plain sorted array with insertion is equally fine and has better cache behaviour; the heap matters asymptotically.

Q. In propagation, do you shift the neighbour's offset or copy it?

Copy it. The state is the offset $u = f(p) $, not the absolute target coordinate. The coherence assumption is precisely that neighbouring source patches have the same offset (they belong to the same coherently-moving/matching region), so testing $u_q$ unchanged at $p$ is exactly testing "the seat next to the one my neighbour found". If you stored absolute positions you would have to shift them by $p-q$, which is the same thing written less conveniently. Note it is still tested honestly: $D(p, u_q)$ is recomputed at $p$, so a bad guess is rejected.

Q. Why must random search be centred on more than the best entry?

Because random search is the only stage that refines an offset. Entries 2..$k$ arrive from random initialisation or from a neighbour and are typically a few pixels off their local optimum; if you never search around them they stay coarse, and their distances stay high, and the "top $k$" is really "the top 1 plus $k-1$ mediocre ones". The thorough version searches around every entry ($O(k\log|T|)$ tests per pixel); the cheap version picks one entry uniformly at random each iteration, which over 4–5 iterations refines all of them in expectation at $1/k$ of the cost, and is what you would implement in practice.

Q. How should $\delta$ be chosen, and what if the target genuinely has two good matches one pixel apart?

$\delta \approx w$ (the patch width) is the standard choice: it means two entries must point at patches that overlap in less than half their area, so they carry mostly independent evidence. If the application genuinely wants sub-$\delta$ resolution — e.g. sub-pixel-accurate correspondence — use the soft variant: entries within $\delta$ compete for a single slot (non-maximum suppression) rather than being hard-rejected, so the best member of each cluster survives and you get $k$ local minima rather than $k$ arbitrary points. That is the more principled formulation: you want the top $k$ modes, not the top $k$ samples.

Q. Does the convergence guarantee still hold?

PatchMatch never had a hard guarantee — it is a randomised approximation, and the argument is probabilistic: with $n$ random initial guesses, the probability that at least one pixel in a coherent region of size $m$ gets a good offset is $1-(1-m/|T|)^n$, which is essentially 1 for image-sized $n$; propagation then distributes it. That argument is unchanged for $k$-NN — in fact it is stronger, since you draw $nk$ initial samples instead of $n$, so the chance of seeding each of the $k$ true modes goes up. What you do lose is monotonicity of any single slot: an entry can be evicted by a better one, so a particular offset does not survive forever. The multiset's worst distance is monotone non-increasing, which is the quantity plotted in Demo 1.

Q. How does this interact with the coarse-to-fine pyramid?

The same way as 1-NN, and it helps more. Initialise level $\ell$'s heaps by upsampling level $\ell+1$'s heaps (each offset doubled), which gives $k$ already-good starting points instead of $k$ random ones, so one or two iterations per level suffice. It also stabilises the modes: a repetition that is visible at a coarse scale seeds all its copies before fine-scale noise can distract the search. The only care needed is that the diversity radius must be scaled with the level, $\delta_\ell = \delta/2^\ell$ in offset units, or you will over-suppress at coarse levels.

Q. Sketch how you would use the output for clone detection.

Run $k$-NN PatchMatch with source = target = the same image, $k$ small (say 3), and a diversity radius large enough to exclude the trivial neighbourhood. Slot 1 is always the patch itself ($u=0$, $D=0$) — discard it, or exclude $\|u\| < \tau$ outright. Then look at slot 2: a clone produces a large region of pixels that all share nearly the same non-trivial offset $v$, and — because a copy-paste is symmetric — the pasted region shows the offset $-v$. So build a histogram of offsets, take the peaks, and verify each candidate offset by checking that the set of pixels supporting it is spatially connected and reasonably large. This is exactly the Moed A Q5 answer, and it is the cleanest example of why $k>1$ is necessary rather than merely nice.
