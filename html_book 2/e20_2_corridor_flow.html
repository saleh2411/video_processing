The wall you are closer to whooshes past faster

A robot drives down a corridor and wants to know if it is centred. It has no ruler, no depth sensor, no idea of its own speed — only optic flow. The answer is three lines long and every unknown quantity cancels out.

Exam 8/7/2020 · Question 2 · 12 points · OPTIC FLOW

A robot equipped with a forward facing camera is moving down a straight and long corridor. The floor, walls and ceiling of the corridor are textured, so optic flow algorithms work well. Let $A$ denote the distance from the camera center of projection to the left wall and let $B$ denote the distance from the camera center of projection to the right wall. Suggest a way to use optic flow to determine if $A = B$.

The 30-second version — read this first Under pure forward translation the flow is radial from the FOE, and for a pixel at image column $x$ looking at a side wall, $\;|u(x)| = \dfrac{x^2 T_z}{f\,A}$.

So take two pixels symmetric about the FOE, $x=+x_0$ and $x=-x_0$, and compare flow magnitudes: $$\frac{|u(-x_0)|}{|u(+x_0)|}=\frac{B}{A}.$$ Equal flow ⟺ $A=B$. Speed $T_z$ and focal length $f$ cancel — you never need to know them.

1 · The story (no math at all)

The bike ride between two hedges

You are riding your bike down a path. On your left is a hedge, on your right is another hedge. You want to know: am I in the middle, or am I closer to one of them? You are not allowed to look down, not allowed to measure anything, and you don't even know how fast you're going.

Here is the thing every kid already knows: things that are close whoosh past fast; things that are far away crawl. The moon “follows you” in the car because it is enormously far away. The fence right next to the window is a blur.

So: listen to the whoosh on each side.

  • Left hedge whooshing faster than the right one → you are hugging the left hedge → A < B.
  • Right one faster → you are hugging the right one → B < A.
  • Both the same → you are dead centre → A = B.

“Whoosh speed” is exactly what optic flow measures. That's the whole question. The only care needed is where you listen: you have to compare the same place on each side — same distance out from the middle of your view — because the whoosh is faster at the edges of your vision than straight ahead, on both sides.

Why the “same place on each side” part matters Straight ahead — the point you are driving into — nothing moves at all. That point is the Focus of Expansion (FOE). Everything streams away from it, faster and faster as you look further out to the side. Compare a pixel near the edge on the left with a pixel near the centre on the right and you learn nothing. Compare mirror-image pixels and the geometry does the rest.

2 · The math, from the two formulas you already know

Ingredient 1 — perspective projection

Camera frame: $Z$ forward along the corridor, $X$ to the right, $Y$ down. A 3-D point $(X,Y,Z)$ lands at

$$ x = f\frac{X}{Z}, \qquad y = f\frac{Y}{Z} $$
Ingredient 2 — the motion field of a translating camera

For a camera translating with $T=(T_x,T_y,T_z)$ and no rotation, differentiating the projection equations gives the standard motion field

$$ u = \frac{-f\,T_x + x\,T_z}{Z}, \qquad v = \frac{-f\,T_y + y\,T_z}{Z} $$

The robot drives straight down the corridor, so $T_x=T_y=0$, $T_z>0$, and this collapses to

$$ (u,v) = \frac{T_z}{Z}\,(x,\,y) $$

Read that out loud: the flow points radially away from the image origin, its length grows linearly with distance from the origin, and it is inversely proportional to depth. The origin is the FOE (here the principal point, because the motion is along the optical axis). Two facts fall out immediately:

  • you cannot get $Z$ on its own — only the ratio $T_z/Z$. Absolute scale is unobservable from one camera. That is fine: the question asks for a comparison, not a distance.
  • flow grows with $|x|$, so “where you sample” matters (this is the point of the callout above).

Ingredient 3 — the walls turn depth into image position

The left wall is the plane $X=-A$; the right wall is $X=+B$. Both run parallel to the direction of travel. For a point on the left wall at depth $Z$,

$$ x = f\frac{-A}{Z} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; Z = \frac{f\,A}{|x|} \qquad (x<0) $$

This is the crucial substitution: on a side wall, image column tells you depth. Feed it back into the flow:

$$ |u(x)| \;=\; \frac{|x|\,T_z}{Z} \;=\; \frac{|x|\,T_z}{f A/|x|} \;=\; \boxed{\;\frac{x^2\,T_z}{f\,A}\;} \qquad\text{(left wall)},\qquad |u(x)| = \frac{x^2\,T_z}{f\,B}\quad\text{(right wall)} $$
The test Evaluate both at the same $|x| = x_0$ and divide. Everything except the two distances cancels: $$\frac{|u(-x_0)|}{|u(+x_0)|} \;=\; \frac{x_0^2T_z/(fA)}{x_0^2T_z/(fB)} \;=\; \frac{B}{A}$$ So: ratio $=1 \iff A=B$. Ratio $>1$ → left flow faster → $A
You get the ratio of the distances for free, not just the yes/no answer — enough to steer.

3 · Making it robust — what a good answer adds

One pixel pair works on paper. In a real robot you would do all of the following, and each is worth a line in the answer:

a. Find the FOE instead of assuming it

If the camera is even slightly misaligned or the robot yaws, the flow is not centred on the principal point. Estimate the FOE as the point where the flow field vanishes (least-squares intersection of the flow vectors' lines), and take your symmetric pairs about that point. If the robot is also rotating, the rotational component of the flow is depth-independent, so it adds the same bias to both sides only after de-rotation — either estimate and subtract it, or rely on the fact that pure yaw adds a constant horizontal term which you can cancel by using the ceiling and floor instead (see c).

b. Fit a curve rather than trust two pixels

Both walls give $|u| = c\,x^2$ with $c_L = T_z/(fA)$ and $c_R = T_z/(fB)$. Take all flow vectors on each wall and least-squares fit the coefficient: $\hat c = \sum x_i^2|u_i| / \sum x_i^4$. Then $A/B = \hat c_R/\hat c_L$. Averaging over thousands of pixels kills noise and the effect of individual bad matches.

c. A cheaper symmetric statistic

Since $|u|$ is an even function of $x$ when $A=B$, you can simply test whether the flow-magnitude image is left–right symmetric about the FOE: $\;S=\sum_{x_0}\big(|u(-x_0)|-|u(+x_0)|\big)$. $S=0$ ⟺ centred; the sign of $S$ tells you which way to steer. This is the classic biologically-inspired “balance the optic flow” corridor-centring rule (bees do exactly this).

d. Use only wall pixels

The formula $|u|=x^2T_z/(fA)$ is derived for the side wall plane. Pixels on the far end of the corridor, on the floor, or on a passing person have different depth laws, so segment them out — e.g. keep only pixels whose flow follows the $x^2$ law (RANSAC on the fit in b), which simultaneously rejects outliers.

4 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

Full-marks version — one page

Model. Put the camera frame with $Z$ along the corridor, $X$ to the right, $Y$ down; then $x=fX/Z$, $y=fY/Z$. The robot translates with $T=(0,0,T_z)$ and no rotation, so the motion field $u=\frac{-fT_x+xT_z}{Z},\ v=\frac{-fT_y+yT_z}{Z}$ reduces to

$$ (u,v)=\frac{T_z}{Z}(x,y) $$

i.e. flow is purely radial, expanding away from the FOE, which for motion along the optical axis is the principal point $(0,0)$.

Key substitution. The left wall is the plane $X=-A$, so a wall point imaged at column $x<0$ has depth $Z=fA/|x|$. Substituting,

$$ |u(x)| = \frac{|x|T_z}{Z} = \frac{x^2T_z}{fA}\quad(\text{left wall}),\qquad |u(x)| = \frac{x^2T_z}{fB}\quad(\text{right wall}). $$

The test. Compute optic flow between consecutive frames. Pick a pair of pixels symmetric about the FOE, one on each wall, at columns $-x_0$ and $+x_0$. Then

$$ \frac{|u(-x_0)|}{|u(+x_0)|} \;=\; \frac{B}{A} $$

(i) estimate the FOE from the flow field rather than assuming the principal point, so small yaw/misalignment does not bias the comparison; (ii) instead of a single pair, least-squares fit $|u| = c\,x^2$ separately on each wall — $\hat c=\sum x_i^2|u_i|/\sum x_i^4$ — and use $A/B=\hat c_R/\hat c_L$; use RANSAC on this fit to reject pixels that are not on the walls (far end of the corridor, floor, moving people); (iii) equivalently, test the left–right symmetry of the flow-magnitude image about the FOE, $S=\sum_{x_0}(|u(-x_0)|-|u(+x_0)|)$, and steer to drive $S$ to zero. This requires texture on the walls, which the question grants.

5 · Exam drill

Q. Why can't you just compute the depth $Z$ of each wall and compare?

Because a single translating camera only ever gives you $T_z/Z$ — the flow equations contain the translation and the depth as a product/quotient, never separately. This is the classic scale ambiguity of monocular structure-from-motion: a slow robot in a narrow corridor and a fast robot in a wide corridor produce exactly the same video. The question is well-posed precisely because a ratio of two depths is scale-free, so the unknown $T_z$ cancels.

Q. Where does the $x^2$ come from? It feels suspicious.

Two effects multiply. (1) Flow grows linearly with distance from the FOE: $|u|\propto|x|$. (2) On a wall parallel to your motion, looking further sideways means looking at a nearer part of the wall, so $Z\propto 1/|x|$, i.e. $1/Z\propto|x|$ — another factor of $|x|$. Multiply: $|u|\propto x^2$. Physically: the wall texture is nearly frozen far ahead near the FOE, and it blurs violently at the image edge.

Q. What if the robot is also rotating (yaw)?

Then the full flow has a rotational part which is independent of depth — it adds the same vector field to every pixel regardless of what is being looked at. That breaks the naive symmetry test, because a constant horizontal bias makes one side look faster. Fixes: subtract the rotational component (estimated e.g. from a gyro, or from distant points whose translational flow $\approx0$); or move the FOE to where the flow actually vanishes and measure symmetry about it; or use the vertical flow on the ceiling and floor, which yaw affects only weakly.

Q. The corridor's far wall is also in view. Does it corrupt the test?

Yes if you include it. The far wall is a plane perpendicular to the motion, $Z=Z_0$ constant, so its flow is $|u|=|x|T_z/Z_0$ — linear in $|x|$, not quadratic, and it is symmetric about the FOE no matter where the robot is. Including it biases the comparison toward “centred”. That is exactly why step (d) — fit the $x^2$ law and reject everything that does not obey it — is part of a good answer.

Q. Which pixels give the most reliable measurement?

Ones far from the FOE (large $|x|$): the flow is large there, so the signal-to-noise ratio of the flow estimate is best, and $|u|\propto x^2$ makes the difference between $A$ and $B$ maximally visible. Near the FOE all flow vanishes and any difference is swamped by noise. The trade-off is that large $|x|$ means nearby wall → large displacement per frame → the linearised brightness-constancy assumption may break, so use a coarse-to-fine pyramid, or a faster frame rate.

Q. Sketch how you'd get $A$ and $B$ in metres, not just the ratio.

You need one extra piece of metric information, because vision alone is scale-blind. Any of: wheel odometry or an IMU giving $T_z$ (then $A = x^2T_z/(f|u|)$ directly from the same formula); a known-size object in view; or a second camera (stereo baseline). This is worth mentioning in an exam answer because it shows you know why the question was phrased as “is $A=B$” rather than “what is $A$”.
