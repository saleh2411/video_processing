Anomaly = “my model of normal gives this a terrible score”

You are handed the model for free: a 3-component GMM. So the detector is one line — threshold the log-likelihood. The marks are in what you wrap around it: how you pick the threshold, why you must not alarm on a single second, and the fact that this is literally background subtraction with sound instead of pixels.

Exam 8/7/2020 · Question 8 · 12 points · ANOMALY DETECTION

Machines in a factory make sound. This sound changes if the machine is about to break. It is therefore useful to record the sound of the machine and give a warning in case an abnormal sound is detected.

Your goal is to design such a system. Assume a microphone records the sound of the machine and produces a feature vector $X_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ every second. You know that the signal $X$ over time fits a Gaussian Mixture Model with $k=3$ Gaussians. Suggest a method to detect anomalies in the sound of the machine.

The 30-second version — read this first Train a $k=3$ GMM on healthy-machine features by EM (init with k-means, regularise $\Sigma_k+\varepsilon I$).
Score each new second by the log-likelihood under that model: $$\ell(X_t)=\log\sum_{k=1}^{3}\pi_k\,\mathcal N\!\big(X_t;\mu_k,\Sigma_k\big)$$
Decide: anomaly if $\ell(X_t)<\tau$, with $\tau$ = the 1st percentile of $\ell$ on the healthy training data (⇒ a 1% false-alarm rate by construction).
Alarm only when $M$ of the last $L$ seconds are flagged (e.g. 5 of 10) — one bad second is noise.
Adapt the GMM online exactly like Stauffer–Grimson background subtraction, but slowly, and freeze it while an alarm is active.

1 · The story (no math at all)

The dad who knows what the car sounds like

Your dad drives the same car every day for six years. He is not a mechanic. He cannot tell you what a camshaft is. But one morning he turns the key and says, before pulling out of the driveway: “something's wrong.” And he's right.

How did he do that? He was not comparing the sound to a list of broken-car sounds. He has never heard this particular fault before in his life. What he has is six years of what normal sounds like, and this did not fit.

That is the whole design. You never model the fault. You model normal, and complain about everything else. This matters practically: you have thousands of hours of a healthy machine and zero recordings of the failure you are trying to catch, because if you had them, the machine would already be broken.

Now, why three Gaussians and not one? Because “normal” is not one thing. The machine idles, it runs under load, it spins up. Three different normal noises. One Gaussian would have to stretch over all three and would then also happily cover the empty space between them — where the interesting broken sounds live. Three blobs describe three normals and leave the gaps correctly empty.

And why not alarm the instant one second sounds odd? Because someone drops a spanner. A truck goes past. One weird second is life; ten weird seconds out of the last twenty is a machine.

You have seen this exact algorithm before Chapter 8, background subtraction: each pixel's colour over time is modelled as a mixture of Gaussians; a new colour that matches a high-weight component is background, one that matches nothing is foreground. Swap “pixel colour” for “sound feature” and “foreground” for “anomaly” and it is the same system, including the online update rules. Saying this in the exam is worth marks.

2 · The model and the score

Step 1 — fit the model of “normal”

Collect $T$ feature vectors from a period of certified-healthy operation and fit

$$ p(X)=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\pi_k\,\mathcal N(X;\mu_k,\Sigma_k),\qquad \mathcal N(X;\mu,\Sigma)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}} e^{-\frac12 (X-\mu)^{\!\top}\Sigma^{-1}(X-\mu)},\qquad \sum_k\pi_k=1 $$

by EM, alternating until the log-likelihood converges:

$$ \text{E: } \gamma_{tk}=\frac{\pi_k\mathcal N(X_t;\mu_k,\Sigma_k)}{\sum_{j}\pi_j\mathcal N(X_t;\mu_j,\Sigma_j)} \qquad \text{M: } \pi_k=\frac{1}{T}\sum_t\gamma_{tk},\quad \mu_k=\frac{\sum_t\gamma_{tk}X_t}{\sum_t\gamma_{tk}},\quad \Sigma_k=\frac{\sum_t\gamma_{tk}(X_t-\mu_k)(X_t-\mu_k)^{\!\top}}{\sum_t\gamma_{tk}} $$

Initialise with k-means (EM is non-convex — same story as everywhere else in this course), and regularise $\Sigma_k \leftarrow \Sigma_k+\varepsilon I$ so a component cannot collapse onto a single point and send the likelihood to infinity. If $d$ is large relative to $T$, use diagonal covariances.

Step 2 — score a new second

The anomaly score is just the model's own opinion of the sample. Use logs — the raw density underflows:

$$ \ell(X_t)\;=\;\log\sum_{k=1}^{3}\pi_k\,\mathcal N(X_t;\mu_k,\Sigma_k) \;=\;\operatorname*{logsumexp}_k\Big[\log\pi_k-\tfrac{d}{2}\log 2\pi-\tfrac12\log|\Sigma_k| -\tfrac12 d^2_k(X_t)\Big] $$
$$ d^2_k(X)=(X-\mu_k)^{\!\top}\Sigma_k^{-1}(X-\mu_k) \quad\text{(squared Mahalanobis distance to component $k$)} $$

Two equivalent ways to declare an anomaly, both acceptable:

  • Likelihood test (preferred): anomaly $\iff \ell(X_t)<\tau$.
  • Gate test: anomaly $\iff \min_k d^2_k(X_t) > \chi^2_{d,\,1-\alpha}$ — “it is far from every component, in units of that component's own spread”. Simpler to explain, and it is the same $\chi^2$ gate as the tracking question, but it ignores the mixture weights $\pi_k$.
Why Mahalanobis and not Euclidean — again A component describing a loud, variable operating mode is a big fat ellipsoid; one describing a steady idle is a tight one. A deviation of “2 units” is normal for the first and alarming for the second. $\Sigma_k^{-1}$ is what converts physical units into surprise. And the $|\Sigma_k|^{-1/2}$ factor makes the components comparable to each other, exactly as in Q5.

3 · The three things that turn a score into a system

a. Choosing $\tau$ — do not guess it

You have no anomalous data, so you cannot optimise a detection rate. But you can control the false alarm rate directly: compute $\ell$ on a held-out slice of healthy data, and set

$$ \tau \;=\; \text{the }\alpha\text{-quantile of }\{\ell(X_t)\}_{\text{healthy}} \qquad(\alpha = 1\%\ \Rightarrow\ \text{1 false flag per 100 seconds by construction}) $$

Use a held-out slice, not the training data — the model was fitted to maximise the likelihood of the training set, so its $\ell$ values are optimistically high and $\tau$ would come out too low. If you ever do obtain labelled faults, tune $\tau$ on a ROC curve at the operating point you care about (in a factory, a missed failure costs far more than a false alarm, so you sit high on the curve).

b. Temporal integration — never alarm on one second

At $\alpha=1\%$ you get a spurious flag every ~100 s, which is unusable. Require persistence:

  • $M$-of-$L$ rule: alarm iff at least $M$ of the last $L$ seconds are flagged. If flags were independent, the false-alarm probability drops from $\alpha$ to $\sum_{i\ge M}\binom{L}{i}\alpha^i(1-\alpha)^{L-i}$ — e.g. $\alpha=1\%$, $M=5$, $L=10$ gives about $2\times10^{-7}$ per window, while a genuine fault (which persists) still trips it in 5 seconds.
  • Sliding-window score: alarm on $\bar\ell_W=\frac1W\sum_{s=t-W+1}^{t}\ell(X_s)<\tau_W$ — smoother, and it aggregates many slightly odd seconds that no single-second test would catch.
  • CUSUM: $g_t=\max(0,\,g_{t-1}+(\tau-\ell(X_t)))$, alarm when $g_t>h$. This is the optimal sequential change detector, and it is the right tool for a slow drift — a bearing degrading over hours — which the other two will miss.

c. Online adaptation — the Stauffer–Grimson update

Machines drift: temperature, lubrication, load schedule. A frozen model will slowly produce more and more false alarms. Update the mixture online, one second at a time, exactly as in background subtraction:

$$ \begin{aligned} &\text{match: } k^\star=\arg\min_k d^2_k(X_t)\ \text{ if } d_{k^\star}\le 2.5\sigma \text{ (else: no match)}\\[4pt] &\omega_k \leftarrow (1-\alpha_{\text{lr}})\,\omega_k + \alpha_{\text{lr}}\,\mathbb 1[k=k^\star]\qquad\text{(all }k)\\ &\mu_{k^\star} \leftarrow (1-\rho)\mu_{k^\star}+\rho X_t,\qquad \Sigma_{k^\star}\leftarrow(1-\rho)\Sigma_{k^\star}+\rho\,(X_t-\mu_{k^\star})(X_t-\mu_{k^\star})^{\!\top},\\ &\rho=\alpha_{\text{lr}}\,\mathcal N(X_t;\mu_{k^\star},\Sigma_{k^\star})\\[4pt] &\text{no match} \Rightarrow \text{replace the lowest-}\omega\text{ component by } (\omega_{\min},\,\mu=X_t,\,\Sigma=\sigma_0^2I) \end{aligned} $$
The trap in adaptation — say this and you get the mark If the model adapts freely, a slowly worsening fault is absorbed into “normal” and you never alarm. The fix is the same as in background subtraction, where a car that parks eventually becomes background: use a slow learning rate $\alpha_{\text{lr}}$ (a time constant of hours, not seconds), freeze the update while an alarm is active, and keep a frozen reference model from commissioning to compare against. A component whose weight $\omega_k$ is growing but which did not exist at commissioning is itself an alarm.

4 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

Full-marks version — one page

Principle. One-class / novelty detection: faults are unseen and unlabelled, so model only the normal sound and flag whatever the model finds improbable. The given GMM is exactly that model of normal; $k=3$ because the machine has several normal operating regimes (idle, load, spin-up), and a single Gaussian would smear over them and assign high likelihood to the empty space between them.

Training. Record features $X_t\in\mathbb R^d$ during certified-healthy operation and fit $p(X)=\sum_{k=1}^3\pi_k\mathcal N(X;\mu_k,\Sigma_k)$ by EM: E-step $\gamma_{tk}=\pi_k\mathcal N(X_t;\mu_k,\Sigma_k)/\sum_j\pi_j\mathcal N(X_t;\mu_j,\Sigma_j)$; M-step $\pi_k=\frac1T\sum_t\gamma_{tk}$, $\mu_k=\sum_t\gamma_{tk}X_t/\sum_t\gamma_{tk}$, $\Sigma_k=\sum_t\gamma_{tk}(X_t-\mu_k)(X_t-\mu_k)^{\!\top}/\sum_t\gamma_{tk}$. Initialise with k-means, and regularise $\Sigma_k+\varepsilon I$ (diagonal covariances if $d$ is large) to prevent a component collapsing onto one point.

Detection. For each new second compute the log-likelihood

$$ \ell(X_t)=\log\sum_{k=1}^{3}\pi_k\,\mathcal N(X_t;\mu_k,\Sigma_k) $$

(evaluated with log-sum-exp) and declare the second abnormal if $\ell(X_t)<\tau$. An equivalent formulation is the $\chi^2$ gate on the Mahalanobis distance to the closest component: $\min_k (X_t-\mu_k)^{\!\top}\Sigma_k^{-1}(X_t-\mu_k) > \chi^2_{d,1-\alpha}$ — “far from every mode, measured in units of that mode's own spread”.

Choosing the threshold. No anomalous data are available, but the false-alarm rate can be fixed directly: evaluate $\ell$ on a held-out healthy segment and set $\tau$ to its $\alpha$-quantile (e.g. $\alpha=1\%$). If labelled faults ever become available, place $\tau$ on the ROC curve at the desired operating point; in a factory the cost of a miss $\gg$ cost of a false alarm.

Temporal integration (essential). Do not alarm on one second. Raise a warning when $M$ of the last $L$ seconds are flagged (e.g. 5 of 10), which reduces the per-window false alarm probability to $\sum_{i\ge M}\binom{L}{i}\alpha^i(1-\alpha)^{L-i}$ while a persistent fault still trips it in seconds. Alternatively threshold the windowed mean $\bar\ell_W$, or run CUSUM $g_t=\max(0,g_{t-1}+\tau-\ell(X_t))$, which is the right detector for a slow degradation.

On-line adaptation. To follow slow drift (temperature, wear), update the mixture per sample as in Stauffer–Grimson background subtraction: find the component within $2.5\sigma$; update $\omega_k\leftarrow(1-\alpha_{lr})\omega_k+\alpha_{lr}\mathbb 1[k=k^\star]$, $\mu_{k^\star}\leftarrow(1-\rho)\mu_{k^\star}+\rho X_t$, $\Sigma_{k^\star}\leftarrow(1-\rho)\Sigma_{k^\star}+\rho(X_t-\mu_{k^\star})(X_t-\mu_{k^\star})^{\!\top}$ with $\rho=\alpha_{lr}\mathcal N(X_t;\mu_{k^\star},\Sigma_{k^\star})$; if nothing matches, replace the lowest-weight component with $(\mu=X_t,\ \Sigma=\sigma_0^2I,\ \omega=\omega_{\min})$ — and that "no match" event is itself the anomaly signal. Caveat: a slowly worsening fault would be learned as normal, so use a very slow $\alpha_{lr}$, freeze adaptation while an alarm is active, and keep the commissioning-time model as a frozen reference.

Practical notes. Features: log-mel band energies or MFCCs plus their deltas, standardised (whitened) so one loud band does not dominate the Mahalanobis distance. Validate with ROC/AUC on any labelled events; if $k$ were not given, choose it by BIC. Localise the fault by reporting which feature dimensions contribute most to $d^2_{k^\star}$, which turns the alarm into a diagnosis.

5 · Exam drill

Q. Why not train a classifier: “normal” vs “broken”?

Because you have no examples of the second class — and by construction you cannot get them without breaking machines. Even if you broke ten, the next failure mode will be one you never sampled, and a discriminative classifier is only ever reliable inside the region it was trained on. Density estimation of the normal class generalises to every deviation, including ones nobody anticipated. This is the defining reason anomaly detection is one-class.

Q. What exactly goes wrong with $k=1$? And with $k=20$?

$k=1$: the single Gaussian must cover all three operating modes, so it becomes wide and centred on the empty region between them — abnormal sounds that live in that gap receive a high likelihood and are missed. $k=20$: the mixture starts fitting individual training seconds, the covariances shrink, and the model memorises noise; then normal-but-unseen variation falls off the model and you drown in false alarms. This is the bias–variance trade-off in density estimation, and the usual instrument is BIC: $\text{BIC}=-2\ell_{\max}+p\log T$.

Q. Why is the log-likelihood the right score, rather than the distance to the nearest mean?

Three reasons, all visible in Demo 1. (i) It uses $\Sigma_k$, so it measures deviation relative to each mode's own spread instead of in raw units. (ii) It uses $\pi_k$, so being near a rare mode is less reassuring than being near a common one. (iii) It is a genuine probability, so the threshold has a meaning — a quantile of $\ell$ is a calibrated false-alarm rate, whereas a distance threshold is an arbitrary number you would have to tune blindly.

Q. Give the false-alarm arithmetic for the $M$-of-$L$ rule.

If a single second is flagged with probability $\alpha$ and flags are independent, a window of $L$ seconds contains $\ge M$ flags with probability $\sum_{i=M}^{L}\binom{L}{i}\alpha^i(1-\alpha)^{L-i}$. For $\alpha=0.01$, $M=5$, $L=10$: $\approx\binom{10}{5}10^{-10}\approx2.5\times10^{-8}$ — one spurious alarm per ~500 days instead of one per 100 seconds. Independence is optimistic (real background noise is correlated), so measure the empirical rate on healthy data rather than trusting the formula; but the order-of-magnitude argument is what justifies the design.

Q. How do you detect a fault that is slow?

A gradual degradation may never push any single second past $\tau$, yet shifts the whole distribution. Options: (i) CUSUM on $(\tau-\ell)$, which accumulates small consistent evidence and is the optimal sequential change detector; (ii) compare the distribution of the last hour against the commissioning distribution with a two-sample statistic (KL divergence between fitted GMMs, or a KS test per feature); (iii) monitor the model parameters themselves — if the online update is drifting $\mu_k$ steadily in one direction, that trend is the signal, even while every individual second still looks normal.

Q. Spell out the mapping to background subtraction.

Background subtraction: per pixel, model the history of colours as a GMM; sort components by $\omega/\sigma$ (frequent and stable first); take the top components summing to $T$ of the weight as "background"; a new pixel matching one of them within $2.5\sigma$ is background, otherwise foreground; update online with the $\omega,\mu,\sigma$ rules. Here: per machine, model the history of sound features as a GMM; components are the normal operating modes; a new second matching one of them is normal, otherwise an anomaly; same online update. Even the failure modes map across — a parked car absorbed into the background is a slow fault absorbed into "normal".
