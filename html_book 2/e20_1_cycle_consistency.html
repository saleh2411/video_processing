Companion page to Chapter 2 · Exam 8/7/2020, question 1 · 13 points

“If I point at you, you must point back at me”

Horn–Schunck, but run in both directions at once, with a third term that punishes the two answers when they disagree. The whole question is: write that third term, and push it through Euler–Lagrange. It turns out the final update equation barely changes.

Exam 8/7/2020 · Question 1 · 13 points · OPTIC FLOW

Cycle-consistency states that the flow from pixel $p$ to pixel $q$ should equal the (negative) flow from $q$ to $p$, where $p$ is a pixel in the first image and $q$ is a pixel in the second image. Modify the Horn–Schunck equations to encode the cycle-consistency prior.

The 30-second version — read this first Solve for two flow fields at once, $w$ (image 1 → 2) and $w'$ (image 2 → 1), and add one extra quadratic penalty $\;\lambda\int \big\| w(\mathbf{x}) + w'(\mathbf{x}+w(\mathbf{x}))\big\|^2 d\mathbf{x}$.

After Euler–Lagrange the classic HS iteration is unchanged in shape: you only (a) replace the neighbourhood average $\bar u$ by a blend of $\bar u$ and the negated backward flow, and (b) replace $\alpha$ by $\alpha+\lambda$.

1 · The story (no math at all)

Two kids and a photograph

Dana stands in the school yard on Monday. Yoni stands in the same yard on Tuesday. You give each of them one job:

  • Dana's job: “point at where the red ball ended up tomorrow.”
  • Yoni's job: “point at where the red ball came from yesterday.”

If both kids are telling the truth, their two arrows are the same arrow, just drawn backwards. Dana points 3 steps right; Yoni, standing where Dana pointed, must point 3 steps left.

Now suppose the ball is sitting on a big, plain, white wall — nothing around it to recognise. Dana guesses “3 steps right.” Yoni guesses “5 steps left.” Nobody can prove either of them wrong from the picture alone, because the wall looks the same everywhere.

Cycle consistency is a referee. It does not know the right answer either. But it can shout at the two kids: “you two disagree by 2 steps — one of you is wrong, fix it between you.” The referee's shouting is a cost you add to the equation, and the optimiser has to pay it unless the two arrows cancel.

Two liars who must agree with each other are much harder to fool than one liar on their own. That's the entire prior.

Why this is genuinely useful, not just cute In the flat/textureless regions where the aperture problem bites, plain Horn–Schunck has nothing pinning the answer down except the smoothness term dragging values in from far away — and it drags them in differently for the forward pass and the backward pass. Forcing the two passes to cancel gives the flat region a second, independent constraint for free, with no new images.

2 · Write the referee's shout as a formula

Where does the backward flow have to be sampled?

Careful — this is the one place people lose marks. $w(\mathbf{x})$ lives at pixel $\mathbf{x}$ in image 1. The matching pixel in image 2 is

$$ q \;=\; \mathbf{x} + w(\mathbf{x}) \qquad\text{(this is $p\to q$)} $$

So the backward flow you must compare against is not $w'(\mathbf{x})$, it is $w'$ evaluated at $q$. The condition “flow $p\to q$ equals minus flow $q\to p$” is

$$ w'\big(\mathbf{x}+w(\mathbf{x})\big) \;=\; -\,w(\mathbf{x}) \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \underbrace{w(\mathbf{x}) + w'\big(\mathbf{x}+w(\mathbf{x})\big)}_{\textstyle c(\mathbf{x})\;=\;\text{cycle residual}} \;=\; 0 $$

Start at $p$, walk forward, walk backward — you must land back on $p$. That round trip is what the word cycle means. Penalise the residual quadratically, exactly like every other Horn–Schunck term:

$$ E_{\text{cyc}} \;=\; \lambda \int_\Psi \Big\| w(\mathbf{x}) + w'\big(\mathbf{x}+w(\mathbf{x})\big) \Big\|^2 \, d\mathbf{x} $$

3 · The full energy

Three ingredients, written once

Let $I_1, I_2$ be the two frames. Write the brightness-constancy residuals for each direction:

  • forward: $\rho(\mathbf{x}) = I_{1x}u + I_{1y}v + I_t$, with $I_t = I_2 - I_1$;
  • backward: $\rho'(\mathbf{x}) = I_{2x}u' + I_{2y}v' + I'_t$, with $I'_t = I_1 - I_2$ (same construction, roles swapped).
$$ \begin{aligned} E(w,w') \;=\; &\int_\Psi \Big[\big(I_{1x}u+I_{1y}v+I_t\big)^2 + \alpha\big(\|\nabla u\|^2+\|\nabla v\|^2\big)\Big]d\mathbf{x} \\[4pt] +\;&\int_\Psi \Big[\big(I_{2x}u'+I_{2y}v'+I'_t\big)^2 + \alpha\big(\|\nabla u'\|^2+\|\nabla v'\|^2\big)\Big]d\mathbf{x} \\[4pt] +\;&\lambda \int_\Psi \Big\|\,w(\mathbf{x}) + w'\big(\mathbf{x}+w(\mathbf{x})\big)\Big\|^2 d\mathbf{x} \end{aligned} $$
What changed vs. the Horn–Schunck you learned Lines 1 and 2 are just plain HS, run twice — once in each direction. Only line 3 is new. The two unknown fields were completely independent before; line 3 is the only thing that couples them.

4 · Euler–Lagrange, done slowly

Step 0 — kill the nonlinearity

$w'(\mathbf{x}+w(\mathbf{x}))$ has the unknown $w$ inside the argument of $w'$. That is not quadratic, and Euler–Lagrange on it is ugly. Standard fix, the same lagged-warp trick used in coarse-to-fine HS: freeze the warp at the current iterate. During iteration $k$ define the constant field

$$ \hat w'(\mathbf{x}) \;:=\; w'^{(k)}\big(\mathbf{x} + w^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\big) \qquad\text{(bilinear interpolation — a known number at each pixel)} $$

Now the cycle term is simply $\lambda\|w(\mathbf{x}) + \hat w'(\mathbf{x})\|^2$ — a plain quadratic in the unknown. You re-warp once per outer iteration, and you alternate: update $w$ with $w'$ frozen, then update $w'$ with $w$ frozen.

Step 1 — differentiate

Using $\nabla^2 u$ for the Laplacian, $\partial E/\partial u = 0$ gives

$$ I_{1x}\big(I_{1x}u+I_{1y}v+I_t\big) \;-\; \alpha\,\nabla^2 u \;+\; \lambda\big(u+\hat u'\big) \;=\; 0 $$
$$ I_{1y}\big(I_{1x}u+I_{1y}v+I_t\big) \;-\; \alpha\,\nabla^2 v \;+\; \lambda\big(v+\hat v'\big) \;=\; 0 $$

Compare with the classic pair — the only new piece is $\lambda(u+\hat u')$. Now substitute the usual discrete Laplacian $\nabla^2 u \approx \bar u - u$ ($\bar u$ = weighted average of the 4/8 neighbours):

$$ \big(\alpha+\lambda+I_{1x}^2\big)u \;+\; I_{1x}I_{1y}\,v \;=\; \alpha\bar u \;-\; \lambda\hat u' \;-\; I_{1x}I_t $$
$$ I_{1x}I_{1y}\,u \;+\; \big(\alpha+\lambda+I_{1y}^2\big)v \;=\; \alpha\bar v \;-\; \lambda\hat v' \;-\; I_{1y}I_t $$

Step 2 — the trick that makes it look like ordinary HS

Two quadratic springs pulling on the same variable are one spring. Complete the square:

$$ \alpha\,(u-\bar u)^2 + \lambda\,\big(u-(-\hat u')\big)^2 \;=\; \underbrace{(\alpha+\lambda)}_{\textstyle \beta}\big(u-m_u\big)^2 + \text{const}, \qquad m_u=\frac{\alpha\bar u-\lambda\hat u'}{\alpha+\lambda} $$

So the cycle prior is literally a second smoothness term whose target is $-\hat w'$ instead of the neighbourhood average. Plug $\beta = \alpha+\lambda$ and $m=(m_u,m_v)$ into the standard HS solution of the $2\times2$ system and you are done:

$$ \boxed{\; \begin{aligned} m_u^{(k)} &= \frac{\alpha\,\bar u^{(k)}-\lambda\,\hat u'^{(k)}}{\alpha+\lambda}, \qquad m_v^{(k)} = \frac{\alpha\,\bar v^{(k)}-\lambda\,\hat v'^{(k)}}{\alpha+\lambda} \\[6pt] u^{(k+1)} &= m_u^{(k)} - I_{1x}\,\frac{I_{1x}m_u^{(k)}+I_{1y}m_v^{(k)}+I_t}{(\alpha+\lambda)+I_{1x}^2+I_{1y}^2} \\[6pt] v^{(k+1)} &= m_v^{(k)} - I_{1y}\,\frac{I_{1x}m_u^{(k)}+I_{1y}m_v^{(k)}+I_t}{(\alpha+\lambda)+I_{1x}^2+I_{1y}^2} \end{aligned}\;} $$

and the mirror image of this for $w'$, with $I_2$'s derivatives, with $\hat w(\mathbf{x}') = w(\mathbf{x}'+w'(\mathbf{x}'))$ in place of $\hat w'$.

5 · The one honest caveat: occlusions

At a pixel that is occluded in the second image, there is no $q$. The round trip is genuinely impossible, and forcing $c(\mathbf{x})=0$ there will corrupt an otherwise good flow field. Two standard fixes, either one earns the mark:

  • Robust penalty: replace $\|c\|^2$ by $\rho(\|c\|)$ with $\rho$ e.g. Charbonnier $\sqrt{\|c\|^2+\varepsilon^2}$ or the Lorentzian — large residuals stop dominating.
  • Spatially varying weight: use $\lambda(\mathbf{x}) = \lambda_0\,\exp\!\big(-\|c(\mathbf{x})\|^2/\sigma^2\big)$, or an explicit occlusion mask $o(\mathbf{x})\in\{0,1\}$ (from the forward–backward check itself, or from the flow divergence) and use $\lambda\,o(\mathbf{x})$. Then the cycle residual doubles as your occlusion detector — which is exactly how it is used in practice.

6 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

Full-marks version — one page

Set-up. Estimate both flow fields simultaneously: $w=(u,v)$ from $I_1$ to $I_2$, and $w'=(u',v')$ from $I_2$ to $I_1$. For $p=\mathbf{x}$ in $I_1$ the corresponding pixel is $q=\mathbf{x}+w(\mathbf{x})$, so cycle consistency reads $w'(\mathbf{x}+w(\mathbf{x})) = -w(\mathbf{x})$.

Energy. Horn–Schunck in both directions, plus a quadratic penalty on the cycle residual:

$$ \begin{aligned} E=\int\Big[(I_{1x}u+I_{1y}v+I_t)^2+\alpha(\|\nabla u\|^2+\|\nabla v\|^2)\Big] +\int\Big[(I_{2x}u'+I_{2y}v'+I'_t)^2+\alpha(\|\nabla u'\|^2+\|\nabla v'\|^2)\Big]\\ +\;\lambda\int\big\|w(\mathbf{x})+w'(\mathbf{x}+w(\mathbf{x}))\big\|^2 \end{aligned}$$

with $I_t=I_2-I_1$, $I'_t=I_1-I_2$.

Linearisation. The warp inside $w'$ makes the last term non-quadratic, so freeze it at the previous iterate: $\hat w'(\mathbf{x}) = w'^{(k)}(\mathbf{x}+w^{(k)}(\mathbf{x}))$ (bilinear interpolation), and alternate between updating $w$ and $w'$.

Euler–Lagrange. $\partial E/\partial u=0$ gives $I_{1x}(I_{1x}u+I_{1y}v+I_t)-\alpha\nabla^2u+\lambda(u+\hat u')=0$, and similarly for $v$. With $\nabla^2u\approx\bar u-u$:

$$(\alpha+\lambda+I_{1x}^2)u+I_{1x}I_{1y}v=\alpha\bar u-\lambda\hat u'-I_{1x}I_t,\qquad I_{1x}I_{1y}u+(\alpha+\lambda+I_{1y}^2)v=\alpha\bar v-\lambda\hat v'-I_{1y}I_t.$$

Update rule. Writing $\beta=\alpha+\lambda$ and $m_u=\dfrac{\alpha\bar u-\lambda\hat u'}{\beta}$, $m_v=\dfrac{\alpha\bar v-\lambda\hat v'}{\beta}$, the solution of that $2\times2$ system is the ordinary HS iteration with $\bar w$ replaced by $m$ and $\alpha$ by $\beta$:

$$u^{(k+1)}=m_u-I_{1x}\frac{I_{1x}m_u+I_{1y}m_v+I_t}{\beta+I_{1x}^2+I_{1y}^2},\qquad v^{(k+1)}=m_v-I_{1y}\frac{I_{1x}m_u+I_{1y}m_v+I_t}{\beta+I_{1x}^2+I_{1y}^2}$$

and symmetrically for $(u',v')$ using $I_2$'s derivatives and $\hat w(\mathbf{x}')=w(\mathbf{x}'+w'(\mathbf{x}'))$. Iterate: warp → update $w$ → update $w'$ → re-warp, inside the usual coarse-to-fine pyramid.

Interpretation and limits. The prior acts as a second smoothness term whose target is the negated backward flow instead of the spatial average. $\lambda=0$ recovers plain Horn–Schunck run twice; $\lambda\to\infty$ forces $w=-\hat w'$ exactly. Its value is largest in textureless regions, where the data term is degenerate (aperture problem) and the two directions would otherwise drift independently. At occluded pixels the constraint is invalid, so use a robust penalty $\rho(\|c\|)$ or a weight $\lambda(\mathbf{x})=\lambda_0e^{-\|c\|^2/\sigma^2}$ — the residual $c$ then doubles as an occlusion detector.

7 · Exam drill

Q. Why is it wrong to write the penalty as $\|w(\mathbf{x})+w'(\mathbf{x})\|^2$?

Because $w'$ is a field defined on image 2, indexed by pixels of image 2. $w'(\mathbf{x})$ is the backward flow of whatever object happens to sit at the same coordinates in the second frame — a completely different object once anything moves. The correct sample point is $q=\mathbf{x}+w(\mathbf{x})$. Writing $w'(\mathbf{x})$ silently assumes zero motion, i.e. it only holds when the answer is already trivial.

Q. The term is non-quadratic. Could you keep it exact instead of freezing the warp?

You can — differentiate through the argument, which brings in the Jacobian $\nabla w'$ evaluated at $q$: $\partial_u \|c\|^2 = 2\,c^{\!\top}\!\big(e_1 + \partial_x w'(q)\big)$. That is legitimate but gives a non-linear, non-symmetric system, and $\nabla w'$ is a noisy quantity early in the optimisation. The frozen warp (lagged fixed-point / “warping” scheme) is the standard course-level answer, and it is the same device used to handle large displacements in coarse-to-fine HS.

Q. Does the cycle term help where the image is well-textured?

Barely. Where $\nabla I$ is strong and well-conditioned, the data term already pins both directions to nearly correct values, so the residual is small and the term contributes almost nothing. The gain is concentrated exactly where HS is weakest: flat regions, single-orientation edges (aperture problem), and repeated texture where the data term has several equally good minima and the two directions can pick different ones.

Q. How would you initialise, and does the order of updates matter?

Initialise $w=w'=0$ at the coarsest pyramid level with $\lambda=0$ for a few iterations (otherwise you are enforcing consistency between two fields that are both meaningless), then switch the prior on and increase it over the outer iterations. The alternation is a block-coordinate descent: each block-update decreases $E$, so the order does not affect convergence to a stationary point, only the path taken. Warp once per outer iteration, not per inner Jacobi sweep.

Q. How is this the same idea as the forward–backward check you already know?

The classical forward–backward consistency check computes both flows independently and then throws away pixels where $\|c\|$ is large. That is the same quantity used as a post-hoc filter. Here it is promoted from a filter to a prior: instead of discarding bad pixels after the fact, you pay for inconsistency during the optimisation, so the estimate is pushed towards consistency rather than merely audited for it.

Q. Is the resulting energy convex? What are you actually guaranteed?

With the warp frozen, each block sub-problem is a positive-definite quadratic, so each update has a unique minimiser and $E$ decreases monotonically. The full problem, with the warp live, is not convex — the composition $w'(\mathbf{x}+w(\mathbf{x}))$ sees to that, just as brightness constancy is non-convex before linearisation. So you are guaranteed a local minimum reachable from your initialisation, which is why the coarse-to-fine pyramid matters.
