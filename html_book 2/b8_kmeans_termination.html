Companion page to Chapters 3 & 6 · Moed B 2022, question 8 · 12 points

Yes — and "the cost goes down" is only half the proof

Almost everyone writes "each step decreases $J$, and $J\ge0$, therefore it stops". That argument is not sufficient — a decreasing bounded sequence can decrease forever ($1,\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\dots$ never terminates). The proof needs a second ingredient: there are only finitely many possible assignments, and $J$ strictly decreases whenever the assignment changes, so no assignment can ever recur. Get both halves in and the question is worth full marks.

Moed B · 1/8/2022 · Question 8 · 12 points · CLUSTERING

Is the k-means algorithm guaranteed to terminate? explain your answer.

1 · The story

The kid, the classroom, and the reading corners

A classroom of children and $k$ reading corners. Every round, two things happen:

Round part 1 — everybody walks to the nearest corner. Nobody would walk further than they have to, so the total walking gets shorter (or stays the same).

Round part 2 — each corner moves to the middle of its group. Standing in the middle of your group is the position that makes the total walking shortest for that group. So again, the total walking gets shorter (or stays the same).

So total walking only ever goes down. Good. But does it stop? Careful — imagine a number that goes $10, 5, 2.5, 1.25, \dots$ It goes down forever and never stops. Going down is not enough!

Here is what saves us. What actually changes between rounds is only who sits with whom. And there is only a finite number of ways to split the class into $k$ groups — a big number, but finite, like the number of ways to deal a deck of cards.

Now put the two facts together. Every time the groups change, the total walking gets strictly smaller. So the class can never return to a grouping it has already had — that grouping had a bigger number, and the number only goes down. Since there are finitely many groupings and none can repeat, sooner or later the groups stop changing. And when the groups stop changing, the corners stop moving too. The algorithm halts. Guaranteed.

The two-part answer Part 1 (monotonicity). Both steps are exact minimisations of the same objective $J$ over complementary blocks of variables, so $J$ never increases — it is coordinate descent.
Part 2 (finiteness, the part people forget). $J$ depends on the continuous $\mu$ only through the discrete assignment; there are at most $k^n$ assignments; a change in assignment makes $J$ strictly decrease; therefore no assignment repeats and the algorithm stops after at most $k^n$ iterations.

2 · Setting it up properly

The objective and the algorithm

Given $\{\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_n\}\subset\mathbb{R}^d$ and $k$, k-means (Lloyd's algorithm) minimises the within-cluster sum of squares over both an assignment $c:\{1..n\}\to\{1..k\}$ and centres $\mu=(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_k)$:

$$ J(c,\mu) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mu_{c(i)}\big\|^2 \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{i:\,c(i)=j}\big\|\mathbf{x}_i-\mu_j\big\|^2 \tag{1} $$

It alternates two steps until nothing changes:

  • (A) Assignment — fix $\mu$, choose $c$:   $c^{(t+1)}(i)=\arg\min_j \|\mathbf{x}_i-\mu_j^{(t)}\|^2$
  • (U) Update — fix $c$, choose $\mu$:   $\mu_j^{(t+1)} = \dfrac{1}{|S_j|}\displaystyle\sum_{i\in S_j}\mathbf{x}_i$,   $S_j=\{i: c^{(t+1)}(i)=j\}$
Step A does not increase $J$

Trivially, term by term: for every $i$, $\|\mathbf{x}_i-\mu_{c^{(t+1)}(i)}\|^2 = \min_j\|\mathbf{x}_i-\mu_j\|^2 \le \|\mathbf{x}_i-\mu_{c^{(t)}(i)}\|^2$. Summing over $i$ gives $J(c^{(t+1)},\mu^{(t)}) \le J(c^{(t)},\mu^{(t)})$. The inequality is strict if any point actually switched (with a deterministic tie-breaking rule — see below).

Step U does not increase $J$ — and the mean is the reason

For fixed $c$ the objective separates over $j$, and each term is a strictly convex quadratic in $\mu_j$:

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial \mu_j}\sum_{i\in S_j}\|\mathbf{x}_i-\mu_j\|^2 = -2\sum_{i\in S_j}(\mathbf{x}_i-\mu_j) = 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \mu_j = \frac{1}{|S_j|}\sum_{i\in S_j}\mathbf{x}_i \tag{2} $$

with Hessian $2|S_j|\,\mathbb{I} \succ 0$, so the mean is the unique global minimiser of that block. Hence $J(c^{(t+1)},\mu^{(t+1)}) \le J(c^{(t+1)},\mu^{(t)})$. Equivalently, via the bias–variance style identity

$$ \sum_{i\in S_j}\|\mathbf{x}_i-\mu_j\|^2 \;=\; \sum_{i\in S_j}\|\mathbf{x}_i-\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{S_j}\|^2 \;+\; |S_j|\,\big\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{S_j}-\mu_j\big\|^2 $$

the second term is a non-negative penalty for not being at the mean, and it vanishes exactly when $\mu_j=\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{S_j}$. Chaining the two steps: $J$ is monotonically non-increasing and bounded below by $0$.

Why that is still not a termination proof Monotone + bounded gives you convergence of the sequence $J^{(t)}$ (a real analysis fact), not termination of the algorithm. The sequence $1,\frac12,\frac14,\dots$ is monotone and bounded and its algorithm never halts. You must show the process cannot take infinitely many distinct steps.
The finiteness argument — the actual proof
  1. After step U, $\mu$ is a deterministic function of $c$ (the vector of cluster means). So the algorithm's whole state is captured by the assignment $c$, a discrete object.
  2. The number of assignments is at most $k^n$ — finite. (Partitions into non-empty labelled clusters: $k!\,S(n,k)$, still finite.)
  3. Define $\Phi(c) = J\big(c,\mu(c)\big)$, the cost after re-centring. If one full iteration changes the assignment from $c$ to $c'\ne c$, then $\Phi(c') < \Phi(c)$ strictly: at least one point moved to a strictly closer centre (strictly, by the tie-break rule), so step A strictly decreased $J$, and step U cannot increase it.
  4. Therefore $\Phi$ is strictly decreasing along the sequence of visited assignments, so no assignment can be visited twice — a repeat would require $\Phi(c)<\Phi(c)$.
  5. A sequence of distinct elements from a finite set is finite. Hence after at most $k^n$ iterations the assignment stops changing; then the means do not change either, so the algorithm is at a fixed point and terminates. $\blacksquare$

4 · The fine print you should mention

Caveats that turn a correct answer into a complete one
PointDetail
Ties must be broken deterministically If $\mathbf{x}_i$ is exactly equidistant from two centres and you re-toss a coin each time, the assignment can change without $J$ changing — and then it can oscillate forever. The standard rule ("keep the current assignment on a tie", or "lowest index wins") restores the implication assignment changed ⇒ $J$ strictly decreased, which is exactly what the proof needs.
Empty clusters $\mu_j$ is undefined when $S_j=\emptyset$. Any deterministic repair keeps the proof: drop the cluster ($k$ decreases), or re-seed it at the point currently furthest from its centre — the latter strictly decreases $J$, so monotonicity survives. But if you re-seed it randomly, $J$ can increase and the finiteness argument collapses. Same trap as the demo's checkbox.
Terminates ≠ optimal It stops at a fixed point of Lloyd's map — an assignment that is stable under "reassign all, then re-centre". That is a local minimum with respect to those block moves, and it may be arbitrarily worse than the global optimum. Finding the global optimum is NP-hard (even for $k=2$ in general dimension, and for general $k$ in the plane). Hence random restarts, and k-means++ seeding, which gives an $O(\log k)$ expected approximation guarantee before Lloyd even starts.
How long is "eventually"? The bound $k^n$ is astronomically loose but it is what the proof gives. The true worst case is superpolynomial: Arthur & Vassilvitskii construct instances needing $2^{\Omega(\sqrt n)}$ iterations, even in the plane. Yet the smoothed complexity is polynomial, and in practice convergence takes tens of iterations — which is why nobody worries about it.
Which distances still work? The proof used only one property: the update step must return the exact minimiser of the within-cluster cost for the chosen dissimilarity. The arithmetic mean is that minimiser for squared Euclidean distance — and, more generally, for every Bregman divergence (Banerjee et al.), so k-means terminates for KL divergence, Itakura–Saito, etc. It does not work if you pair the mean with $L_1$: there the minimiser is the median, so using the mean can increase $J$ and termination is no longer guaranteed. Use the median ($k$-medians) or a medoid ($k$-medoids / PAM) and the same finiteness proof goes through again.

5 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

One page, as the exam asks

Yes — k-means (Lloyd's algorithm) always terminates after a finite number of iterations (assuming a deterministic tie-breaking rule and a deterministic rule for empty clusters). The proof has two parts, and the second is the essential one.

Part 1 — the objective never increases. The algorithm is block coordinate descent on

$$ J(c,\mu)=\sum_{i=1}^n \|\mathbf{x}_i-\mu_{c(i)}\|^2 . $$

Assignment step: for fixed $\mu$, $c(i)=\arg\min_j\|\mathbf{x}_i-\mu_j\|^2$ minimises each term independently, so $J$ cannot increase. Update step: for fixed $c$, $J$ separates over clusters and each part is a strictly convex quadratic in $\mu_j$ whose unique minimiser is the mean ($\nabla=-2\sum_{i\in S_j}(\mathbf{x}_i-\mu_j)=0 \Rightarrow \mu_j=\frac{1}{|S_j|}\sum_{i\in S_j}\mathbf{x}_i$, Hessian $2|S_j|\mathbb{I}\succ0$), so again $J$ cannot increase. Hence $J^{(t)}$ is monotonically non-increasing and bounded below by $0$.

Part 2 — finiteness (why part 1 alone is insufficient). A monotone bounded sequence only converges; it need not terminate (e.g. $2^{-t}$). But after the update step $\mu$ is a deterministic function of the assignment $c$, so the algorithm's state is the discrete object $c$, and there are at most $k^n$ possible assignments — a finite set. Moreover, if one full iteration changes the assignment then at least one point moved to a strictly closer centre (strict, by the tie-break rule), so $J$ strictly decreased. Since $J$ never increases, a strictly decreasing value means no assignment can ever recur. A sequence of distinct elements of a finite set must be finite, so after at most $k^n$ iterations the assignment stops changing; then the means do not change either, the algorithm is at a fixed point, and it halts. $\blacksquare$

What terminating does not mean. It converges to a local minimum (a fixed point of Lloyd's map), not the global one; the global problem is NP-hard, so in practice one uses multiple random restarts or k-means++ seeding ($O(\log k)$ expected approximation). The $k^n$ bound is very loose, but the true worst case is still superpolynomial — $2^{\Omega(\sqrt n)}$ iterations exist even in the plane — while the smoothed complexity is polynomial and practice needs tens of iterations.

What the proof depends on. Only that the update step returns the exact minimiser of the within-cluster cost. That holds for squared Euclidean distance and, more generally, for any Bregman divergence. It fails if you mix a distance with the wrong centroid — e.g. $L_1$ distance with the arithmetic mean — in which case $J$ can increase, assignments can cycle, and termination is no longer guaranteed. Likewise, re-seeding an empty cluster at random (rather than deterministically, e.g. at the point furthest from its centre) breaks monotonicity and the guarantee with it.

6 · Exam drill

Q. Give the shortest complete answer you could write under time pressure.

"Yes. Both steps exactly minimise $J$ over one block of variables, so $J$ never increases. After the update, $\mu$ is determined by the assignment, so the state is the assignment; there are at most $k^n$ of them. If the assignment changes, $J$ strictly decreases (with deterministic tie-breaking), so no assignment repeats. Finitely many states, none repeated ⇒ it halts, in at most $k^n$ iterations. It reaches a local, not global, minimum." That is five sentences and it contains every load-bearing idea.

Q. Is "$J$ decreases and is bounded below" wrong, or just incomplete?

Incomplete, and specifically it proves the wrong statement. It proves the sequence of costs converges, which would also be true of an algorithm that runs forever making ever-smaller improvements. Termination is a statement about the algorithm's state, not its cost, and the state is discrete. The reason the two get conflated is that in k-means the cost happens to certify the state (a strict decrease certifies non-repetition), but you have to say that step out loud — it is exactly the step being tested.

Q. Could the centres keep moving forever while the assignment stays fixed?

No. Once the assignment is fixed at $c$, the update step sets $\mu_j$ to the mean of $S_j$ — a single formula, evaluated once, giving one specific value. Applying it again gives the same value (the mean of a fixed set is idempotent). So the centres reach their final positions in one update. That is why the whole dynamic lives in the discrete assignment and why the state space is finite. It is also why a good implementation's stopping test is "the assignment did not change" rather than "$\|\mu^{(t+1)}-\mu^{(t)}\|<\varepsilon$" — the former is exact, the latter is a heuristic that can stop early.

Q. Where exactly does the tie-breaking rule enter the proof?

In the claim "assignment changed ⇒ $J$ strictly decreased". Suppose $\mathbf{x}_i$ is exactly equidistant from $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$. Moving it from cluster 1 to cluster 2 changes the assignment but changes $J$ by exactly zero. Now the strict-decrease argument fails and the algorithm could shuttle that point back and forth forever between two assignments of equal cost. Rules that fix it: "on a tie keep the current assignment" (the usual choice — then a change implies a strict improvement) or "on a tie choose the lowest cluster index" (then the map is a deterministic function of $\mu$, so the whole iteration is a deterministic map on a finite set, and combined with non-increasing $J$ it must reach a fixed point rather than a longer cycle). In floating point exact ties are rare, which is why this subtlety is usually invisible — but it is the kind of detail an exam question about guarantees is fishing for.

Q. Does k-means terminate if you use the $L_1$ distance?

Only if you also change the centroid. The proof's single requirement is that step U returns the minimiser of $\sum_{i\in S_j} d(\mathbf{x}_i,\mu_j)$. For $L_1$ that minimiser is the coordinate-wise median, not the mean. Pair $L_1$ with the median ($k$-medians) and every line of the proof goes through unchanged. Pair $L_1$ with the mean and step U can increase $J$; monotonicity is lost, an assignment can recur, and you can cycle. The general statement is Banerjee et al.: the mean is the minimiser precisely for Bregman divergences, and k-means terminates for exactly that family.

Q. How is this used in the course?

Chapter 6, layered motion representation: estimate a local affine/translational motion model per block, then cluster those motion parameters with k-means to discover how many independently-moving layers the video contains and which pixels belong to each. The clustering is over motion vectors, not colours, but the algorithm and every property discussed here apply unchanged — including the local-minimum problem, which is why layer extraction is sensitive to initialisation and typically warm-started from the previous frame's layers. It also appears in the clustering material alongside the distortion/elbow criterion for choosing $k$.

Q. Is the $k^n$ bound tight?

Far from it as a practical estimate, but it is the right kind of bound for this proof, which only needs some finite number. The genuinely tight picture: the worst case is superpolynomial — Arthur & Vassilvitskii exhibit planar instances forcing $2^{\Omega(\sqrt n)}$ iterations, so no polynomial bound exists; the best known general upper bound is $O(n^{kd})$ (the number of distinct Voronoi partitions of $n$ points by $k$ centres in $\mathbb{R}^d$), which is much better than $k^n$; and the smoothed complexity, under tiny random perturbations of the input, is polynomial — which explains the gap between the terrifying worst case and the observed handful of iterations.
