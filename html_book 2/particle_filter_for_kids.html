The Particle Filter, explained like you are five

No formulas in the first half. Just a story, a game, and three demos you can press. The math shows up at the end — by then it will look like a description of the game you already played.

1 · The story

Lost puppy in a dark park

Your puppy ran into a big dark park. You cannot see it. You have 100 friends and each friend stands somewhere in the park and shouts "maybe the puppy is HERE!"

Each friend is a guess. That is a particle.

Every second you hear a faint bark. The bark is not exact — echoes make it fuzzy — but a friend standing near where the bark came from is probably right. The bark is the measurement.

So every second you do three things:

  • Score — friends close to the bark get a big star. Far friends get a tiny star.
  • Copy — friends with big stars get cloned; friends with tiny stars go home.
  • Wander — everyone takes a step in the direction they think the puppy is running, plus a random little stumble (nobody is sure).

Do that every second and the crowd of friends squeezes together right on top of the puppy — and it keeps following it. That crowd IS the particle filter. Nothing else.

The one sentence to remember A particle filter answers "where is it?" not with one number, but with a crowd of guesses, each holding a score. The crowd is the answer.

The #1 confusion — x vs z

$x$ is what you WANT and never see. Position, size, and speed of the car. Speed is in $x$ even though no sensor ever measures speed directly — the filter figures it out.

$z$ is what you GET and never trust. The camera frame. You only use $z$ to hand out scores. A measurement never becomes the state; it only judges the guesses.

2 · Play it once, by hand

How to use this demo

Press Next stage over and over. One click = one stage of the algorithm, so you can see exactly what changes and what does not. The dark box on top is the "park", seen from above and squashed into a line (1-D, to keep it simple).

Watch how the crowd starts spread out and knows nothing — then locks on after 3–4 full rounds. That "locking on" moment is the whole magic.

  1. Start. N friends stand at random spots. Every one has the same star (weight $=1/N$) because you know nothing yet. Total ignorance = flat distribution.
  2. Resample (select). Build a new crowd by picking from the old crowd in proportion to the stars. A friend with a fat star gets copied 5 times; a friend with a crumb star vanishes. Crowd size stays N. Nobody moves in this stage — only the identities change.
  3. Predict (drift + diffusion). Every friend walks by their own guessed velocity, then adds a random stumble. This is the motion model $P(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$. The image is not used here at all.
  4. Measure (weight). NOW look at the frame. Score each friend by how well the picture at their spot matches what you are tracking: $\pi^{(n)} = P(z_t \mid x_t = S^{(n)})$. Then normalise so all stars sum to 1.
  5. Estimate. Report the weighted average (smooth) or the biggest-star particle (sharp but jumpy). Then go back to step 2 for the next frame.
Why resample BEFORE predict? Because you want to spend your N guesses where the probability already is. Copying good guesses and then stumbling them creates a fresh dense cloud around the good area. If you never resampled, after 20 frames one particle would hold 99.99% of the weight and the other 99 would be useless — this is called degeneracy.

3 · The roulette wheel (the part exams love)

Why the weird cumulative array C

Step 1 of Condensation says: "generate $r$ uniform in $[0,1]$, find the smallest $j$ with $C^{(j)} > r$." That sounds cryptic. It is just a roulette wheel made of a ruler.

Lay all the weights end to end on a ruler from 0 to 1. A particle with weight 0.4 owns a 40 cm strip of a 1 m ruler. Throw a dart at a random spot $r$ on the ruler — whoever owns that spot gets copied. Throw N darts, get N new particles. Heavy particles own long strips, so they get hit often. That is all $C$ is: the ruler marks, i.e. the running total of the weights.

4 · Now a real car

Same game, 2-D, with the two things that break Kalman

Here the state is 4 numbers per particle: $x = (\text{px}, \text{py}, v_x, v_y)$ — exactly your ex3 state minus the box size. Two switches make this interesting:

  • Tunnel — while the car is in the grey band, no measurement arrives. All weights stay equal, so the filter runs on the motion model alone and the cloud spreads out (uncertainty grows). When the car exits, the cloud snaps back. This is prediction earning its keep.
  • Twin car — a second identical car that keeps drifting across the real one. When they are close, 35% of measurements come from the wrong car and the cloud splits into two blobs — watch ESS collapse and the white estimate wobble between them. A Kalman filter cannot represent that: one Gaussian has one bump, so it would sit uselessly in the empty space between the two cars. This is the reason particle filters exist.
  • And the honest limitation: if the decoy is far away, no particle is sitting there, so its likelihood is numerically zero and the filter simply ignores it. A particle filter can only represent a hypothesis it has particles for — this is why occasionally scattering a few random particles ("re-initialisation") is standard practice in real trackers.

5 · Only now, the math

Each symbol is a thing you already saw

The recursion in the lecture notes:

$$ \underbrace{P(x_t \mid z_{1:t})}_{\text{belief now}} \;=\; C \cdot \underbrace{P(z_t \mid x_t)}_{\text{the star / stage 3}} \cdot \underbrace{P(x_t \mid z_{1:t-1})}_{\text{after the walk / stage 2}} $$
$$ \underbrace{P(x_t \mid z_{1:t-1})}_{\text{after the walk}} \;=\; \int_{x_{t-1}} \underbrace{P(x_t \mid x_{t-1})}_{\text{step + stumble}} \; \underbrace{P(x_{t-1} \mid z_{1:t-1})}_{\text{yesterday's crowd}} \, dx_{t-1} $$

That integral is impossible to compute for a real image. So the particle filter never computes it — it samples it. Sampling N times from $P(x_{t-1}\mid z_{1:t-1})$ (= stage 1, the roulette) and pushing each sample through $P(x_t\mid x_{t-1})$ (= stage 2, the walk) gives you N samples from the integral for free. That single trick is the whole method.

Two assumptions that make it work

  • Markov: $P(x_t \mid x_{1:t-1}) = P(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$ — the car's next position depends only on where it is now, not on its whole life story. Lets you throw away history.
  • Conditional independence: $P(z_{1:t}\mid x_{1:t}) = \prod_\tau P(z_\tau \mid x_\tau)$ — given where the car actually is, this frame's pixels tell you nothing about last frame's pixels. Lets the likelihood factor into one term per frame.

Drop either one and the recursion collapses, and you are back to storing the entire video.

Condensation, line by line (as in the notes) — with the plain meaning

Input: $\{S_{t-1}^{(n)}, \Pi_{t-1}^{(n)}, C_{t-1}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^N$  — last frame's crowd, their stars, and the ruler marks.

1) Select: draw $r \sim U[0,1]$; find smallest $j$ with $C_{t-1}^{(j)} > r$; set $S_t^{'(n)} = S_{t-1}^{(j)}$.
→ throw a dart at the ruler, copy whoever owns that spot. Repeat N times.

2) Predict: $S_t^{(n)} = A\,S_t^{'(n)} + B\,w_t^{(n)}$.
→ $A$ = the deterministic step (drift, e.g. add velocity to position), $B w$ = the random stumble (diffusion). Nonlinear $A$ is perfectly fine here — that is the freedom Kalman does not have.

3) Measure: $\Pi_t^{(n)} = P(Z_t \mid x_t = S_t^{(n)})$, then normalise so $\sum_n \Pi_t^{(n)} = 1$, and rebuild $C_t^{(0)}=0$, $C_t^{(n)} = C_t^{(n-1)} + \Pi_t^{(n)}$.
→ hand out stars from the image (e.g. $e^{-\text{SSD}}$, or Bhattacharyya on colour histograms as in ex3), then rebuild the ruler for next frame.

Return $\sum_n \Pi_t^{(n)} f(S_t^{(n)})$.
→ the weighted average. With $f = $ identity you get the average box.

6 · Condensation with real numbers

The one thing that unlocks it $C$ is not new information. $C$ is just $\Pi$ added up. If you know the weights you can rebuild $C$ in one line of code. It exists only to make step 1 fast. Read the rest of this section with that in mind and Condensation stops being mysterious.

The three arrays

Input and output are the same three lists, each of length N. All three describe the same N particles — array index $n$ is one particle in all of them.

ArrayTypeWhat it holds
$S^{(n)}$vector (6 numbers in ex3)particle n's guess of the state — where / how big / how fast
$\Pi^{(n)}$scalar in [0,1]particle n's weight. All N of them sum to 1
$C^{(n)}$scalar in [0,1]running total of the weights: $C^{(n)} = \Pi^{(1)} + \cdots + \Pi^{(n)}$

Input = last frame's crowd. Output = this frame's crowd. Same three arrays, one timestep later. That is the only reason $C$ appears on both sides — you rebuild it at the end of step 3 so that step 1 of the next frame can use it.

What $C$ actually is

A ruler of length 1, cut into strips

Give each particle a strip of the ruler whose length equals its weight:

Π  = [0.05, 0.35, 0.10, 0.40, 0.10]

0     0.05            0.40  0.50              0.90    1.00
|--P1--|------P2-------|-P3--|-------P4--------|--P5--|
       ↑               ↑     ↑                 ↑      ↑
     C¹=0.05        C²=0.40 C³=0.50         C⁴=0.90  C⁵=1.00

$C$ is the list of cut marks. Nothing else. Always $C^{(0)}=0$ and $C^{(N)}=1$. Heavy particle = long strip. Light particle = sliver.

Why a random number in $[0,1]$

You want to draw a particle with probability equal to its weight. Your computer only gives you uniform(0,1). So you convert one into the other: throw a dart at a random spot $r$ on the ruler, and whoever owns that spot wins. The probability the dart lands in particle j's strip is the length of the strip, which is $\Pi^{(j)}$ — exactly what you asked for.

The exam-proof version, one line:

$$ P(\text{pick } j) \;=\; P\big(C^{(j-1)} \le r < C^{(j)}\big) \;=\; C^{(j)} - C^{(j-1)} \;=\; \Pi^{(j)} $$

First equality: "smallest $j$ with $C^{(j)} > r$" means precisely that $r$ fell inside strip $j$. Second equality: for $r \sim U[0,1]$, the probability of landing in an interval is that interval's length.

And the range is $[0,1]$ because the weights were normalised to sum to 1 — so the ruler has length exactly 1. Skip the normalisation and the ruler ends somewhere else, and drawing $r \in [0,1]$ breaks.

This trick has a name: inverse-CDF sampling (inverse transform sampling). $C$ is the CDF of the discrete particle distribution. The notes calling it a "slack variable" is just unfortunate wording.

The critical follow-up

After resampling, all weights reset to $1/N$. The information did not vanish — it moved out of the weights and into how many copies each guess has. Before: one particle at 70 carrying weight 0.40. After: three particles at 70 carrying 0.20 each. Same probability mass, different storage.

Three identical copies at 70 would be useless, which is why step 2 immediately scatters them with diffusion noise. Copy, then scatter — they become three slightly different hypotheses around 70.

Two things exams poke at

Why is $C$ monotonically increasing? Because weights are $\ge 0$, so each step only ever adds. That monotonicity is what makes "find the smallest $j$ with $C^{(j)} > r$" well-defined, and it lets you do the search by binary search in $O(\log N)$ instead of a linear scan. (Your ex3 uses np.argmax(cdf >= r) — correct, but the $O(N)$ version.)

Why random darts instead of just handing P4 exactly $0.4N$ copies? Randomness keeps the sample unbiased and works for weights that are not neat multiples of $1/N$. A lower-variance middle ground exists and is worth naming: systematic resampling — draw a single $r_0 \sim U[0, 1/N]$ and then use the evenly spaced darts $r_i = r_0 + i/N$. Same expected counts, much less noise.

7 · Exam drill

Q. In one sentence, what does a particle filter represent that a Kalman filter cannot?

A non-Gaussian, possibly multi-modal posterior — several competing hypotheses at once — and it allows an arbitrary nonlinear motion model and an arbitrary likelihood. Kalman stores one mean and one covariance, so it has exactly one bump and needs linear dynamics with Gaussian noise.

Q. Why do we resample at all? What goes wrong without it?

Degeneracy. Without resampling, weights keep multiplying frame after frame; after a few dozen frames one particle carries essentially all the weight and the rest carry ~0. You are then paying for N particles but effectively running with 1. Resampling kills the near-zero particles and clones the strong ones, keeping all N guesses useful.

Q. And what is the danger of resampling too aggressively?

Sample impoverishment: after enough resampling every particle is a copy of the same ancestor, so the cloud has zero spread and cannot recover if the target manoeuvres. The standard fix is the diffusion noise added in the predict stage every single frame — it re-injects diversity. (Try demo 3 with diffusion = 1.)

Q. Weighted mean or max-weight particle for the final answer?

Weighted mean is smooth and stable but can land on an empty spot — with two candidate cars it reports the midpoint, where there is no car. Max-weight always lands on a real hypothesis but jitters between competing peaks frame to frame. Choose by whether your posterior is uni- or multi-modal.

Q. What exactly is $C^{(n)}$ and why is it monotonically increasing?

$C^{(n)} = \sum_{k \le n} \Pi^{(k)}$, the cumulative sum of the normalised weights, so $C^{(0)}=0$ and $C^{(N)}=1$. It is non-decreasing because weights are non-negative. That monotonicity is what allows the "find the smallest $j$ with $C^{(j)} > r$" search to be a valid inverse-CDF sample — and lets you do it by binary search in $O(\log N)$.

Q. During an occlusion no measurement is available. What should the filter do?

Skip the re-weighting (or give every particle equal weight) and run predict only. The cloud keeps moving with the motion model and spreads out, since diffusion is added but nothing pulls it back in. Uncertainty grows — which is correct — and the filter can re-lock when the target reappears, as long as the cloud has stayed wide enough to cover the true position.

Q. Complexity — why not just use 100,000 particles?

Cost is $O(N \cdot M)$ per frame where $M$ is the cost of one likelihood evaluation — and $M$ is expensive in vision (crop a patch, build a 16×16×16 histogram, compare). Kalman is $O(n^3)$ in the state dimension only. Also, particles needed grows roughly exponentially with state dimension (curse of dimensionality), which is why practical trackers keep the state to 4–6 numbers, exactly as ex3 does.

Q. In ex3, why is the likelihood $e^{20 \cdot BC}$ and not just $BC$?

$BC = \sum \sqrt{p q}$ is the Bhattacharyya coefficient — 1 for identical histograms, 0 for disjoint ones. Raw $BC$ values for near-matches are all bunched close together, so the weights would be almost uniform and the filter would barely discriminate. The exponential with a large gain sharpens the contrast: a slightly better match gets a disproportionately larger weight, so resampling actually concentrates the cloud.
