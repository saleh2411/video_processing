A recipe that works on any of them

  1. Read the ALL-CAPS header. He has told you the chapter. Write down that chapter's core equation before anything else.
  2. Name the assumption that is being violated or extended. Nearly every question is "the standard assumption is wrong here" or "here is extra information the standard method ignores."
  3. Change the smallest possible thing. Keep the data term, weight the prior. Keep the loss, change the input. Answers that rebuild the algorithm from scratch lose marks.
  4. Write the modified equation. "Show all work" means an equation, not a paragraph.
  5. Close with the degenerate case. One sentence on what happens at the extreme parameter value, or when the new input is absent. This is cheap and it reads as understanding.

Optic Flow

13 points

Figure 1: (a) Input image $I$. (b) The corresponding confidence map $C$, where dark regions mark pixels whose brightness the network considers unreliable.

Consider Figure 1. A neural network takes a grayscale image $I$ and outputs a confidence map $C(x,y) \in [0,1]$, where $C$ is close to $1$ at pixels whose brightness is reliable and close to $0$ at pixels corrupted by specular highlights, saturation, or shadow boundaries — places where the brightness constancy assumption is known to break.

Modify the Horn–Schunck algorithm to take as input two grayscale images $I_1, I_2$ and their confidence maps $C_1, C_2$, and produce the optic flow between them.

What does your algorithm produce in a region where $C \equiv 0$ everywhere?

Ch. 2
Show solution
How to think about it

Base Horn–Schunck minimises $\iint (I_xu + I_yv + I_t)^2 + \alpha(|\nabla u|^2 + |\nabla v|^2)\,dx\,dy$. The first term is the data term — it is exactly the brightness-constancy constraint, and the confidence map is a direct statement about where that constraint is trustworthy. So this question is the mirror image of the semantic-segmentation one: there the extra input told you about the smoothness term, here it tells you about the data term. Do not touch the smoothness.

Solution

Weight the data term pointwise by the confidence:

$$ \min_{u,v} \iint C(x,y)\,(I_xu + I_yv + I_t)^2 \;+\; \alpha\bigl(|\nabla u|^2 + |\nabla v|^2\bigr)\,dx\,dy $$

Take $C = \min(C_1(x,y),\, C_2(x+u,y+v))$ so a pixel counts only if it is reliable in both frames (evaluate the second at the current warp inside the coarse-to-fine loop; $C_1$ alone is acceptable if you say so).

Redoing the Euler–Lagrange derivation, $C$ simply rides along on the data terms:

$$ C\,I_x(I_xu + I_yv + I_t) - \alpha \Delta u = 0, \qquad C\,I_y(I_xu + I_yv + I_t) - \alpha \Delta v = 0 $$

giving the iteration

$$ u^{k+1} = \bar u^{k} - \frac{C\,I_x(I_x\bar u^k + I_y\bar v^k + I_t)}{\alpha + C(I_x^2+I_y^2)} $$

and symmetrically for $v$. Note this is exactly standard HS with $C=1$, which is the check that you have not broken anything.

The closing sentence

Where $C \equiv 0$ the data term vanishes and the update collapses to $u^{k+1} = \bar u^k$ — pure diffusion. The flow there is filled in entirely by the smoothness term from the surrounding reliable pixels, which is exactly the desired behaviour: an unreliable pixel contributes no evidence but still receives an interpolated answer. This is the same fill-in mechanism that handles textureless regions in standard HS.

Trap. Weighting the smoothness term instead. That would say "motion is allowed to jump at specular highlights", which is nonsense — a highlight is not an object boundary. Also avoid setting $C$ to a hard $0/1$ mask and deleting those pixels: keeping the soft weight means a pixel with $C = 0.3$ still contributes a little, and it keeps the system well-conditioned.

Tracking

12 points

You implement a particle filter with $N = 1000$ particles to track a person walking across a room. It works for the first few frames. Then you notice that after re-weighting, one particle holds almost all of the weight and the other 999 hold essentially zero; a few frames later the tracker has lost the person entirely and never recovers.

Explain how this can happen, and propose two different fixes.

Ch. 4
Show solution
How to think about it

This is the classic degeneracy / sample impoverishment failure of Condensation, and the question is really asking whether you understand what the weights and the resampling step are for.

Why it happens

Weights are multiplied by the likelihood every frame, $w_t^{(i)} \propto w_{t-1}^{(i)}\, p(z_t \mid x_t^{(i)})$. The variance of the importance weights can only grow over time, so generically one particle's weight tends to 1 and the rest to 0. Two consequences:

  • Degeneracy: you are spending 1000 particles' worth of computation to represent a single hypothesis — the effective sample size $N_{\text{eff}} = 1/\sum_i (w^{(i)})^2$ collapses to ~1.
  • Impoverishment: resampling then makes 1000 identical copies of that one particle. Since the copies are identical, the posterior has collapsed to a point. If that particle was on the wrong hypothesis — a distractor, a moment of occlusion — the true state is no longer represented anywhere in the particle set, and no amount of future evidence can bring it back. Hence "never recovers".
Fixes (any two)
  • Resample adaptively. Monitor $N_{\text{eff}}$ and resample only when it drops below a threshold (say $N/2$), instead of every frame. Resampling is what destroys diversity, so do it only when it buys something.
  • Add process noise / jitter after resampling. Perturb each duplicated particle by the motion-model noise so 1000 copies become 1000 nearby-but-distinct hypotheses. Equivalently, widen $p(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$ — the predict step is the only stage that creates diversity.
  • Flatten the likelihood. An over-peaked $p(z\mid x)$ (too small an observation variance) is usually the real culprit: it makes one particle win outright. Increasing the assumed measurement noise spreads the weights.
  • Increase $N$, or inject a fraction of particles drawn uniformly / from a detector each frame so the tracker can re-acquire after a loss.
Trap. Answering only "use more particles". It delays the collapse but does not prevent it — the weight variance still grows monotonically. You must name the mechanism (weight variance growth → resampling → identical copies) to get the marks.

Dynamic Texture

13 points

A sensor bolted to a pedestrian bridge records a vibration feature vector $y_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ once per second. You are given a 10-minute recording of the bridge under normal conditions.

Suggest an algorithm that takes this recording as input and generates as output an arbitrarily long, realistic continuation of the vibration signal — one that a domain expert would not be able to distinguish from a genuine recording.

Ch. 5
Show solution
How to think about it

Costume change. "Take a short recording of something that varies stochastically but stationarily, and synthesize more of it forever" is exactly the dynamic texture problem of Ch.5 — the same question as smoke, fire, or water, with $y_t$ a sensor vector instead of a vectorised video frame. Nothing about Ch.5 requires $y_t$ to be pixels.

Solution — model

Model the signal as a linear dynamical system (the ARMA / state-space model of Ch.5):

$$ x_{t+1} = A\,x_t + v_t, \qquad y_t = C\,x_t + w_t $$

with hidden state $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $n \ll d$, and $v_t, w_t$ zero-mean Gaussian noise. $A$ carries the dynamics (how the bridge evolves), $C$ the appearance (how state maps to the measured features).

Solution — identification by SVD
  1. Stack the 600 observations into $Y = [\,y_1\ y_2\ \cdots\ y_T\,] \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times T}$ and subtract the mean.
  2. Take the SVD $Y = U\Sigma V^{\!\top}$ and truncate to the top $n$ singular values. Set $\hat C = U_n$ and the state sequence $\hat X = \Sigma_n V_n^{\!\top}$. ($n$ from the singular-value knee.)
  3. Estimate the dynamics by least squares on the recovered states: $\hat A = X_{2:T}\,X_{1:T-1}^{+}$ (pseudo-inverse).
  4. Estimate the driving-noise covariance from the residual $\hat v_t = x_{t+1} - \hat A x_t$.
Solution — synthesis

Seed $x_0$ from the recording (e.g. its last state), then iterate $x_{t+1} = \hat A x_t + v_t$ with $v_t$ sampled from the fitted noise, and emit $y_t = \hat C x_t + \text{mean}$. Run for as many steps as you like — the model is generative, so output length is unbounded and never repeats verbatim.

The closing sentence

Stability matters: if any eigenvalue of $\hat A$ has modulus $> 1$ the synthesis diverges, and if all are much less than 1 it decays to the mean and goes dead. Check $\rho(\hat A)$ and rescale the eigenvalues to just inside the unit circle if needed.

Trap. Proposing to loop or shuffle chunks of the original recording. That is not synthesis — it repeats verbatim, has visible seams, and cannot generate anything unseen. The marks are for the generative model. (This is the same answer as the Word2Vec-generates-text question from Moed B, with a different costume.)

Video Magnification

12 points

Figure 2: Vertical displacement of a point on the baby's chest, measured over time. The dashed band marks $\pm 1$ pixel. The breathing signal never leaves a small fraction of one pixel.

Consider Figure 2. A camera on a tripod films a sleeping baby. The baby's breathing moves the chest by well under one pixel per frame — invisible to the eye when you watch the video.

Suggest a way to produce a video in which the breathing motion is clearly visible. State which frequencies you would keep and why.

What happens to your output if, halfway through the recording, the baby throws an arm across the frame?

Ch. 7
Show solution
How to think about it

Sub-pixel motion rules out anything Lagrangian: you cannot track what you cannot localise, and optic flow at this amplitude is pure noise. This is the case Ch.7's Eulerian approach was built for — never track anything, just watch each pixel's intensity over time and amplify the part of that signal you care about.

Solution
  1. Spatial decomposition. Build a multi-scale pyramid of each frame. Work at a coarse scale — small motions are best represented there, and it suppresses sensor noise.
  2. Temporal bandpass per pixel. Treat each pixel (each pyramid coefficient) as a 1-D time series and bandpass it. A sleeping infant breathes at roughly 30–60 breaths/min, so keep ~0.5–1.0 Hz. This is the crucial step: it isolates breathing and rejects both DC (the static scene) and higher frequencies (noise, flicker).
  3. Amplify. Multiply the bandpassed signal by $\alpha$ and add it back to the original: $\hat I(x,t) = I(x,t) + \alpha\,B(x,t)$.
  4. Collapse the pyramid to get the output video.
Why amplifying intensity magnifies motion

First-order Taylor: a small translation $\delta(t)$ of a signal $f$ gives $I(x,t) = f\bigl(x+\delta(t)\bigr) \approx f(x) + \delta(t)\,\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}$. The time-varying part is exactly $\delta(t)\,\partial f/\partial x$, so multiplying it by $\alpha$ and adding back yields $f(x) + (1+\alpha)\delta(t)\,\partial f/\partial x \approx f\bigl(x + (1+\alpha)\delta(t)\bigr)$ — a motion magnified by $(1+\alpha)$. Amplifying a temporal intensity change is amplifying motion, provided the motion is small.

The arm

It breaks the method, and saying so earns marks. The Taylor approximation is only valid for motion small relative to the spatial wavelength; a large fast arm movement violates it, so the amplification is no longer a translation and you get severe artefacts — ringing, ghosting and blow-out around the arm's edges. It is also broadband in time, so it leaks into the 0.5–1 Hz band rather than being filtered out. Mitigations: cap $\alpha$ (there is a known bound relating $\alpha$ to spatial wavelength), attenuate $\alpha$ at high spatial frequencies, or detect and mask the large-motion region and leave it unamplified.

Trap. Answering "compute optic flow and scale it up" (the Lagrangian answer). At sub-pixel amplitude the flow estimate is dominated by noise, and you would be magnifying the noise. Name Eulerian explicitly and say why.

PatchMatch

13 points

Figure 3: A patch $P$ in the source and three occurrences of the same content in the target. Standard PatchMatch recovers only the translated copy (green); the rotated and scaled copies (amber) are missed.

Consider Figure 3. PatchMatch finds, for every patch in the source image, its nearest neighbour in the target image, under the assumption that a patch matches another patch by translation only.

Suggest how to extend PatchMatch so that a patch may also match a target patch that is rotated and scaled. Describe precisely what changes in each of the algorithm's two steps, and what your algorithm now stores per pixel.

Ch. 11
Show solution
How to think about it

The key realisation: PatchMatch is not an algorithm about offsets. It is a generic randomised search over a parameter space, and it works because of two properties that have nothing to do with translation — (i) neighbouring pixels tend to have similar parameters, so guesses can be propagated, and (ii) a randomly-seeded field contains a few lucky good guesses. Both survive when you enlarge the parameter space. So: keep the machinery, widen $f$.

What is stored

Instead of a 2-parameter offset $f(x) = (dx, dy)$, store a 4-parameter search state per pixel:

$$ f(x) \;=\; (dx,\; dy,\; \theta,\; s) $$

with $\theta$ the rotation and $s$ the scale. The candidate patch is now $Q_{x+(dx,dy)}$ sampled through the similarity transform $\;T(\theta,s) = s\!\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$, i.e. the target patch is extracted on a rotated/scaled grid around the target point (bilinear interpolation), then compared with the usual $\|P-Q\|_2^2$.

Step 1 — Propagation

Unchanged in structure: in alternating raster order, each pixel takes the best of its own state and its two causal neighbours' states,

$$ f(x) \leftarrow \arg\min_{f \in \{f(x),\, f(x-e_1),\, f(x-e_2)\}} \bigl\| P_x - Q_{x+f} \bigr\| $$

but now the whole 4-tuple is copied, not just the offset. This is still justified: a coherent region that has been rotated and scaled as a unit shares one $(\theta, s)$ as well as one offset, so spatial coherence holds in all four parameters.

Step 2 — Random search

Sample perturbations in all four dimensions around the current best, with independently shrinking radii:

$$ dx' = dx + r_i w_x,\quad dy' = dy + r_i w_y,\quad \theta' = \theta + r_i w_\theta,\quad s' = s\,(1 + r_i w_s) $$

with $r_i = \gamma^i$, $\gamma = 1/2$, $i = 0,1,2,\dots$ until the radius is below one unit; keep the candidate only if it lowers the distance. Initialise uniformly over the full ranges, e.g. $\theta \sim U[-\pi,\pi]$ and $s \sim U[s_{\min}, s_{\max}]$.

The closing sentence

Cost per pixel stays $O(1)$ in image size — still ~3 propagation candidates plus $O(\log w)$ random samples — so the asymptotic advantage over brute force is preserved. The price is a larger constant and a harder search: the space is 4-D instead of 2-D, so more random-search trials (or more iterations) are needed for the same match quality, and scaled patches need interpolation, which costs a little accuracy.

Trap. Proposing to run ordinary PatchMatch once per $(\theta,s)$ from a discretised set and keep the best. It works but it multiplies the runtime by the number of bins and throws away the whole point — propagation should be sharing the rotation and scale too.

Video Colorization

12 points

You are given a grayscale video. A user draws a few coloured scribbles on the first frame only — red on a car, green on the grass, blue on the sky.

Suggest an algorithm that colorizes the entire video.

Now suppose a pedestrian, not present in frame 1, walks into the scene at frame 50. What does your algorithm do, and how would you change it?

Ch. 9
Show solution
How to think about it

Ch.9 exactly. Colour must spread from the scribbles to every pixel, and it must spread within objects but not across their boundaries. The tool that does this is the geodesic distance, where "distance" is measured through the image so that crossing a strong edge is expensive.

Solution
  1. Treat the video as a 3-D volume $(x,y,t)$ — this is the move that makes it a video algorithm rather than a per-frame one.
  2. Let each scribble $k$ define a seed set $\Omega_k$ with colour $c_k$. For every pixel compute the geodesic distance to each seed set, $D_k(x) = \min_{x_0 \in \Omega_k} \inf_\Gamma \int_0^{l(\Gamma)}\!\sqrt{1+\gamma^2\bigl(\nabla I(s)\cdot\Gamma'(s)\bigr)^2}\,ds$, with the gradient taken in space and time. Because the integrand is large where the path runs across a strong gradient, paths prefer to stay inside homogeneous regions.
  3. Assign colour by the nearest seed, $k^*(x) = \arg\min_k D_k(x)$, or better blend softly, $w_k(x) \propto e^{-D_k(x)/\sigma}$, and set the chrominance to $\sum_k w_k c_k$. Keep the original grayscale as the luminance channel and only synthesize chrominance.
  4. Compute the distances with a fast-marching / Dijkstra sweep over the volume, which is what makes this tractable.

The temporal dimension is what carries colour from frame 1 to frame 200: a point on the car in frame 50 is connected to the scribble by a short path through the volume that runs along the car through time, since the car's appearance changes slowly. Equivalently, one may warp the seeds forward with optic flow (Ch.2) and re-solve per frame; say which you chose.

The pedestrian

It gets coloured wrong. Every pixel is forced to take the colour of some scribble — the algorithm has no "none of the above" — so the pedestrian is assigned whichever seed is geodesically closest, most likely bleeding the grass or car colour onto them. Nothing in frame 1 describes them.

Fixes, any of:

  • Detect and ask. Flag pixels whose smallest geodesic distance $\min_k D_k(x)$ exceeds a threshold — that is precisely "this pixel is not well explained by any scribble" — and request a new scribble on those regions. This is the honest answer and it needs no extra machinery.
  • Keyframes. Let the user scribble on a sparse set of keyframes rather than only frame 1, and let colour propagate through the volume from all of them.
  • Detect new content first with background subtraction (Ch.8): a newly-appearing object shows up as foreground against the learned per-pixel GMM, so you can automatically identify the region that needs a new seed.
Trap. Colorizing each frame independently. Colours will flicker frame to frame because the geodesic solution is not temporally constrained, and you would have to re-scribble every frame. The space-time volume is the answer.

Segmentation

13 points

Figure 4: The intensity recorded at a single pixel of the car park over three hours, and the label the background-subtraction system assigns to it. The transition from foreground to background happens long after the car has stopped moving.

Consider Figure 4. A fixed camera monitors a car park, and you run the per-pixel Gaussian Mixture Model background subtraction of the course. A car arrives, parks, and stays for three hours.

(a) Describe what happens to that pixel's mixture model over those three hours, and state precisely the criterion by which the parked car eventually becomes "background".

(b) The operator complains that parked cars take far too long to be absorbed. How would you change the model so that a stationary object joins the background within roughly ten minutes? What do you lose?

Ch. 8
Show solution
(a) What happens

Each pixel holds $K$ Gaussians with weights $\omega_i$, means $\mu_i$ and variances $\sigma_i^2$. When the car arrives, the pixel's colour stops matching any existing Gaussian, so the least-likely component is replaced by a new one centred at the car's colour, with a low initial weight and a high initial variance. That is why the car is initially reported as foreground.

Each subsequent frame matches this new component, so it is updated:

$$ \omega_i \leftarrow (1-\alpha)\,\omega_i + \alpha, \qquad \mu_i \leftarrow (1-\rho)\mu_i + \rho X_t, \qquad \sigma_i^2 \leftarrow (1-\rho)\sigma_i^2 + \rho (X_t-\mu_i)^{\!\top}(X_t-\mu_i) $$

with $\alpha$ the learning rate. So over time its weight grows towards 1 and its variance shrinks (the car's colour is consistent). Meanwhile the old asphalt component, no longer matched, has its weight decayed by $(1-\alpha)$ every frame.

The criterion. Sort the components by $\omega_i/\sigma_i$ — high weight and low variance, i.e. "seen often and consistently". The background is the first $B$ components in that order, where

$$ B = \arg\min_b \Bigl( \sum_{i=1}^{b} \omega_i > T \Bigr) $$

for a threshold $T$. The car becomes background once its component climbs the $\omega/\sigma$ ranking far enough to fall inside that top-$B$ set. Note the ratio is what stops a brief flicker of a consistent colour from being promoted immediately, and what stops a high-variance component (a swaying tree) from being promoted at all.

(b) Making it faster

The time constant is set by the learning rate: a component's weight approaches 1 like $1-(1-\alpha)^t$, so the absorption time scales as $\approx 1/\alpha$ frames. Raise $\alpha$. At 25 fps, ten minutes is $1.5\times10^4$ frames, so $\alpha \sim 10^{-4}$ rather than the much smaller value that gives hours. (Equivalently, keep $\alpha$ but re-run the update on temporally sub-sampled frames.)

What you lose. The learning rate is a single knob controlling all adaptation, so raising it trades in both directions:

  • Slow-moving or briefly-stopped foreground gets swallowed. A person standing still at a ticket machine for a minute now starts being absorbed into the background and disappears from the detections.
  • The model becomes noisier and less stable — it tracks short-term fluctuations (a passing cloud, camera noise) instead of averaging them out, giving more false foreground.
  • Objects that move away leave a "ghost" that clears faster, which is a genuine gain — the trade is not one-sided.

A better answer if you have room: decouple the two, using a fast $\alpha$ for weights and a slow one for $\mu,\sigma$, or explicitly detect "stopped objects" and promote them on a separate timer, so that absorption speed is no longer tied to noise robustness.

Trap. Saying background = "the component with the largest weight". The criterion is the ratio $\omega/\sigma$ and the cumulative-weight threshold $T$, which allows more than one background mode — essential for genuinely multi-modal pixels like a swaying branch or a flickering screen.

Optic Flow

12 points

The Lucas–Kanade method estimates the flow of a pixel by assuming the flow is constant over a small window $W$ around it and solving the resulting over-determined system in the least-squares sense.

Is this system always solvable? Prove it or give a counter-example, and state exactly the condition under which a unique solution exists.

Ch. 2
Show solution
Set-up

Each pixel in the window contributes one brightness-constancy equation $I_x u + I_y v + I_t = 0$, so with $|W| = N$ pixels we get $A\,\mathbf{u} = b$ with

$$ A = \begin{pmatrix} I_x(p_1) & I_y(p_1) \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ I_x(p_N) & I_y(p_N)\end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}, \quad b = -\begin{pmatrix} I_t(p_1)\\ \vdots \\ I_t(p_N)\end{pmatrix} $$

The least-squares solution is $\mathbf{u} = (A^{\!\top}\!A)^{-1}A^{\!\top}b$, so everything hinges on the $2\times2$ matrix

$$ A^{\!\top}\!A \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} \sum I_x^2 & \sum I_xI_y \\[2pt] \sum I_xI_y & \sum I_y^2 \end{pmatrix} $$

— the structure tensor, exactly the matrix of the Harris corner detector.

Answer: no, not always

A unique solution exists if and only if $A^{\!\top}\!A$ is invertible, i.e. both eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 > 0$ are non-zero. In practice one demands $\lambda_2$ be comfortably above the noise floor, and that $\lambda_1/\lambda_2$ not be too large.

Two counter-examples
  • A uniform window (blank wall, clear sky): $I_x = I_y = 0$ everywhere in $W$, so $A^{\!\top}\!A = 0$. Both eigenvalues zero, rank 0 — no information at all, any $\mathbf{u}$ fits.
  • A straight edge through the window: every gradient points the same direction $\mathbf{n}$, so every row of $A$ is a multiple of $\mathbf{n}^{\!\top}$ and $A^{\!\top}\!A = (\sum \|\nabla I\|^2)\,\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{\!\top}$ — an outer product, hence rank 1, $\lambda_2 = 0$, singular. The solution set is a whole line: motion along the edge is unrecoverable. This is the aperture problem, and it is not a numerical accident — the information is genuinely absent.
The link to Harris

Reading the eigenvalues classifies the window, which is precisely the Harris corner detector:

Trap. Answering "yes, because least squares always has a solution." Motion Applications

13 points

Figure 5: Frame selection for a timelapse. (a) Uniform sub-sampling picks frames regardless of how well consecutive choices align. (b) A selection chosen to trade off playback speed against inter-frame alignment.

Consider Figure 5. A helmet camera records a two-hour hike at 30 fps. You want a two-minute timelapse. Keeping every 30th frame gives the right length, but the result is violently shaky and unwatchable, because consecutive kept frames happen to point in very different directions.

Suggest an algorithm that selects which frames to keep so that the output is both approximately the requested length and as smooth as possible. Write down the cost you minimise and say how you minimise it.

Ch. 3 · never examined
Show solution
How to think about it

The naive method fails because it makes the selection decision using only the clock, ignoring what the frames look like. Turn it into an optimisation over which frames to keep. The key structural observation: a selection is an increasing sequence of frame indices, so the search space is a directed acyclic graph and the optimum is a shortest path — solvable exactly by dynamic programming, not by greedy or by search.

The graph

Node $i$ = "output frame $i$ of the input is used". Edge $i \to j$ (with $j > i$) = "frame $j$ is the next one shown after frame $i$". A path from the first frame to the last is a selection.

The cost on an edge
$$ C(i,j) \;=\; \underbrace{C_m(i,j)}_{\text{alignment}} \;+\; \lambda_s\underbrace{C_s(i,j)}_{\text{speed}} $$
  • Alignment / motion cost $C_m(i,j)$ — how badly frames $i$ and $j$ line up. Estimate the homography $H_{ij}$ between them (Ch.1 + RANSAC) and penalise the residual displacement of the frame centre, $\;C_m = \| H_{ij}\,c - c \|$. Small when the camera was pointing the same way; large across a head-turn. This is the term that buys smoothness.
  • Speed cost $C_s(i,j) = \bigl( (j-i) - \tau \bigr)^2$, where $\tau$ is the target skip (here $\tau = 30$). This keeps the output near the requested length while permitting local deviation — the algorithm may skip 22 frames here and 41 there if that avoids a jump.

Add a third term over pairs of edges to penalise changes in speed, $C_a(h,i,j) = \bigl((j-i)-(i-h)\bigr)^2$, so the timelapse does not visibly speed up and slow down. This makes the state $(h,i)$ rather than $i$ — still a DP, just on edges instead of nodes.

Minimising it

Let $D(j)$ be the cost of the best path ending at $j$:

$$ D(j) \;=\; \min_{\,i \,<\, j,\; j-i \le w} \Bigl[\, D(i) + C(i,j) \,\Bigr] $$

Sweep $j$ in increasing order, store the arg-min as a back-pointer, then backtrack from the last frame to read off the chosen sequence. Capping the jump at $j - i \le w$ (say $w = 2\tau$) keeps it $O(N w)$ instead of $O(N^2)$, which matters at $2\text{h} \times 30\,\text{fps} = 216{,}000$ frames.

Closing

Optionally follow with a stabilisation pass — warp the selected frames by a smoothed camera path (fit a low-order curve through the $H$'s and re-render), since frame selection alone can only choose among motions that were actually recorded; it cannot invent a viewpoint that was never captured.

Trap. A greedy answer ("from the current frame, jump to whichever nearby frame aligns best"). It gets stuck: a locally cheap jump can force an expensive one later, and it cannot guarantee the total length. The point of this question is recognising the shortest-path structure and saying dynamic programming — it is the one place in the course where DP is the answer.

Motion Representation

12 points

Figure 6: A camera translating straight along its optical axis at speed $V$ toward an obstacle at distance $Z$, and the resulting optic flow field in the image plane. The point from which the flow appears to radiate is marked FOE.

Consider Figure 6. A camera moves with constant speed $V$ straight along its own optical axis. Focal length is $f$, and image coordinates $(x,y)$ are measured from the principal point.

(a) Derive the optic flow $(u,v)$ at an image point $(x,y)$ corresponding to a scene point at depth $Z$, and show that the flow field is radial.

(b) A robot with this camera must brake before hitting the obstacle. Show that it can compute the time to collision from the flow field alone, without knowing $V$, $Z$, or $f$.

Ch. 1 · never examined
Show solution
(a) Derivation

Pinhole projection of a scene point $(X,Y,Z)$:

$$ x = f\frac{X}{Z}, \qquad y = f\frac{Y}{Z} $$

The camera translates along $+Z$ at speed $V$, so relative to the camera the scene point has $\dot X = 0$, $\dot Y = 0$, $\dot Z = -V$. Differentiate $x$ with respect to time using the quotient rule:

$$ u = \dot x = f\,\frac{\dot X Z - X\dot Z}{Z^2} = f\,\frac{0 - X(-V)}{Z^2} = \frac{f X V}{Z^{2}} = \underbrace{\frac{fX}{Z}}_{=\,x}\cdot\frac{V}{Z} $$

and identically for $y$. Therefore

$$ \boxed{\;(u,v) \;=\; \frac{V}{Z}\,(x,\,y)\;} $$

The flow at every point is parallel to the vector from the principal point to that point — i.e. purely radial, pointing outward, with magnitude proportional to the radius $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. The single point of zero flow, $(0,0)$ here, is the focus of expansion: it is the image of the direction of travel, which is why it stays fixed while everything else streams away from it.

(b) Time to collision

Time to collision is $\text{TTC} = Z/V$. Take the magnitude of the flow, $|(u,v)| = \frac{V}{Z}r$, and divide:

$$ \frac{r}{|(u,v)|} \;=\; \frac{r}{\tfrac{V}{Z}r} \;=\; \frac{Z}{V} \;=\; \text{TTC} $$

Every unknown cancels. $f$, $V$ and $Z$ all disappear; TTC is the ratio of two quantities you can measure directly in the image — a point's distance from the FOE and its flow magnitude. This is why a camera with no calibration, no stereo and no speedometer can still brake in time.

An equivalent and more robust form uses the divergence, which averages over the whole field rather than trusting one point:

$$ \nabla\!\cdot\!(u,v) = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = \frac{V}{Z} + \frac{V}{Z} = \frac{2}{\text{TTC}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{TTC} = \frac{2}{\nabla\!\cdot\!(u,v)} $$
Closing

In practice: estimate the FOE by intersecting the flow vectors (least squares, with RANSAC to reject independently moving objects), then fit $\text{TTC}$ from all points at once. Note the two failure cases — the estimate is ill-conditioned near the FOE itself, where $r$ and $|(u,v)|$ are both near zero and the ratio is noise; and $Z$ is recoverable only up to the scale set by $V$, which is the usual monocular scale ambiguity.

Trap. Claiming you need to recover depth first. You do not, and the whole point is that the ratio $Z/V$ survives while $Z$ and $V$ individually do not. Also do not confuse the FOE with the image centre in general — they coincide only because the motion here is along the optical axis.

Video Matting

13 points

Figure 7: (a) A frame of the input video. (b) A trimap supplied by the user on frame 1 only: known foreground (white), known background (black), unknown band (grey). (c) A zoom on the recovered opacity around the hair. (d) A later frame, for which no trimap was supplied.

Consider Figure 7. A person with loose curly hair walks in front of an ordinary cluttered background — not a green screen. The user supplies a trimap on frame 1 only. Your task is to composite the person onto a new background for the whole 300-frame sequence.

(a) Write down the model you are solving and explain why a hard binary mask cannot work here.

(b) Describe how you obtain a trimap for frames 2 to 300, and what you do to stop the result from flickering.

Ch. 10 · never examined
Show solution
(a) The model

A pixel integrates light over a finite sensor area. Where a hair strand is thinner than a pixel — or at any object boundary — that area contains both hair and background, so the recorded colour is a genuine mixture. There is no correct binary label for such a pixel: foreground keeps a halo of background colour, background chops the hair off. Curly hair is almost entirely such pixels.

Hence the compositing equation with a per-pixel opacity:

$$ I(x) \;=\; \alpha(x)\,F(x) \;+\; \bigl(1-\alpha(x)\bigr)B(x), \qquad \alpha \in [0,1] $$

and the new composite is $I' = \alpha F + (1-\alpha)B_{\text{new}}$.

It is ill-posed: 3 equations (RGB) and 7 unknowns ($\alpha$, and $F,B$ with three channels each) at every pixel — which is exactly why the user must supply a trimap and why we need a colour prior. Note that unlike a green screen, $B$ is now unknown too, so none of the 7 comes for free.

(a, continued) Solving frame 1

Inside the known regions $\alpha$ is 1 and 0 by construction; only the unknown band is solved. For each unknown pixel, collect nearby known foreground and background colours and build non-parametric kernel density estimates $p(F)$ and $p(B)$. Then choose the $(\alpha, F, B)$ that best explains the observed colour — maximise $p(F)\,p(B)$ subject to the compositing equation, or equivalently minimise $\|I - \alpha F - (1-\alpha)B\|^2$ plus the two colour-model penalties.

(b) Propagating to the rest of the sequence

The user will not trimap 300 frames, so the trimap must be carried forward. Two mechanisms, best used together:

  • Warp the trimap with optic flow. Compute the flow from frame $t$ to $t+1$ (Ch.2) and warp the frame-$t$ trimap forward. Then re-dilate the unknown band — flow is unreliable exactly at the boundary and on hair, so widening the band by a few pixels converts a potential hard error into an "I don't know", which the matting step is designed to resolve.
  • Re-derive the band from the previous $\alpha$. Threshold $\alpha_{t-1}$ at, say, $0.05$ and $0.95$ and set the band to the region in between, dilated. This re-anchors the trimap to the matte rather than compounding flow error frame after frame.
(b, continued) Stopping the flicker

Solving each frame independently gives a matte that jitters — the hair boundary "crawls", which is far more visible in motion than any single frame's error. Fixes:

  • Solve on the space-time volume. Treat the video as $(x,y,t)$ and add a temporal smoothness term on $\alpha$, e.g. $\;\mu\sum_t \|\alpha_t - \alpha_{t-1}\circ W_t\|^2$ where $W_t$ warps by the flow. Coherence along motion trajectories, not along fixed pixels.
  • Share the colour models over time — build $p(F)$ and $p(B)$ from a temporal window of frames rather than one, so the estimate does not lurch when a single frame is noisy.
  • Re-key periodically: flag frames whose matting residual is large (occlusion, the person turning, a new background object) and ask the user for a fresh trimap there — a handful of keyframes across 300 is a reasonable interaction budget.
Trap. Recovering $\alpha$ but taking $F$ straight from the input image. The observed colour at a boundary pixel is already mixed, so using it as $F$ carries the old background's colour into the new composite — the classic coloured fringe. $F$ must be un-mixed, which is precisely why it appears as an unknown in the model.

Video Magnification

12 points

Figure 8: A one-dimensional intensity profile of spatial wavelength $\lambda$ (blue) and its shifted version (dashed). (a) The displacement $\delta$ is small compared with $\lambda$. (b) After amplification the displacement is no longer small, and the reconstruction is not a shift of the original.

Consider Figure 8. In Eulerian video magnification, the temporally bandpassed signal is multiplied by $\alpha$ and added back to the original frame.

(a) Show, using a first-order expansion, why adding an amplified intensity change is equivalent to magnifying motion, and state precisely the assumption this relies on.

(b) The method has a bound of the form $(1+\alpha)\,\delta(t) < \lambda/8$. Explain where such a bound comes from, what you see when it is violated, and how you would choose $\alpha$ for an image containing both coarse and fine detail.

Ch. 7 · never examined
Show solution
(a) Why amplifying intensity amplifies motion

Take a 1-D intensity profile $f(x)$ translating by a small time-varying displacement $\delta(t)$, so the observed signal is

$$ I(x,t) = f\bigl(x + \delta(t)\bigr) $$

First-order Taylor expansion about $x$:

$$ I(x,t) \;\approx\; f(x) \;+\; \delta(t)\,\frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x} $$

The temporal bandpass isolates exactly the time-varying part, $B(x,t) = \delta(t)\,\partial f/\partial x$ (assuming $\delta$ lies in the passband and $f(x)$, being constant in time, is removed as DC). Amplify and add back:

$$ \hat I(x,t) = I(x,t) + \alpha B(x,t) = f(x) + (1+\alpha)\,\delta(t)\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \;\approx\; f\bigl(x + (1+\alpha)\delta(t)\bigr) $$

— the same profile, displaced by $(1+\alpha)\delta(t)$. Motion magnified by $(1+\alpha)$, with no tracking and no correspondence.

The assumption is the one made in the very first line: the first-order Taylor expansion is accurate, i.e. $\delta$ is small relative to the scale over which $\partial f/\partial x$ itself changes. Note the last step runs the expansion backwards, so it needs $(1+\alpha)\delta$ — the amplified displacement — to be small too. That is the whole content of part (b).

(b) Where the bound comes from

For a sinusoidal component $f(x) = A\cos(2\pi x/\lambda)$, the Taylor expansion is accurate only while the shift is a small fraction of the wavelength; beyond roughly $\lambda/8$ the linear term is no longer a good model of the true shift, and the reconstruction stops being a translation of the original. Hence

$$ (1+\alpha)\,\delta(t) \;<\; \frac{\lambda}{8} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \alpha \;<\; \frac{\lambda}{8\,\delta(t)} - 1 $$

The bound is per spatial wavelength: fine detail (small $\lambda$) tolerates far less amplification than coarse structure.

When violated you no longer get a shifted image, you get $f(x)$ plus a large multiple of its derivative — which looks like ringing and overshoot at edges, ghosting/doubling, and clipping where the amplified signal leaves the valid intensity range. Noise is amplified by the same factor, so it also becomes visibly grainy.

Choosing α with mixed detail

Do not use one $\alpha$. Since the pyramid already separates spatial scales, use a spatial-frequency-dependent $\alpha$: apply the full $\alpha$ at coarse pyramid levels (large $\lambda$) and attenuate it toward zero at fine levels, following the bound above. In practice: pick a target $\alpha$ and a cutoff wavelength $\lambda_c$, use $\alpha$ for $\lambda > \lambda_c$, and taper linearly below it. This is what lets you push amplification hard on the slow, large-scale signal you care about without the fine texture exploding.

Trap. Answering that the limit is "when the motion gets too big to see properly". The bound is not about visibility, it is about the validity of the first-order approximation, and it is stated relative to the spatial wavelength, not in absolute pixels. A 2-pixel displacement is fine on a smooth gradient and catastrophic on a checkerboard.

Layer Decomposition

13 points

Figure 9: (a) A video shot through a shop window. (b) The transmitted layer — the display behind the glass. (c) The reflected layer — the street behind the camera. The observed video is the sum of the two.

Consider Figure 9. You film a shop window while walking slowly past it. Each frame shows both the display behind the glass and a reflection of the street behind you, superimposed. The two contents move differently across the sequence.

Suggest an algorithm that takes the video and outputs two separate videos, one per layer. State the image formation model you assume and explain what property of the sequence makes the separation possible at all.

Ch. 6 · never examined
Show solution
The model

Glass both transmits and reflects, and neither layer occludes the other, so the layers combine additively rather than by opacity:

$$ I(x,y,t) \;=\; T(x,y,t) \;+\; R(x,y,t) $$

(Contrast this with matting, Ch.10, where the composite is $\alpha F + (1-\alpha)B$ — here there is no $\alpha$, which actually makes the algebra simpler.) This is severely ill-posed per frame: one equation, two unknowns per pixel, and $T = I,\ R = 0$ always fits.

What makes it solvable

The two layers undergo different, coherent parametric motions. The display is at the window plane; the reflection is a virtual image located behind the camera, at a different effective depth. As you walk past, the two therefore move with different parallax — each layer is internally consistent but the two disagree. A single frame contains no information to separate them; the motion difference across frames is the entire signal. (If you stood perfectly still, the problem would be unsolvable — worth saying.)

The algorithm
  1. Estimate two dominant motions. Compute local motion on blocks (Lucas–Kanade, Ch.2), fit an affine/homography model per block, and cluster the parameter vectors with K-means, $K = 2$ (Ch.6). Two clusters emerge, $\theta_T$ and $\theta_R$ — the display's motion and the reflection's motion. Blocks where one layer dominates vote cleanly; mixed blocks fall between and are simply unreliable votes at this stage.
  2. Align to layer $T$. Warp every frame by $\theta_T^{-1}$ into a common reference. In this stack the display is stationary and the reflection slides.
  3. Separate by a temporal statistic. Take the per-pixel temporal median of the aligned stack. What is constant survives; what moves is a minority at any given pixel and is rejected as an outlier. That gives $\hat T$. Then $\hat R_t = I_t - \hat T$ warped back.
  4. Refine by alternating. Now align to $\theta_R$ instead, median the stack $I_t - \hat T$ to get a cleaner $\hat R$, subtract to re-estimate $\hat T$, and iterate. Each pass sharpens both layers and lets you re-fit the two motion models on cleaner data.

Add a prior if the residual is ambiguous: natural images have sparse gradients (Ch.12), so prefer the decomposition minimising $\sum \|\nabla T\|_1 + \|\nabla R\|_1$ — this discourages splitting a single real edge across both layers.

Closing

Note the family resemblance: step 3 is exactly the argument of Ch.12 — align, then let a temporal median reject whatever is inconsistent — and the setup is the additive-transparency case that appears there as the Reagan/Einstein experiment. Failure cases: the reflection is often much dimmer, so it is recovered with a lower signal-to-noise ratio; and if the two motions happen to coincide (you move exactly parallel to the glass at the right distance) the two clusters merge and separation fails.

Trap. Reaching for background subtraction (Ch.8). The GMM assumes the background is what a pixel sees most of the time and that foreground occludes it. Here nothing occludes anything — both layers are present in every pixel of every frame simultaneously — so the occlusion model does not apply and you must use the additive model plus motion.

drill

Figure 10: (Left) Top view of the setup: a camera rotating about its centre of projection $C$ on a tripod. (Right) The scene — a static building, with a bus driving through part of the field of view.

Consider Figure 10. A camera on a tripod rotates about its optical centre and zooms while filming a static building.

(a) What is the minimal motion model that exactly describes the mapping between any two frames, and how many free parameters does it have? Justify why a simpler model is insufficient and a more general one unnecessary.

(b) A bus drives past, covering about 20% of the frame. How do you estimate the model reliably?

Ch. 1 · Ch. 3
Show solution
(a)

A homography (projective transform), 8 free parameters — a $3\times3$ matrix defined up to scale.

Why it is exact: when the camera rotates about its centre of projection, there is no parallax — the mapping between two views is $H = K_2 R K_1^{-1}$, independent of scene depth. Zoom just changes the intrinsics $K$, which stays inside the same form. So one homography maps any frame to any other exactly, however complicated the 3-D scene.

Why not simpler: an affine model (6 params) cannot represent the perspective foreshortening induced by rotation; straight lines stay straight but the vanishing-point behaviour is wrong. A pure translation or similarity is even further off.

Why not more general: nothing more is needed — there is no parallax to explain, so optic flow or a per-pixel model would be over-parameterised and less robust. (Note the crucial caveat: this holds only because the camera rotates about its centre. Translate the tripod and parallax appears, no single homography exists, and you need depth.)

(b)

RANSAC. The bus is a coherent 20% of the measurements that follow a different motion — exactly the outlier structure least squares handles worst, since a consistent outlier population biases the fit rather than averaging out.

  1. Detect and match features between frames (Harris corners + descriptors).
  2. Repeat: sample 4 point correspondences at random (the minimum for a homography), solve for $H$ exactly, and count inliers — matches with reprojection error $\|x' - Hx\| < \tau$.
  3. Keep the $H$ with the largest inlier set, then re-fit by least squares on all its inliers.

Iterations needed: $N = \log(1-p)/\log(1-w^s)$ with $w \approx 0.8$ inlier rate and $s=4$, so a few dozen samples suffice for $p = 0.99$.

Trap. Answering "least squares over all matches". The bus's matches are mutually consistent, so they pull the estimate toward a compromise between two motions rather than cancelling out. Robustness here requires a consensus method.

drill

A security camera records a car driving away. You extract 30 consecutive frames, each containing the licence plate, but in every single frame the plate is too low-resolution to read.

Suggest an algorithm that produces one readable image of the plate. Under what condition does your method gain nothing at all?

Ch. 3
Show solution
How to think about it

Ch.3, super-resolution. The 30 frames are not 30 copies of the same measurement: because the car moves, each frame samples the underlying continuous plate on a slightly different sub-pixel grid. That sub-pixel diversity is the extra information, and fusing it recovers detail no single frame contains.

Solution
  1. Register to sub-pixel accuracy. Pick a reference frame and estimate the motion of the plate region from every other frame to it, at sub-pixel precision (Lucas–Kanade; the plate is planar and roughly fronto-parallel over a short burst, so a homography or even affine model suffices). Registration accuracy is the whole ballgame — errors here translate directly into blur.
  2. Warp onto a finer grid. Choose an output grid $k\times$ denser (say $k=3$) and map every pixel of every frame onto it using the estimated motion. Each low-res pixel lands at a non-integer position, so together the 30 frames populate the fine grid densely.
  3. Fuse. Combine the scattered samples into the high-res image — e.g. iteratively minimise $\sum_t \| D\,B\,W_t\,x - y_t \|^2 + \lambda\,\Gamma(x)$, where $W_t$ warps, $B$ blurs by the camera PSF, $D$ downsamples, $y_t$ are the observed frames and $\Gamma$ is a smoothness/edge prior. Solve by gradient descent (back-projection).
When it gains nothing

If the motion between frames is a whole number of pixels (or zero — a stationary car). Then every frame samples the scene on exactly the same grid, the 30 images are shifted copies carrying identical information, and averaging them reduces noise but adds no resolution whatsoever. Sub-pixel motion is not an inconvenience to be corrected — it is the signal the method feeds on.

Trap. Answering "upsample and sharpen" (bicubic + unsharp mask). That invents no new information; it only redistributes what one frame already had. The marks are for using the multiple frames and saying why sub-pixel shifts matter.

A static camera films a street. A train passes horizontally in front of a stationary building, and a flag flutters on the building's roof.

Suggest an algorithm that takes the video and outputs a labelling of every pixel into coherently-moving layers. How many layers should your algorithm find, and what decides that number?

Ch. 6
Show solution
How to think about it

Ch.6: layer decomposition = "group pixels that share one parametric motion". The recipe is estimate motion → cluster the motions → assign pixels → iterate.

Solution
  1. Local motion estimation. Divide the frame into blocks (or use optic flow, Ch.2) and fit an affine motion model to each block by Lucas–Kanade, giving a 6-vector $\theta_j = (a_1,\dots,a_6)$ per block.
  2. Cluster the motion parameters with K-means in the 6-D parameter space. Blocks on the train share one $\theta$ (pure horizontal translation), blocks on the building share another (identity), so they form distinct clusters.
  3. Assign every pixel to the layer whose motion model best predicts it — minimise the residual $|I_2(x + \theta_k(x)) - I_1(x)|$ over $k$.
  4. Iterate (an EM-style loop): re-fit each layer's affine model using all pixels currently assigned to it, then re-assign. Repeat to convergence. This is what sharpens the boundaries — the initial block grid straddles the train's edge, and re-fitting fixes it.
How many layers

Two dominant ones — train and building/background. The flag is the interesting case: it moves, but not according to any single parametric model (it is a non-rigid, stochastic dynamic texture, Ch.5). So it will not form a clean affine cluster; it either gets absorbed into the background layer with a large residual, or fragments across several tiny spurious layers.

What decides $K$: it is a parameter of K-means and must be chosen, e.g. by the usual distortion-vs-$K$ knee, by merging clusters whose motion models are within a tolerance of each other, or by a model-order criterion (MDL) that penalises layers which do not reduce the residual enough to pay for themselves. Saying "$K$ is a free parameter and here is how I would set it" is the expected answer.

Trap. Forgetting the iterate step and reporting the raw block clustering. Block boundaries do not coincide with object boundaries, so a one-pass answer gives a blocky, wrong mask. Also worth one sentence: pixels near the train's edge are ambiguous (occlusion), and a residual threshold should leave them unassigned rather than forcing a label.

Video Matting

drill

A presenter with loose curly hair stands in front of a green screen. An engineer implements the compositing as a hard binary decision — "if the pixel is green, replace it with the new background; otherwise keep it" — and the result looks wrong around the hair.

Explain precisely why this fails, write the model that fixes it, and describe how to estimate its unknowns.

Ch. 10
Show solution
Why the hard decision fails

A pixel is not a point — it integrates light over a finite sensor area. Where a hair strand is thinner than a pixel, or at any object boundary, that area contains both hair and green screen, so the recorded colour is a mixture of the two. There is no correct binary answer for such a pixel: calling it foreground keeps a green fringe, calling it background chops the hair away. Curly hair is nearly all such pixels, which is why it is the standard failure case.

The model

The compositing equation, with a per-pixel opacity $\alpha \in [0,1]$:

$$ I(x) \;=\; \alpha(x)\,F(x) \;+\; \bigl(1-\alpha(x)\bigr)\,B(x) $$

$\alpha = 1$ is pure foreground, $\alpha = 0$ pure background, and intermediate values are the mixed pixels. Compositing onto a new background is then $I' = \alpha F + (1-\alpha)B_{\text{new}}$.

This is ill-posed: per pixel there are 3 equations (RGB) and 7 unknowns ($\alpha$, $F$, $B$ — 3 channels each). Hence the need for user input and a prior.

Estimating the unknowns
  1. Trimap. The user (or an automatic step) partitions the frame into definite foreground, definite background, and a narrow unknown band around the boundary. Inside the first two, $\alpha$ is 1 and 0 by construction; only the band must be solved.
  2. Model the colour distributions. For each unknown pixel, gather nearby known foreground and background samples and build non-parametric estimates of $p(F)$ and $p(B)$ using kernel density estimation (Ch.10).
  3. Solve per pixel for the $(\alpha, F, B)$ that best explains the observed colour under those densities — maximise $p(F)p(B)$ subject to $I = \alpha F + (1-\alpha)B$, or equivalently minimise the compositing residual plus the two colour-model penalties.
  4. For video, add temporal coherence: solve on the space-time volume, or propagate the trimap between frames with optic flow (Ch.2), so $\alpha$ does not flicker frame to frame — a per-frame solution produces visible boundary crawl.

The green screen is a genuine simplification worth mentioning: $B$ is known and nearly constant, which removes 3 of the 7 unknowns and makes the system far better conditioned than natural-image matting.

Trap. Stopping at "use a soft threshold on greenness". That produces an $\alpha$ but does not recover $F$, so the hair keeps its green spill — the recovered foreground colour must be un-mixed too, which is why $F$ is an unknown in the model and not just taken from $I$.

Name the chapter/method in the first lineShows you decoded the costume. Half the difficulty of these questions is identification.
State the assumption being violated or extendedNearly every question is built on one.
Write the modified equation"Show all work" — a paragraph without an equation reads as vague, however correct.
Change the minimumRebuilding from scratch signals you did not see the connection.
Say what breaks / the degenerate caseOne sentence, very cheap, reads as real understanding rather than recall.
Fit on one pageIt is an explicit instruction on every paper.
