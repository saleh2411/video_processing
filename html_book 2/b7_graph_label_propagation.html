Companion page to Chapters 9 & 6 · Moed B 2022, question 7 · 13 points

It says "social network". It means colorization.

A handful of labelled nodes, a weighted graph, and a request for a mixture at every other node. That is not a new problem — it is scribble propagation from the colorization chapter with the pixel grid replaced by an arbitrary graph. The answer is the harmonic solution of the graph Laplacian, it has a closed form, a two-line iterative version that scales, and a random-walk interpretation that explains why the numbers are percentages in the first place.

Moed B · 1/8/2022 · Question 7 · 13 points · SEGMENTATION

Consider a Social network defined as a graph $G=(V,E)$, where nodes $v\in V$ denote users and edges $(u,v)\in E$ denote connections between users. The weight function $w(u,v):E\to\mathbb{R}$ defines the strength of the connection between users $u$ and $v$.

Assume the owner of the graph marks a few of the users (i.e., nodes) with a label (say, user $u_1$ is a financial expert and user $u_2$ is a fashion expert). We wish to learn, for each node in the graph, his/her mix of interests (i.e., user $v$ is 30% interested in finance and 70% interested in fashion). Suggest an algorithm for doing that.

1 · The story

The kid, the paint, and the string

Imagine every person is a little bowl, and the friendships between them are straws connecting the bowls. A thick straw means best friends; a thin straw means you met once.

Now pour blue paint into the bowl of the finance expert and keep pouring — that bowl stays pure blue forever, because he is the finance expert. Pour pink paint into the fashion expert's bowl, and keep it pure pink forever.

Then just wait. Paint flows through the straws. A bowl connected by three thick straws to blue bowls and one thin straw to a pink bowl ends up mostly blue with a hint of pink. A bowl exactly between the two groups ends up genuinely purple.

When everything stops moving, look in each bowl: 70% blue and 30% pink. That is the answer the question asks for, and the "70/30" is not a metaphor — it comes out of the maths as an exact proportion, because paint is never created or destroyed as it flows.

Two ways to say what "stopped moving" means, and they are the same statement:

  • Balance. Every unlabelled bowl holds exactly the (weight-)average of its neighbours. Nothing wants to flow any more.
  • Smoothness. Friends ended up with similar mixes. Formally, we minimised the total disagreement across all the straws, thick straws counting more.

And a third way to say it, which is the one that explains the percentages: drop an ant on a node and let it wander along the straws, preferring thick ones. The chance it bumps into the finance expert before it bumps into the fashion expert — that is exactly the blue fraction.

The whole answer, in one sentence Solve for the harmonic function on the graph: fix $f = $ one-hot at the labelled nodes, and at every other node set $f(v)$ = the weighted average of its neighbours. Equivalently minimise $\frac12\sum_{(u,v)\in E} w(u,v)\|f(u)-f(v)\|^2$ subject to the label constraints — a quadratic in the graph Laplacian with the closed form $f_U = (D_{UU}-W_{UU})^{-1}W_{UL}f_L$, computed in practice by the two-line iteration $f \leftarrow D^{-1}Wf$ with the labels re-clamped each round.

2 · Setting it up

Notation
  • $W$ — the $n\times n$ symmetric weight matrix, $W_{uv}=w(u,v)\ge0$ ($0$ if not connected).
  • $D = \mathrm{diag}(d_1,\dots,d_n)$, $d_u = \sum_v W_{uv}$ — the degree matrix.
  • $L = D-W$ — the graph Laplacian.
  • $c$ labels (here $c=2$: finance, fashion). $F \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times c}$, row $f(v)^{\!\top}$ is $v$'s mixture. We will need $f(v)\ge 0$ and $\sum_\ell f_\ell(v)=1$ for it to be a mixture.
  • $L$ (as an index set) = the labelled nodes, $U$ = the unlabelled ones. $F_L$ is fixed to one-hot rows: $f(u_1)=(1,0)$, $f(u_2)=(0,1)$.
The objective — say the assumption out loud

The only thing we assume is homophily: strongly connected users have similar interests. Turn that into an energy that punishes disagreement in proportion to the connection strength:

$$ E(F) \;=\; \tfrac12\sum_{(u,v)\in E} w(u,v)\,\big\|f(u)-f(v)\big\|^2 \;=\; \operatorname{tr}\!\big(F^{\!\top} L\,F\big) \quad\text{s.t.}\quad F_L = Y_L \tag{1} $$

$L$ is positive semi-definite ($x^{\!\top}Lx = \frac12\sum w_{uv}(x_u-x_v)^2 \ge 0$), so (1) is a convex quadratic and has a unique minimiser as long as every connected component contains at least one labelled node. Differentiating with respect to the free rows:

$$ \frac{\partial E}{\partial f(v)} = 2\sum_{u} w(u,v)\big(f(v)-f(u)\big) = 0 \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; f(v) = \frac{1}{d_v}\sum_{u} w(u,v)\, f(u) \quad \forall v\in U \tag{2} $$
Equation (2) is the whole algorithm Every unlabelled node equals the weighted average of its neighbours. A function with that property is called harmonic — the discrete analogue of a soap film stretched over a wire frame, where the wire is the labelled set. Everything below is just different ways of computing it.
Why the answer is automatically a set of percentages

Two facts, both immediate from (2), and both worth stating in the exam:

  • Non-negativity and boundedness (the maximum principle). A weighted average of numbers in $[0,1]$ is in $[0,1]$. So no node can be $-15\%$ interested in finance, and none can be $130\%$: the extremes are attained only at the labelled nodes.
  • Sum to one. Let $s(v) = \sum_\ell f_\ell(v)$. Summing (2) over $\ell$ shows $s$ is itself harmonic, and $s \equiv 1$ on the labelled nodes. A harmonic function with constant boundary values is that constant, so $s\equiv1$ everywhere.

So the output is a genuine probability distribution per node — "30% finance, 70% fashion" — with no normalisation hack required. That is a real advantage over, say, fitting a regressor per label independently.

3 · Three ways to compute it

Same answer, different trade-offs
MethodFormulaCost / when
A · Closed form Partition $L=\begin{pmatrix}L_{LL}&L_{LU}\\L_{UL}&L_{UU}\end{pmatrix}$. Then $L_{UU}F_U + L_{UL}F_L = 0$, i.e. $$F_U = -L_{UU}^{-1}L_{UL}F_L = (D_{UU}-W_{UU})^{-1}W_{UL}F_L$$ $L_{UU}$ is symmetric, diagonally dominant and non-singular (whenever every component has a label), so it is an M-matrix: sparse Cholesky or preconditioned conjugate gradient solves it fast. Exact. Best for up to $\sim10^6$ nodes.
B · Iterative (label propagation) $F \leftarrow D^{-1}WF$, then re-clamp $F_L \leftarrow Y_L$. Repeat until $\|\Delta F\|_\infty<\varepsilon$. $O(|E|\,c)$ per sweep, no matrix stored, trivially parallel/distributed — the practical choice for a real social graph. This is exactly Jacobi iteration on system A, and it converges geometrically (see below).
C · Random walk (Monte-Carlo) $f_\ell(v) = \Pr[$ a random walk from $v$ with $P=D^{-1}W$ is absorbed at a node labelled $\ell]$. $O(\text{walks}\times\text{length})$ and needs no global pass — the only option if you want a single user's mixture without touching the whole graph. Also the interpretation that explains the percentages.

A, B and C compute the same function. A is the linear system, B is an iterative solver for it, C is its probabilistic meaning.

Why B converges

Write the iteration on the free block only: $F_U^{(t+1)} = P_{UU}F_U^{(t)} + P_{UL}Y_L$ with $P = D^{-1}W$ row-stochastic. Then

$$ F_U^{(t)} - F_U^\star = P_{UU}^{\,t}\big(F_U^{(0)} - F_U^\star\big), \qquad \|P_{UU}\|_\infty = \max_{v\in U}\sum_{u\in U}P_{vu} \;\le\; 1 $$

and the inequality is strict for any unlabelled node adjacent to a labelled one, because that node leaks some of its row mass into $P_{UL}$. Since every unlabelled node can reach a labelled node (our standing assumption), some power $P_{UU}^{\,k}$ has all row sums $<1$, so $\rho(P_{UU})<1$ and the error decays geometrically. The rate is governed by the spectral gap — a graph with a narrow bottleneck between two communities converges slowly, which is precisely the regime where the answer is most interesting.

Two communities joined by a thin bridge. Ring colour shows each user's mixture — blue = finance, pink = fashion — with the exact percentage printed inside. Edge thickness is $w(u,v)$. Click any node to cycle it through unlabelled → finance expert → fashion expert. Labelled nodes have a white outline and never change.

Things to try 1. Step once, twice, three times: information spreads exactly one hop per iteration — that is why the number of iterations you need is roughly the graph's diameter divided by the spectral gap. 2. Drag the bridge strength to almost zero: the right community becomes uniformly $0\%$ finance, because the only path to the finance expert has dried up. Raise it and watch a real gradient appear across the bridge. 3. Label a third node in the middle as a finance expert and see the whole right community shift — this is why a handful of well-placed labels beats many badly-placed ones. 4. Turn on soft clamping: even the labelled nodes now drift slightly, which is what you want if the owner's labels might be wrong.

5 · Variants, edge cases, and the course connection

What to add for full marks
IssueHandling
Noisy or contradictory labels Hard clamping trusts the owner absolutely. Use the soft variant (Zhou et al., "local and global consistency"): with $S = D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}$, $F^{(t+1)} = \alpha S F^{(t)} + (1-\alpha)Y$, whose fixed point is $F^\star=(1-\alpha)(I-\alpha S)^{-1}Y$. A labelled node can now be overruled by an overwhelming neighbourhood. $\alpha\to1$ recovers hard propagation; $\alpha\to0$ ignores the graph.
A component with no label $L_{UU}$ becomes singular and the mixture is genuinely undefined — there is no information. Detect it (connected components) and either report "unknown" or fall back to a prior. The clean fix is a teleport: add a virtual node connected to everyone with small weight $\epsilon$ carrying the prior distribution. This is exactly damping in personalised PageRank, and it also makes $L_{UU}$ strictly diagonally dominant, hence better conditioned.
Class imbalance If there are 50 finance experts and 1 fashion expert, mass floods in from the finance side and every node looks financial. Fix with class mass normalisation: rescale each column of $F_U$ so the total mass per class matches a desired prior, then re-normalise the rows.
Where do the weights come from? The question hands you $w$. If it did not, you would build it — e.g. $w(u,v)=\exp(-\|x_u-x_v\|^2/2\sigma^2)$ from user features, or interaction counts. Note the answer is very sensitive to $\sigma$; this is the same "how do I set the affinity scale" question as in normalized cuts.
Directed / asymmetric graphs "$u$ follows $v$" is not symmetric. The energy (1) needs a symmetric $W$; either symmetrise ($\tilde W = (W+W^{\!\top})/2$) or drop the energy formulation and keep the random-walk one, which works verbatim for any row-stochastic $P$ — the absorption probabilities are still the solution of $f = Pf$ with clamped rows.
Scale Method B is $O(|E|c)$ per sweep and needs only neighbour messages, so it maps directly onto Pregel/GraphX-style distributed processing. For a billion-edge graph that is the only viable option.
Why this is a video processing question

Replace "user" with "pixel", "connection strength" with "colour similarity between neighbouring pixels", and "expert" with "user scribble", and you have Chapter 9's colorization verbatim: the user scribbles a few colours, and the algorithm solves exactly this quadratic to spread them, with weights $w(u,v)=\exp(-(I_u-I_v)^2/2\sigma^2)$ so colour flows within a region and stops at edges. The same solve is Chapter 10's matting (labels = foreground/background, output $\alpha \in [0,1]$ is literally the mixture) and it is the supervised sibling of the normalized-cut segmentation in the layers material — there you have no labels and must take eigenvectors of $L$; here you have labels and get a plain linear solve. Saying this connection explicitly is the difference between an answer and a good answer.

6 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

One page, as the exam asks

Model. Let $W$ be the symmetric weight matrix ($W_{uv}=w(u,v)\ge0$), $D=\mathrm{diag}(d_u)$ with $d_u=\sum_v W_{uv}$, and $L=D-W$ the graph Laplacian. Represent each user's interests as a row $f(v)\in\Delta^{c-1}$ of $F\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times c}$. Clamp the labelled users to one-hot rows: $f(u_1)=(1,0)$, $f(u_2)=(0,1)$.

Assumption and objective. Assume homophily — strongly connected users share interests — and minimise the weighted disagreement

$$ \min_F\ E(F)=\tfrac12\!\!\sum_{(u,v)\in E}\!\! w(u,v)\|f(u)-f(v)\|^2 = \operatorname{tr}(F^{\!\top}LF) \quad\text{s.t. } F_L=Y_L. $$

$L\succeq0$, so this is convex with a unique solution whenever every connected component contains a label. Setting the gradient to zero gives the harmonic condition: each unlabelled node is the weighted average of its neighbours, $f(v)=\frac{1}{d_v}\sum_u w(u,v)f(u)$.

Algorithm (iterative — the one to implement). (1) initialise $F$ arbitrarily (uniform) and set $F_L=Y_L$; (2) $F \leftarrow D^{-1}WF$; (3) re-clamp $F_L\leftarrow Y_L$; (4) repeat (2)–(3) until $\|\Delta F\|_\infty<\varepsilon$. Cost $O(|E|c)$ per sweep, no matrix assembly, distributes across machines. It is Jacobi iteration for the linear system, and it converges geometrically because $\rho(P_{UU})<1$ where $P=D^{-1}W$ — each unlabelled node adjacent to a labelled one leaks row mass, so some power of $P_{UU}$ is a strict contraction.

Closed form (equivalent). Partitioning into labelled/unlabelled blocks, $L_{UU}F_U+L_{UL}F_L=0$, hence

$$ F_U = -L_{UU}^{-1}L_{UL}F_L = (D_{UU}-W_{UU})^{-1}W_{UL}F_L, $$

a sparse symmetric diagonally-dominant system solved by Cholesky or conjugate gradient.

Why the output is a valid mixture. By the maximum principle a weighted average of values in $[0,1]$ stays in $[0,1]$, so all entries are non-negative; and $\sum_\ell f_\ell$ is itself harmonic with boundary value $1$, hence identically $1$. So $f(v)$ is a probability distribution — "30% finance, 70% fashion" — with no post-normalisation. Its meaning: $f_\ell(v)$ is the probability that a random walk started at $v$, moving with $P=D^{-1}W$, reaches a node labelled $\ell$ before any other labelled node.

Practical notes. For unreliable labels use soft clamping $F\leftarrow\alpha SF+(1-\alpha)Y$ with $S=D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}$ (fixed point $(1-\alpha)(I-\alpha S)^{-1}Y$). For components with no labelled node the answer is undefined — add a small-weight teleport edge to a virtual prior node (personalised PageRank damping), which also improves conditioning. With very unequal numbers of labels per class, apply class-mass normalisation. This is exactly the scribble-propagation solve used for video colorization and matting, with pixels replaced by users and colour similarity replaced by connection strength.

7 · Exam drill

Q. Why not just k-means / spectral clustering on the graph?

Because they answer a different question. Clustering is unsupervised: it partitions the graph into groups and then you would still have to decide which group is "finance", and every node would get a hard 0/1 assignment rather than a mixture. This question hands you labels and asks for soft proportions, so the right tool is semi-supervised. The connection is close, though: the normalized-cut relaxation minimises the same Dirichlet energy $x^{\!\top}Lx$ but subject to a norm constraint rather than boundary conditions, so it ends up as an eigenvector problem instead of a linear solve. Labels turn an eigenproblem into a much easier linear system — that is the whole benefit of supervision here.

Q. Prove that the mixture at a node lies between the min and max of its neighbours.

Directly from (2): $f(v)=\sum_u \frac{w(u,v)}{d_v}f(u)$, and the coefficients $\frac{w(u,v)}{d_v}$ are non-negative and sum to $1$ — so $f(v)$ is a convex combination of its neighbours' values and therefore lies in their convex hull, hence between their min and max. Consequently the global extrema of $f$ over the graph must occur at labelled nodes (the discrete maximum principle): if an interior node were a strict maximum it would have to exceed the average of its neighbours, contradicting (2). This is the argument behind both "no negative percentages" and "the rows sum to one".

Q. How many labels do you need, and where should they go?

At minimum, one per connected component per class — otherwise a class simply cannot reach part of the graph. Beyond that, placement matters far more than count: labels should cover the graph's bottlenecks, because the solution changes fastest there and is nearly constant inside a tightly-knit community. This is the standard active-learning result for graph SSL: query the node whose label reduces the expected error most, which in practice means high-centrality nodes and nodes whose current mixture is closest to $50/50$ (maximum uncertainty). One well-placed label in a community beats ten labels piled inside another one.

Q. What if the graph is huge — a billion edges?

Use method B and never build a matrix. Each sweep is "every node sends $f(v)$ weighted by $w$ along each edge, then divides by its degree", which is one map-reduce / Pregel superstep and is embarrassingly parallel. Convergence typically needs tens of sweeps, not thousands, because information travels one hop per sweep and social graphs have small diameter. Two useful accelerations: run it on a coarsened graph first and use the result to initialise the fine level (algebraic multigrid), and stop early — you rarely need the mixture to more than two decimals. If you only want one user's mixture, use method C (random walks from that node), which touches only a local neighbourhood.

Q. Show that the random-walk interpretation really gives the same $f$.

Let $h_\ell(v)$ be the probability that a walk from $v$ is absorbed at a node labelled $\ell$. Condition on the first step: from an unlabelled $v$ the walk moves to $u$ with probability $P_{vu}=w(u,v)/d_v$, so $h_\ell(v)=\sum_u P_{vu}h_\ell(u)$ — which is exactly the harmonic condition (2). And on labelled nodes $h_\ell$ is $1$ for its own label, $0$ otherwise — exactly the clamping. The harmonic function with those boundary values is unique (by the maximum principle applied to the difference of two solutions), so $h_\ell \equiv f_\ell$. This is the classical connection between the Dirichlet problem and Brownian motion, on a graph.

Q. What breaks if some weights are negative (an "enemy" edge)?

Nearly everything. $L$ is no longer positive semi-definite, so (1) may be unbounded below and the "minimiser" meaningless; the coefficients in (2) stop being a convex combination, so mixtures can go negative or exceed 1; and the random-walk interpretation dies because $P$ is no longer a stochastic matrix. Signed graphs need a different formulation — e.g. the signed Laplacian $\bar L = \bar D - W$ with $\bar D = \mathrm{diag}(\sum_v|w_{uv}|)$, which is PSD again — or a model based on structural balance. Worth one sentence in the exam: it shows you know why the non-negativity of $w$ is load-bearing rather than incidental.

Q. The question says "segmentation". Where is the segmentation?

Take the argmax per node and you have a hard partition of the graph into a finance region and a fashion region — a segmentation, with the boundary sitting where the mixture crosses 50/50, which is exactly at the graph's bottleneck. The soft mixture is strictly more informative: it is the graph analogue of an alpha matte rather than a binary mask, and the pixels/users where it is near $0.5$ are precisely the ones you are unsure about. That is also why the same solve is used for matting in Chapter 10 — there the "mixture" is the opacity $\alpha$.
