Every ray you will ever need was already filmed

Do not try to reconstruct the 3-D scene. Do not try to estimate depth. The whole answer is a lookup table: for each pixel of the virtual camera, work out which ray it wants, then work out which frame and which column of the video already recorded that exact ray.

This is the same question as Moed B 2022 Q4. If you want the long derivation, the sampling analysis and the depth-aliasing discussion, go to B4 · Camera on a rotating arm. This page is the short, story-first version.

Exam 8/7/2020 · Question 4 · 12 points · LIGHT FIELD

Consider Figure 3. On the left you see a camera attached to an arm that is rotating around an axis. The camera is recording a video sequence as it is rotating. On the right, you see a schematic top view of the setup. Suggest a way to synthesize a novel view from the virtual camera inside the circle, using the images taken by the rotating camera.

The 30-second version — read this first Pixel column $\beta$ of frame $\theta$ records a ray whose distance from the rotation axis is $$ r \;=\; R\sin\beta $$ — independent of $\theta$. So the video is really a stack of $360°$ panoramas ("concentric mosaics"), one per column.

To render: for each virtual pixel find the ray's direction $\psi$ and its distance from the axis $r$, then read it straight out of the video at $$ \beta=\arcsin(r/R),\qquad \theta=\psi-\beta. $$ No depth, no 3-D, $O(1)$ per pixel. Works for any virtual camera inside the disc of radius $R\sin\beta_{\max}$.

1 · The story (no math at all)

The kid on the roundabout with a camera

You sit on a playground roundabout holding a camera, looking outwards. Somebody spins you. You film the whole spin.

Afterwards your friend says: “I want to see what the park looks like from over there” — pointing at a spot inside the circle, somewhere you never sat.

The naive thought is: impossible, I was never there. But think about what a photo actually is. A photo is not a place. A photo is a bundle of light rays that happened to arrive at a point. If I can find, for each ray your friend wants, a moment when that very same ray of light passed through my camera, I can just hand them that pixel. It does not matter that my camera was somewhere else — the ray is the same ray.

So the job is a librarian's job, not a sculptor's. You do not rebuild the park. You look up rays.

And here is the lovely fact that makes the lookup easy. As you spin, look at one fixed column of your camera's picture — say 20° to the left of straight ahead. Because the arm and the direction you face rotate together, that column always points “the same amount sideways”, so the line of sight always brushes past the centre pole at the same distance. Over one full spin, that single column sweeps out a complete circle of rays, all touching an invisible ring around the pole.

One column ⇒ one ring. All the columns ⇒ a family of rings, one inside the other. Concentric mosaics. Your friend's view is then just: for each of their rays, work out which ring it touches, and read the pixel off that ring.

Which rays do you have? A ray touching the ring of radius $r$ exists in your footage only if $r=R\sin\beta$ for some real pixel column $|\beta|\le\beta_{\max}$. So you own every ray with $|r|\le R\sin\beta_{\max}$ — and any virtual camera whose centre lies inside that disc only ever asks for rays with $|r|\le\|\mathbf P\|$. That is exactly why the exam figure puts the virtual camera inside the circle.

2 · The one formula, in four lines

Set-up (top view, everything in 2-D)
  • Rotation axis at the origin; arm length $R$; the real camera centre at time $\theta$ is $\mathbf C(\theta)=R(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$.
  • The camera looks radially outward, so its optical axis direction is $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$.
  • A pixel column is labelled by the angle $\beta$ it makes with the optical axis, $|\beta|\le\beta_{\max}$ (for a pinhole, column $u=f\tan\beta$).
  • Any 2-D ray is labelled by two numbers: its direction $\psi$, and its signed distance from the origin $r$. For a ray through point $\mathbf X$ in direction $(\cos\psi,\sin\psi)$: $\,r=X_x\sin\psi-X_y\cos\psi$.

Now compute $r$ for the ray of pixel $(\theta,\beta)$. Its direction is $\psi=\theta+\beta$, and it passes through $\mathbf C(\theta)$:

$$ r = R\cos\theta\,\sin(\theta+\beta) - R\sin\theta\,\cos(\theta+\beta) = R\,\sin\big((\theta+\beta)-\theta\big) = \boxed{\,R\sin\beta\,} $$
Read what just happened $\theta$ cancelled. The distance of a ray from the axis depends only on which column of the sensor it came from — not on when it was taken. So column $\beta$, gathered over the whole rotation, is a complete $360°$ panorama of rays all tangent to the circle of radius $R\sin\beta$: $$\mathrm{CM}_{R\sin\beta}(\psi) \;=\; \text{frame}(\psi-\beta)\,[\,\text{column }\beta\,]$$ That rebinning of the video into rings is the data structure. It is a 3-D light field $(\theta,\beta,v)$, not the full 4-D one — which is why one spin is enough.

3 · The rendering algorithm

  1. Pre-process (once). Rebin the video: for every column $\beta$, stack that column from all frames into the concentric mosaic $\mathrm{CM}_{R\sin\beta}(\psi)=\text{frame}(\psi-\beta)[\beta]$. This is a pure re-indexing — no pixel value is changed. (You can skip it and index the raw video directly; the mosaics just make the lookup cache-friendly.)
  2. Which ray does this virtual pixel want? Virtual camera at $\mathbf P$ with its own pose; its pixel at column angle $\phi$ shoots a ray of direction $\psi$ (viewing direction $+\,\phi$) through $\mathbf P$. Its distance from the axis is $r = P_x\sin\psi - P_y\cos\psi$.
  3. Do I own that ray? Yes iff $|r| \le R\sin\beta_{\max}$. Since $|r|\le\|\mathbf P\|$ always, every ray of every virtual camera with $\|\mathbf P\|\le R\sin\beta_{\max}$ is available. Otherwise leave it black or extrapolate.
  4. Where is it stored? Invert $r=R\sin\beta$: $\;\boxed{\beta=\arcsin(r/R)}$, then $\;\boxed{\theta=\psi-\beta}$.
  5. Fetch. Output pixel $=$ frame $\theta$, column $\beta$, at the appropriate row $v$. Bilinear interpolation in $(\theta,\beta)$ — i.e. blend the two nearest frames and the two nearest columns — otherwise you see angular aliasing (vertical banding) when the frame count is low.

Demo 2 — route every ray of a virtual camera

drag the virtual camera

Drag the purple virtual camera anywhere inside the circle. Each of its rays is traced back to the real camera position $\theta=\psi-\beta$ that recorded it (teal dots on the circle). The teal disc is the guaranteed-renderable region $\|\mathbf P\|\le R\sin\beta_{\max}$; step outside it and rays start turning red = never filmed.

The honest caveat the examiner wants to hear This is exact only for a scene at infinity, or when the rays are sampled densely enough. In reality the rotation is sampled at a finite number of frames, so a ray you need falls between two captured rays and you interpolate. The error of that interpolation grows for nearby objects (depth-dependent aliasing) and shows up as ghosting/vertical stretching. Standard remedies: film more frames, or apply a depth correction using a constant/estimated depth to reproject before blending.

4 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

Full-marks version — one page

Idea. Do not reconstruct geometry. Treat the video as a sampled light field and render the novel view by ray lookup: for every pixel of the virtual camera, identify the ray it needs and find which frame/column of the recorded video contains that same ray (image-based rendering — concentric mosaics, Shum & He).

Parametrisation. Top view, axis at the origin. Camera centre $\mathbf C(\theta)=R(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$, optical axis radially outward, pixel column at angle $\beta$ from the axis with $|\beta|\le\beta_{\max}$. A 2-D ray is labelled by direction $\psi$ and signed distance from the axis $r=X_x\sin\psi-X_y\cos\psi$.

Key property. The ray of pixel $(\theta,\beta)$ has $\psi=\theta+\beta$ and

$$ r=R\cos\theta\sin(\theta+\beta)-R\sin\theta\cos(\theta+\beta)=R\sin\beta $$

independent of $\theta$. Hence for a fixed column $\beta$, sweeping $\theta$ over $360°$ yields a full panorama of rays tangent to the circle of radius $R\sin\beta$ — a concentric mosaic $\mathrm{CM}_{R\sin\beta}(\psi)=\text{frame}(\psi-\beta)[\beta]$. Rebinning the video by column gives a family of such mosaics, i.e. a 3-D light field $(\theta,\beta,v)$.

Rendering algorithm. For a virtual camera at $\mathbf P$ inside the circle, for each of its pixels:

1. compute the ray direction $\psi$ and $r=P_x\sin\psi-P_y\cos\psi$;
2. if $|r|>R\sin\beta_{\max}$ the ray was never captured → leave black / extrapolate;
3. otherwise $\beta=\arcsin(r/R)$ and $\theta=\psi-\beta$;
4. output the pixel of frame $\theta$ at column $\beta$ and the corresponding row, bilinearly interpolated in $(\theta,\beta)$.

Coverage. $|r|\le\|\mathbf P\|$ for any ray through $\mathbf P$, so all rays are available whenever $\|\mathbf P\|\le R\sin\beta_{\max}$ — this is exactly why the virtual camera in the figure is drawn inside the circle. Cost is $O(1)$ per output pixel (one $\arcsin$ and one subtraction), independent of scene complexity, with no depth estimation and no correspondence.

Limitations. Exact for a scene at infinity; with finite frame count nearby objects suffer depth-dependent aliasing (ghosting, vertical distortion), fixed by denser sampling or a constant/estimated depth correction before interpolation. Vertical parallax is not captured (the arm sweeps a horizontal circle only), so vertical motion of the virtual camera is not supported, and the scene must be static during the spin, since $\theta$ is also time.

5 · Exam drill

Q. Prove $r=R\sin\beta$ without trigonometric identities.

Draw the right triangle: origin $O$, camera centre $C$ (with $|OC|=R$), and the foot $F$ of the perpendicular from $O$ onto the ray. The angle at $C$ between $CO$ and the ray is exactly $\beta$, because the optical axis points radially outward along $OC$ extended and the ray makes angle $\beta$ with it. In the right triangle $OFC$, $|OF| = |OC|\sin(\angle OCF) = R\sin\beta$. It is opposite-over-hypotenuse — nothing more.

Q. Why can you not put the virtual camera outside the circle?

A ray through a point $\mathbf P$ can have any distance from the axis up to $\|\mathbf P\|$ — in particular a virtual camera at distance $d>R\sin\beta_{\max}$ will look at directions whose rays pass further from the axis than anything the arm ever captured (they simply never entered the real lens). Those pixels have no data. You can extrapolate or fall back to the nearest available ray, but it is no longer exact.

Q. Why $\theta=\psi-\beta$ and not $\psi+\beta$?

Because $\psi=\theta+\beta$ by construction: the ray direction is the camera heading plus the pixel's offset from the optical axis. Inverting gives $\theta=\psi-\beta$. Also note $\arcsin$ returns $\beta\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$, which selects the intersection of the ray with the circle ahead of the virtual point — the other algebraic solution $\pi-\beta$ would be a camera looking backwards along its own arm, which never occurs.

Q. What is the role of the rows (vertical direction)?

The construction is purely horizontal: the arm sweeps a circle in one plane, so the light field is 3-D, $(\theta,\beta,v)$, where $v$ is the row. A virtual ray at height $v$ is fetched from the same row. That means no vertical parallax: you cannot move the virtual camera up or down, and objects at very different heights are rendered with the horizontal parallax of their row only. This is the price of using a single rotating arm instead of a full 4-D light-field capture.

Q. How many frames do you need?

By the light-field sampling argument, enough that the angular gap between consecutive captured rays keeps the disparity of the nearest scene point below about one pixel. Roughly, with nearest depth $Z_{\min}$, focal $f$, and $N$ frames over $360°$, you need $\;\frac{2\pi R}{N}\cdot\frac{f}{Z_{\min}}\lesssim 1$ pixel, i.e. $N \gtrsim 2\pi R f/Z_{\min}$. Fewer frames ⇒ visible vertical banding and ghosting on near objects; that is exactly the aliasing that depth correction buys you back.
