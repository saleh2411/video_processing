Geodesic Distance — explained like you're five 🐜

Every demo on this page is live. Click, drag the sliders, watch what changes. By the end you will know exactly what the exam is asking when it says "geodesic distance", "distance map", "$W(D)=1/D^b$", "trimap" — and why.

1The whole idea in one story

🍼 Baby version

Imagine a tiny ant 🐜 standing on a photograph.

The ant wants to walk to a candy on the other side. But the ant has a rule:

"Walking on the same colour is easy. Stepping over a colour change is like climbing a wall."

So the ant's "how far is it?" is not how far your ruler says. It's how tired the ant gets. That tiredness is called the geodesic distance.

Two points can be 5 cm apart on paper but "very far" for the ant, because a black line sits between them. And two points 30 cm apart can be "very close", because the whole road between them is one flat grey.

🎓 Grown-up version

Geodesic distance = shortest path length, where "length" is measured with a metric that the image itself defines. Flat regions are cheap, edges are expensive. Hence: distance follows image structure, not the page.

2What is a "wall" inside a picture?

🍼 Baby version

A wall is where the picture suddenly changes brightness.

Inside the apple: red, red, red, red — no wall. At the apple's rim: red → white — WALL!

🎓 Grown-up version

The wall detector is the gradient $\nabla Y$ of the luminance channel:

$$\nabla Y=\Big(\tfrac{\partial Y}{\partial x},\ \tfrac{\partial Y}{\partial y}\Big),\qquad \|\nabla Y\|\ \text{large}\iff\text{edge}$$

Two facts to memorise:

  • $\nabla Y$ points across the edge (from dark side to bright side).
  • Inside a flat region $\nabla Y\approx 0$ → walking there is free.

🍼 The whole formula in baby words

"Walk from A to B. At every tiny step, ask: did I just step across a colour change? If yes, pay. Add up all the payments. Now try every possible road and keep the cheapest bill."

⚠ Exam trap #1

$L(p)$ is not a geometric length. The notes say it explicitly. A path that wanders 500 pixels through one flat grey region has $L(p)=0$. Length in metres ≠ length in this metric.

⚠ Exam trap #2 — why the dot product, not just $\|\nabla Y\|$?

$\langle \dot p,\nabla Y\rangle$ only charges you for the part of your step that goes across the edge. Walking along the edge (tangent direction) is perpendicular to $\nabla Y$ → dot product $=0$ → free. That is exactly what we want: you may travel along an object's rim for free, but you pay to jump off it.

4The practical metric with $\gamma$ (the one you compute with)

The pure formula above lets you walk forever for free inside a flat region. In practice we mix real walking and edge pain, with a knob $\gamma$:

$$L_\gamma(p)=\int_p \sqrt{\ \underbrace{1}_{\text{cost of moving}}\ +\ \gamma^2\, \underbrace{\langle \dot p(s),\nabla Y\rangle^2}_{\text{cost of crossing edges}}}\ \ ds$$
$\gamma$What the metric becomesBehaviour
$\gamma=0$$L=\int ds$ = plain arc lengthEuclidean. Distance rings are perfect circles. Image ignored.
small $\gamma$mostly spatial, slight edge awarenessRings bend a little near objects; colour leaks across weak edges.
large $\gamma$mostly image-drivenRings stop dead at object boundaries. Texture "prints through". Fragile to noise.
🍼 Baby version of $\gamma$

$\gamma$ is "how tall are the walls?"   $\gamma=0$: no walls at all, the world is an empty field. Big $\gamma$: walls up to the sky.

5🔬 The big playground — the distance map $D_k(x)$

🎓 Definitions you must be able to write
$$d_L(x,\Omega_k)=\min_{y\in\Omega_k} d_L(x,y)\qquad\text{(point-to-set distance)}$$
$$\boxed{\,D_k(x)=d_L(x,\Omega_k)\,}\qquad\text{(the distance map: one number per pixel)}$$

$D_k(x)=0$ on the scribble itself, and grows as you move away through the image.

  • Set $\gamma=0$: the rings are perfect circles — the picture is invisible to the distance.

⚠ Exam trap #3

Iso-contours of a geodesic distance map are not circles and are not necessarily connected-looking around obstacles — they are wavefronts. This is literally the Eikonal equation $\|\nabla D\|=1/\text{speed}$ being solved. "Fast marching / fast sweeping" solve exactly this.

6Use #1 — Colorization (Ch. 9)

🍼 Baby version

You have a grey photo. You scribble red on the apple and blue on the sky. Every other pixel asks: "which scribble is closest — for an ant?" and takes mostly that colour.

🎓 The blending formula
$$c(x)=\frac{\sum_k W\!\big(D_k(x)\big)\,c_k}{\sum_k W\!\big(D_k(x)\big)}, \qquad W(D)=\frac{1}{D^{\,b}},\quad b\in[1,6]$$

It is a convex combination (weights are positive and sum to 1) — so the result always lands inside the set of scribble colours. Never a colour nobody scribbled.

Why $1/D^b$? Because we need $\dfrac{W(D+\varepsilon)}{W(D)}\to 0$ behaviour at the right places: a far scribble's vote must die out. Bigger $b$ = faster death = sharper boundary. Smaller $b$ = smoother, mushier transition.

  • $\gamma=0$ → colours ignore the objects completely (just nearest-scribble Voronoi cells). This is exactly why the course uses geodesic and not Euclidean.

7Use #2 — Matting / trimap (Ch. 10)

🍼 Baby version

Two scribbles now: one means "this is the thing I want" (FG), one means "this is junk" (BG). Every pixel asks the ant: "am I closer to the thing, or to the junk?" If it's a close call, we mark it "don't know" and deal with it later.

🎓 Exactly as in the notes
$$V_F=\{x:D_F(x)D_B(x)\},\qquad \Delta=\{x:D_F(x)=D_B(x)\}$$
$$B_\rho(\Delta)=\bigcup_{x\in\Delta}B_\rho(x)\;\Longleftrightarrow\; x\in B_\rho(\Delta)\iff \exists y\in\Delta:\ \|x-y\|\le\rho$$
$$\alpha_T(x)=\begin{cases} 1 & x\in V_F\setminus B_\rho(\Delta)\\ 0 & x\in V_B\setminus B_\rho(\Delta)\\ \varphi & x\in B_\rho(\Delta)\ \text{(unknown)}\end{cases}$$

Note: here the underlying field driving the geodesic distance is not luminance — it is the posterior $P(F|c)$ from the KDE step. Same machinery, different "image".

⚠ Exam trap #4 — why is $\Delta$ "usually empty"?

Because $D_F(x)=D_B(x)$ exactly is a measure-zero event on a discrete grid — floating point ties essentially never happen. So in practice you find $\Delta$ by interpolation (sign change of $D_F-D_B$ between neighbouring pixels), not by testing equality. Say this sentence and you get the point.

⚠ Exam trap #5 — choosing $\rho$

Too small → band is 1 px wide, the opacity map cannot represent soft hair/motion blur. Too large → most of the image is "unknown" and you gave the hard problem back to yourself. Typical $\rho\approx 5\!-\!15$ px.

8How is $D_k$ actually computed? Bellman → Dijkstra

🍼 Baby version of Bellman

If the cheapest road from your house to school goes past the bakery, then the piece house → bakery must itself be the cheapest way to the bakery. Otherwise you'd swap it for a cheaper one and get a cheaper total — which contradicts "cheapest".

🎓 Bellman's principle of optimality

If $P^*$ is optimal $x\to y$ and $z\in P^*$, then $P^*_{xz}$ and $P^*_{zy}$ are optimal too. Proof by contradiction:

Consequence — the local recursion that Dijkstra implements:

$$D(x)=\min_{z\in N_1(x)}\big[\,L(x,z)+D(z)\,\big]$$

Without Bellman you'd have to enumerate exponentially many paths. With it: $O(N\log N)$.

⚠ Exam trap #6

Dijkstra requires non-negative edge weights. Our weights are $\sqrt{\cdot}$ or $|\cdot|$ — always $\ge0$. Fine. Also: with 4-neighbours you get "Manhattan-flavoured" artefacts; 8-neighbours with $\sqrt2$ diagonal weights is the usual fix (and still not perfectly isotropic — that's what fast marching solves).

The 6 sentences that get you the marks

  1. Geodesic distance = shortest-path distance under an image-defined metric; edges are expensive, flat regions cheap.
  2. $L(p)=\int|\langle\dot p,\nabla Y\rangle|dl$ is not a geometric length — the dot product charges only for crossing edges, so travelling along an edge is free.
  3. $\gamma=0$ reduces the metric to Euclidean; large $\gamma$ makes it purely image-driven.
  4. The distance map $D_k(x)=d_L(x,\Omega_k)$ is computed by dynamic programming (Bellman) → Dijkstra $O(N\log N)$, or fast sweeping $O(N)$ for real time.
  5. Colorization blends scribble colours with $W(D)=1/D^b$ — a convex combination; $b$ controls smoothness.
  6. Matting classifies by $D_F\lessgtr D_B$, and marks a band of radius $\rho$ around the tie set $\Delta$ as unknown $\varphi$.

10Test yourself (click to reveal)

Q1. Two pixels are 3 px apart but on opposite sides of a strong edge. Is their geodesic distance small or large?
Large. Euclidean is 3 px, but any path must cross the edge and pay $\gamma|\langle\dot p,\nabla Y\rangle|$. That is the whole point: geodesic separates objects that are spatially adjacent.
Q2. What happens to the colorization result if you set $\gamma=0$?
The metric becomes Euclidean, so $D_k$ are plain distance transforms and the blend becomes a soft Voronoi diagram of the scribbles. Colour bleeds straight across every object boundary. Useless for colorization — this is the motivation for the geodesic metric.
Q3. Why $W(D)=1/D^b$ and not $W(D)=e^{-D}$?
Both are valid decaying weights and the notes say "for example, an exponentially decaying function". The paper picked $1/D^b$, $b\in[1,6]$. What matters for the exam: $W$ must be positive and decreasing, must satisfy $W(D+\varepsilon)/W(D)\to0$ as $D\to\infty$ so far scribbles stop voting, and its decay rate controls transition smoothness. Also $1/D^b$ blows up at $D\to0$, which pins the colour exactly on the scribble ($c(x)\to c_k$) — a nice built-in hard constraint.
Q4. Increase $b$ from 1 to 6 — what changes visually?
Transitions get sharper. At $b=6$ the closest scribble dominates almost completely (near-nearest-neighbour labelling); at $b=1$ you get long, soft gradients between scribble colours.
Q5. State Bellman's principle and why we need it here.
Any sub-path of an optimal path is optimal. Proof by contradiction (swap the sub-path for a cheaper one → cheaper total → contradiction). It gives the recursion $D(x)=\min_{z\in N_1(x)}[L(x,z)+D(z)]$, turning an exponential path enumeration into a polynomial DP — Dijkstra / fast sweeping.
Q6. In matting, which field is the geodesic distance computed over?
Not the raw image — over the foreground posterior $P(F|c)$ obtained from KDE + Bayes. Same machinery as colorization, different underlying "image". This lets spatial proximity break ties when FG and BG colours overlap (brown jacket on brown sofa).
Q7. Why is the set $\Delta=\{D_F=D_B\}$ usually empty, and what do we do?
Exact equality of two floats on a discrete grid is a measure-zero event. We locate $\Delta$ by interpolation — find neighbouring pixels where $D_F-D_B$ changes sign, then place the boundary between them (sub-pixel by linear interpolation).
Q8. Give the complexity of Dijkstra vs fast sweeping and when you'd choose each.
Dijkstra $O(N\log N)$ with a heap, exact for the graph metric, simple. Fast sweeping $O(N)$ with a small constant, done as a handful of raster sweeps in alternating directions, cache-friendly and parallelisable — pick it for video / real-time.
Q9. Your colorized region "leaks" through a boundary. Name three fixes.
(1) Raise $\gamma$ so the boundary costs more. (2) Raise $b$ so distant scribbles stop voting. (3) Add a scribble inside the leaking region — geodesic distance grows slowly inside a large homogeneous region, so big regions need their own seed. (Also: pre-smooth $Y$ to strengthen real edges vs. noise.)
Q10. Is $d_L$ a true metric?
With the $\gamma$-form ($\sqrt{1+\gamma^2\langle\cdot\rangle^2}$) yes: non-negative, symmetric, triangle inequality holds (it's a path-infimum), and $d_L(x,y)=0\iff x=y$ since the integrand is $\ge1$. With the pure $|\langle\dot p,\nabla Y\rangle|$ form it is only a pseudo-metric: distinct points inside one flat region have distance 0.
