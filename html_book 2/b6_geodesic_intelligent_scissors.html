Companion page to Chapters 9 & 10 · Moed B 2022, question 6 · 12 points

Same machinery as colorization — with the sign flipped

Two clicks and a curve appears, glued to the brim of the hat. This is Intelligent Scissors (Mortensen & Barrett), and it is a shortest path on the pixel graph. The trap: the geodesic metric you memorised for colorization and matting makes crossing an edge expensive. Here you want to travel along an edge, so the cost must be cheap where the gradient is strong. Get that sign right and the rest is Dijkstra.

Moed B · 1/8/2022 · Question 6 · 12 points · GEODESIC DISTANCE

Consider Figure 3. The user clicks on points $A$ and $B$ and the system automatically draws the black path between them that follows the edge of Lena's hat. Suggest a way to compute the path between these two points, as shown in the figure.

1 · The story

The kid, the field, and the footpath

You are standing at point A in a big field and your friend is at point B. You want to walk over.

The shortest way is a straight line. But suppose the field is muddy everywhere except along a nice hard footpath that curves across it. Then the fastest way is not the straight line — it is: step onto the footpath, run along it, step off at the end. Even though that is longer in metres, it is cheaper in effort.

Now look at the picture of Lena. The brim of the hat is a place where the picture suddenly changes from bright hat to dark background. That sudden change is an edge. And an edge is our footpath: we will simply declare that walking on an edge is cheap and walking anywhere else is expensive.

Then we ask the computer the simplest question in the world: what is the cheapest way from A to B? The computer does not know what a hat is. It just adds up costs. But because we made the edge cheap, the cheapest route is the brim of the hat, and the curve appears as if by magic.

Now the part that trips everyone up. In the colorization chapter we used almost this exact formula — but there we wanted the opposite! There, colour had to stay inside an object, so crossing an edge had to be expensive. Here we want to ride the edge, so being on an edge must be cheap. Same graph, same Dijkstra, opposite sign. Whether you flip that sign is the entire question.

The whole answer, in one sentence Build a graph whose nodes are pixels and whose 8-connected edge weights are small where the gradient magnitude is large (plus a term that keeps the path aligned with the edge direction), then run Dijkstra from $A$; the minimum-cost path to $B$ is the drawn curve. Because Dijkstra gives you the cost to every pixel in one run, the path to a moving cursor is free — which is what makes the tool feel interactive ("live wire").

2 · The material this sits on, and the sign flip

The geodesic distance from Chapters 9 & 10

The course defines the geodesic distance between two points as the infimum, over all paths, of an integrated cost:

$$ d(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}) \;=\; \inf_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}} \int_0^{l(\Gamma)} W\big(\Gamma(s)\big)\, ds \tag{1} $$

Everything is in the choice of the local cost $W$ — the "how tired do I get per step here" function. Two opposite choices appear in this course:

TaskLocal cost $W$Effect
Colorization / matting (Moed A Q6) $\sqrt{1+\gamma^2(\nabla I\cdot\Gamma')^2}$ — increases with the gradient Crossing an edge is expensive ⇒ distance stays inside a region ⇒ good for spreading colour/opacity without leaking.
This question — boundary tracing decreases with the gradient, e.g. $\;W = 1-\dfrac{|\nabla I|}{\max|\nabla I|}\;$ or $\;W=\exp(-|\nabla I|^2/2\sigma^2)$ or $\;W=\dfrac{1}{1+|\nabla I|}$ Being on an edge is cheap ⇒ the shortest path runs along the boundary ⇒ good for segmentation by contour.

Both are the same equation (1) and both are computed by the same algorithm. Only $W$ differs — and it differs by a sign. Saying this explicitly is worth marks, because it shows you understand that "geodesic distance" is a framework, not a formula.

The classical cost — Intelligent Scissors / Live Wire

Mortensen & Barrett's local cost on the directed link from pixel $p$ to a neighbour $q$ is a weighted sum of three features:

$$ l(p,q) \;=\; \omega_Z\, f_Z(q) \;+\; \omega_G\, f_G(q) \;+\; \omega_D\, f_D(p,q) \tag{2} $$
  1. Gradient magnitude — the main term, the one that makes edges cheap:
    $$ f_G(q) \;=\; 1 - \frac{|\nabla I(q)|}{\max_r |\nabla I(r)|} \;\in[0,1] $$
    $f_G=0$ on the strongest edge in the image, $f_G=1$ in a flat region. For diagonal links the cost is scaled by $1/\sqrt2$ (equivalently, multiply the whole local cost by the step length) so that $8$-connected paths are not artificially cheap.
  2. Laplacian zero-crossing — sub-pixel edge localisation: $f_Z(q) = 0$ if a zero-crossing of $\nabla^2 I$ passes through $q$, else $1$. This is a binary snap: it costs nothing to sit exactly on the edge and a fixed penalty to sit one pixel beside it, which stops the path wobbling across the boundary.
  3. Gradient direction — smoothness, and the term that keeps you travelling along rather than across. With the unit edge direction $\mathbf{D}(p) = \big(I_y(p), -I_x(p)\big)/|\nabla I(p)|$ (perpendicular to the gradient) and the link direction $\mathbf{L}(p,q)$ oriented so that $\mathbf{D}(p)\cdot\mathbf{L}\ge0$:
    $$ f_D(p,q) \;=\; \tfrac{2}{3\pi}\Big[\arccos\big(\mathbf{D}(p)\!\cdot\!\mathbf{L}(p,q)\big) + \arccos\big(\mathbf{L}(p,q)\!\cdot\!\mathbf{D}(q)\big)\Big] $$
    It is $0$ when the step is parallel to the local edge and grows as the step turns away, so the path is penalised for cutting across a boundary or making sharp turns.
Why $f_D$ matters more than it looks Without it, any bright cluster of gradient pixels is cheap to enter, and the path will happily zig-zag through a textured region, hopping between unrelated strong-gradient pixels. $f_D$ asks the step to be tangent to the local edge at both ends, which is what turns "visit cheap pixels" into "follow a contour". It is the discrete analogue of the tangency condition $\Gamma' \perp \nabla I$ that made a path free along an isophote in the colorization metric — the same geometric quantity $\nabla I\cdot\Gamma'$, used for the opposite purpose.
The algorithm
  1. Smooth $I$ (Gaussian $\sigma$) and compute $I_x, I_y, |\nabla I|, \nabla^2 I$ once.
  2. Build the 8-connected graph with weights (2). $|V| = N$ pixels, $|E| = 8N$.
  3. Dijkstra from $A$ with a binary heap / priority queue: $O(N\log N)$. Store a back-pointer per pixel.
  4. Follow back-pointers from $B$ to $A$ — that is the path. $O(\text{path length})$.

Correctness needs $l(p,q)\ge0$, which (2) satisfies by construction. Dijkstra is therefore exact for this graph; no dynamic-programming subtleties, no local minima (unlike snakes).

4 · Practical points that make it usable

Refinements from the paper — mention two or three
FeatureWhat it does
Live wireRun Dijkstra once from the last clicked seed. Then the path to any cursor position is just a back-pointer walk — $O(\text{length})$ — so the wire snaps around interactively at every mouse move. This is the single most important implementation observation, and it comes free from the fact that Dijkstra is a single-source, all-destinations algorithm.
Path coolingIf the wire stays stable over a stretch as the mouse moves, freeze that stretch and plant a new seed automatically. The user gets long boundaries with very few clicks.
On-the-fly trainingSample the gradient magnitude/orientation along the segment the user has already accepted, build a histogram, and re-weight the cost to favour edges that look like the one being traced. This is what lets the wire ignore a strong but irrelevant edge crossing the one you want.
Multi-scale / seeded pyramidCompute the cost map on a coarse level to get the path roughly, refine near it. Cuts the $O(N\log N)$ down when the image is large.
A\*Since $B$ is known, replace Dijkstra by A\* with the admissible heuristic $h(p) = W_{\min}\|p-B\|$ ($W_{\min}$ = the smallest possible local cost). Same optimal path, far fewer expansions. Trade-off: you lose the live-wire property, because A\* only solves for one target.
Continuous alternativeInstead of a graph, solve the Eikonal equation $|\nabla U(\mathbf{x})| = W(\mathbf{x})$, $U(A)=0$ by fast marching (or fast sweeping, as in Chapter 9), then trace the path by gradient descent on $U$ from $B$. Same complexity class, sub-pixel accurate, no 8-connectivity metrication artefacts — but more code.
What you would say about alternatives

Active contours (snakes) minimise a similar energy but by local gradient descent from an initial curve, so they need a good initialisation, can get stuck in local minima, and cannot guarantee they pass through $A$ and $B$. Dijkstra on the pixel graph is globally optimal for the stated cost, and needs only the two clicks. Level sets / geodesic active contours handle topology changes but again solve a PDE to convergence rather than returning the exact minimiser between two prescribed points. For "user clicks two points, draw the boundary between them", the shortest-path formulation is the right tool, which is why it is the one that shipped in Photoshop as the Magnetic Lasso.

5 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

One page, as the exam asks

Formulation. Treat the image as a weighted graph $G=(V,E)$: $V$ = pixels, $E$ = 8-connected neighbour links, and assign each link a non-negative cost that is low on edges. The drawn curve is then the minimum-cost path from $A$ to $B$ — a geodesic under a metric induced by the image, $d(A,B)=\inf_\Gamma\int W(\Gamma(s))ds$ with a cost $W$ that decreases with $|\nabla I|$.

The cost (Intelligent Scissors / Live Wire, Mortensen & Barrett). Pre-smooth $I$, compute $I_x,I_y,|\nabla I|,\nabla^2 I$, then set

$$ l(p,q) = \omega_G\underbrace{\Big(1-\tfrac{|\nabla I(q)|}{\max|\nabla I|}\Big)}_{f_G:\ \text{strong edge} \Rightarrow \text{cheap}} + \omega_Z\underbrace{f_Z(q)}_{\substack{0 \text{ on a Laplacian}\\ \text{zero-crossing}}} + \omega_D\underbrace{\tfrac{2}{3\pi}\big[\arccos(\mathbf{D}_p\!\cdot\!\mathbf{L}) + \arccos(\mathbf{L}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{D}_q)\big]}_{f_D:\ \text{step must be tangent to the edge}} $$

with $\mathbf{D}(p)=(I_y,-I_x)/|\nabla I|$ the unit edge direction and $\mathbf{L}(p,q)$ the (orientation-corrected) unit link direction; scale the cost by the step length ($1$ or $\sqrt2$) so diagonal links are not free. $f_G$ makes the boundary a cheap corridor, $f_Z$ localises it to one pixel, $f_D$ forces the path to travel along the boundary rather than hop across strong-gradient noise.

Algorithm. All weights are $\ge 0$, so run Dijkstra from $A$ with a binary heap, $O(N\log N)$ for $N$ pixels, storing a back-pointer per pixel; then walk back-pointers from $B$ to $A$ to obtain the path. Since Dijkstra computes the cost to every pixel, the path from $A$ to any cursor position is available instantly — this is the "live wire" that makes the tool feel interactive. If only the fixed $B$ is needed, A\* with $h(p)=W_{\min}\|p-B\|$ expands far fewer nodes.

Key point — the sign. This is the same geodesic-distance machinery as colorization and matting, but with the opposite metric. There, $W=\sqrt{1+\gamma^2(\nabla I\cdot\Gamma')^2}$ grows with the gradient so that distance does not leak across boundaries; here $W$ must shrink with the gradient so that the cheapest route is the boundary. Same graph, same Dijkstra, inverted cost.

Refinements. Path cooling (auto-seed the stable part of the wire), on-the-fly training (re-weight from the gradient statistics of the already-accepted segment, so the wire ignores irrelevant crossing edges), and a continuous variant: solve the Eikonal equation $|\nabla U|=W,\ U(A)=0$ by fast marching and back-trace by gradient descent on $U$ for sub-pixel accuracy. Compared with snakes, this is globally optimal and needs no initial curve.

6 · Exam drill

Q. Why is Dijkstra valid here — what would break it?

Dijkstra requires non-negative edge weights, which (2) guarantees since each $f\in[0,1]$ and each $\omega\ge0$. That is all it needs; the graph may have cycles and any structure. What would break it is a cost that can be negative — e.g. if you tried to express "reward" for being on an edge as a negative weight instead of a small positive one, Dijkstra's greedy invariant fails and you would need Bellman–Ford ($O(NE)$) or, if a negative cycle exists, the problem becomes ill-posed (an infinitely cheap path that loops on the edge forever). Always encode preference as small positive, never as negative.

Q. Why is a pure "$1/|\nabla I|$" cost a bad idea in practice?

Two reasons. It is unbounded as $|\nabla I|\to0$, so flat regions get astronomically expensive and the algorithm's dynamic range explodes (numerically, and in the heap's key ordering). And it is scale-dependent: doubling the image contrast halves every cost, so the same $\omega$ weights behave completely differently on a different image. Use $1-|\nabla I|/\max|\nabla I|$ (bounded in $[0,1]$, contrast-normalised, which is what the paper does) or $\exp(-|\nabla I|^2/2\sigma^2)$ with $\sigma$ set from the gradient statistics. Also add a small floor $\epsilon>0$ so the strongest edge still costs something — otherwise a zero-cost path can wander along the edge for free and the shortest path is no longer unique or well behaved.

Q. What happens if the hat's edge is broken — a gap where the contrast vanishes?

Nothing catastrophic, and this is the method's best property. The path pays full price to cross the gap, but the gap is short, so the total cost of "run along the edge, pay for the gap, continue along the edge" is still far below any alternative. Shortest paths are a global criterion, so they bridge local failures automatically — unlike an edge-following/tracing algorithm that would simply stop at the gap. This is exactly why the technique is robust on real images, where boundaries are almost never continuous.

Q. The path is 8-connected. Doesn't that distort distances?

Yes — this is metrication error. On an 8-connected grid the reachable directions are quantised to multiples of $45°$, so a "straight" path in a direction of, say, $20°$ must be rendered as a staircase, and its measured length is up to $\approx8\%$ too long ($\sec$ of the angle error). Fixes, in increasing order of effort: multiply diagonal links by $\sqrt2$ (essential, and cheap); use a $16$- or $32$-neighbourhood, which shrinks the worst-case error; or abandon the graph entirely and solve the Eikonal equation with fast marching, which is consistent with the continuous metric by construction. For this application the error is cosmetic, because the path is pinned to a curve by $f_G$ anyway.

Q. How would you close the contour — get the whole hat, not just $A$ to $B$?

Let the user keep clicking: each click plants a new seed, Dijkstra is re-run from it, and the segments concatenate. To close the loop, the last segment runs back to the first seed. If you want it fully automatic from two clicks, compute the two disjoint shortest paths from $A$ to $B$ — run Dijkstra, take the best path, delete its interior nodes from the graph, run again — and join them into a closed curve. That is a standard trick, and the second path will typically go around the other side of the hat. Note the risk: without the deletion step you would get the same path twice.

Q. Relate this precisely to the colorization geodesic distance.

Both compute $\inf_\Gamma \int W\,ds$ and both discretise to a shortest path solved by Dijkstra/fast-marching. The colorization cost $W=\sqrt{1+\gamma^2(\nabla I\cdot\Gamma')^2}$ is anisotropic: it depends on the direction of travel through $\nabla I\cdot\Gamma'$, being free along an isophote and expensive across one. The scissors cost also depends on direction (through $f_D$) but with the preference reversed: it wants $\Gamma'$ tangent to the edge while sitting on the edge, whereas colorization wants $\Gamma'$ tangent to the isophote in order to avoid the edge. In one sentence: colorization measures distance within a region; scissors measures distance along a boundary. Chapter 9's machinery, one sign apart.

Q. Complexity, and what dominates in practice?

Pre-processing (smoothing + derivatives) is $O(N)$. Graph construction is $O(8N)$ and can be done lazily — compute a link cost when it is first relaxed, never store the graph. Dijkstra with a binary heap is $O(E\log V) = O(8N\log N)$; with a bucket/radix priority queue it is $O(N)$ amortised, which is what a real implementation uses since costs are quantised to a small integer range. Back-tracing is $O(\text{path length})$. In practice the wall-clock cost is dominated by the heap operations, and the reason live wire feels instant is that all of that happens once per click, not once per mouse move.
