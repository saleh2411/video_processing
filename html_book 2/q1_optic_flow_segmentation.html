Companion page to Chapter 2 · Moed A, question 1

Flow that knows where the objects are

Horn–Schunck blurs motion across object boundaries. You've just been handed a map of where the objects are. Use it. Story first, then a demo where you can watch the blur appear and disappear, then the math.

Moed A · 19/6/2022 · Question 1 · 13 points · OPTIC FLOW

Assume you have access to a semantic segmentation neural network that given an input image $I$ computes its corresponding semantic segmentation map $S$. Each pixel in $S$ holds a value in $\{0,\dots,c-1\}$ where $c$ is the number of classes (car, road, pedestrian, …). Observe that all instances of the same class receive the same label. Modify the Horn–Schunck algorithm to take as input two grayscale images $I_1$ and $I_2$ and their corresponding semantic segmentation maps $S_1$ and $S_2$, and produce the optic flow between them.

1 · The story

The crowd that holds hands

Imagine every pixel in the image is a person, and each person must guess which way they moved.

Most people are standing in a plain-coloured area — a blank wall, the sky, a patch of road. They look around, see nothing but sameness, and honestly cannot tell whether they moved left or right. They have no information.

So Horn–Schunck tells everybody: hold hands with your neighbours and try to agree. The people standing on a corner or an edge do know where they went, and by holding hands they drag the clueless ones along with them. That's how a blank wall gets a sensible motion: it's borrowed from the edges around it.

Beautiful. Except for one thing.

Somebody standing on the car is holding hands with somebody standing on the road. The car moves one way, the road moves another, and because they're holding hands they compromise — both end up with a smeared, wrong, in-between answer. The sharp edge of the car's motion turns into a blur.

So what does the segmentation map give you? A map of who should let go of whose hand. If two neighbouring pixels have different labels, there's a real object boundary between them, and their motions have every right to disagree. Cut that hand-hold. Everything else stays exactly as Horn–Schunck had it.

That's the whole answer. The rest of this page is making sure you can say why precisely, and defend it against the obvious follow-up questions.

Two experiments to actually run

1. Set α to 0. Both curves go to garbage, and the textureless zones become meaningless. This is the proof that the smoothness term is not optional — without it the data term alone is one equation with two unknowns per pixel (in 2-D), i.e. the aperture problem everywhere.

2. Set ε up to 1. The gated version becomes the plain version — because $w=1$ everywhere is exactly ordinary Horn–Schunck. Your modification is a strict generalisation: plain HS is the special case $w \equiv 1$. Saying that sentence in the exam is worth a point on its own.

3 · The mistake almost everyone makes

"If p and q are in the same segment, the flow is the same there, so the smoothness term is zero anyway"

No. $|\nabla u|^2$ is not a fact about the world — it is a price you charge. $u$ is the unknown you are solving for; nothing says it is constant inside a segment. The term exists to push neighbours together, and the data term pushes back. The solution sits where those two forces balance.

And the flow genuinely is not constant inside a segment:

  • All cars share one label — two different cars are one segment with two different motions.
  • The road is one segment; under forward camera motion its flow diverges radially, varying smoothly across it.
  • A pedestrian is one segment; arms and legs move differently from the torso.
  • Even a single rigid object rotating or moving in depth has spatially varying flow.

And if the term really were zero everywhere, the energy would collapse to the data term alone, giving $I_xu+I_yv+I_t=0$ — one equation, two unknowns, at every pixel. Unsolvable. The smoothness term is what makes the system invertible in the first place.

4 · Only now, the math

Start from the Chapter 2 energy

Standard Horn–Schunck:

$$ \min_{u,v} \iint \underbrace{(I_xu + I_yv + I_t)^2}_{\text{data term}} \;+\; \alpha\underbrace{\big(|\nabla u|^2 + |\nabla v|^2\big)}_{\text{smoothness prior}}\; dx\,dy $$

The modification is one symbol. Introduce a per-edge weight driven by the label map, and put it in front of the smoothness term only:

$$ w(p,q) = \begin{cases} 1 & S_1(p) = S_1(q) \\[2pt] \varepsilon \approx 0 & S_1(p) \neq S_1(q)\end{cases} $$
$$ \min_{u,v} \iint (I_xu + I_yv + I_t)^2 \;+\; \alpha\, w(\mathbf{x})\big(|\nabla u|^2 + |\nabla v|^2\big)\, dx\,dy $$

What that does to the Euler–Lagrange equations

Chapter 2 derived, for the unweighted case,

$$ (I_xu + I_yv + I_t)I_x - \alpha(u_{xx} + u_{yy}) = 0 $$

where $u_{xx}+u_{yy}$ is the Laplacian, implemented as the kernel with $4$ in the centre and $-1$ at each of the four neighbours. With the weights in place, that plain Laplacian becomes a weighted graph Laplacian: each neighbour contributes $-w(p,q)$ instead of $-1$, and the centre becomes $\sum_q w(p,q)$ instead of $4$:

$$ (L_w u)(p) \;=\; \sum_{q \in N(p)} w(p,q)\,\big(u(p) - u(q)\big) $$

An edge crossing a label boundary now has weight $\approx 0$, so it contributes nothing — flow no longer leaks across it, and a sharp motion discontinuity is free to appear exactly at the object silhouette. Everything else about Chapter 2 survives untouched: still a sparse linear system, still solved by the same iterative scheme, still $\alpha$ as the data-vs-prior dial.

The iterative update, if you want to write it

The standard Jacobi/Gauss–Seidel form of HS replaces the plain neighbourhood average $\bar u$ with the weighted average:

$$ \bar u_w(p) = \frac{\sum_q w(p,q)\,u(q)}{\sum_q w(p,q)}, \qquad u \leftarrow \bar u_w - \frac{I_x(I_x\bar u_w + I_y\bar v_w + I_t)}{\alpha + I_x^2 + I_y^2} $$

That is literally the only line of code that changes, and it is exactly what the demo above runs.

And what is $S_2$ for?

$S_1$ did all the work above. $S_2$ gives you an optional extra data term: after moving by $(u,v)$, a pixel should land on a pixel of the same class.

$$ \lambda \cdot \big[\, S_2(x+u,\, y+v) \neq S_1(x,y) \,\big] $$

Penalise sending a "car" pixel onto "road". Two honest caveats to state: it must be a soft penalty (all instances of a class share a label, so it cannot separate two adjacent cars), and the indicator is non-differentiable, so in practice you'd use a smooth surrogate — e.g. the distance transform of each class mask, or a soft class-probability map from the network instead of the hard $\arg\max$ label.

5 · The algorithm, written properly

  1. Derivatives. Compute $I_x, I_y$ from $I_1$ and $I_t = I_2 - I_1$, exactly as in plain HS.
  2. Edge weights from the labels. For every neighbouring pair $(p,q)$ set $w(p,q)=1$ if $S_1(p)=S_1(q)$, else $w(p,q)=\varepsilon\approx 0$. (One pass over the label map.)
  3. Minimise the gated energy $\iint (I_xu+I_yv+I_t)^2 + \alpha\,w\,(|\nabla u|^2+|\nabla v|^2)$, i.e. run the ordinary HS iterations with the plain neighbourhood average replaced by the $w$-weighted average.
  4. Optional label-consistency data term using $S_2$: add $\lambda[S_2(x+u,y+v) \neq S_1(x,y)]$, softened as described above.
  5. Optional but recommended: run it coarse-to-fine on an image pyramid, since traffic scenes have large displacements and the Taylor linearisation only holds for small motion.

6 · The traps

1 · All instances of a class share a label The question says this explicitly, which means it is being graded. Two cars side by side are one segment. Your $w$ will not cut between them, so their motion boundary still blurs. Say this limitation out loud, and note the fix: an instance segmentation map would separate them, or combine $w$ with the image gradient so strong intensity edges also lower the weight.
2 · Use $\varepsilon$, not exactly zero With $w\equiv 0$ across every boundary, the linear system decouples into independent blocks, one per connected segment. A small or textureless segment then has no neighbour to borrow flow from and can come out underdetermined. A tiny residual coupling keeps the global system connected and well-posed while still letting real motion boundaries stay sharp.
3 · Do not gate the data term The weight belongs on the smoothness term only. The data term is a per-pixel statement about brightness constancy; it has nothing to do with neighbours, so there is no hand to let go of.
4 · Nice extras, one line each

• Segmentation is not perfect. A mislabelled pixel cuts a hand-hold that should have stayed. Softening $w$ with the network's class confidence makes it degrade gracefully.

• Relation to known methods. This is exactly the idea behind edge-preserving / anisotropic-diffusion flow and robust smoothness terms (total variation); you're just getting the edge map from a network instead of from the image gradient.

• Sanity check. Setting $w\equiv 1$ recovers plain Horn–Schunck, so the method is a strict generalisation.

7 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

One page, in order

Diagnosis. Plain Horn–Schunck minimises $\iint (I_xu+I_yv+I_t)^2 + \alpha(|\nabla u|^2+|\nabla v|^2)$. Its smoothness prior is isotropic: it diffuses flow across every neighbouring pair, including pairs that straddle an object boundary, so motion discontinuities get blurred. The segmentation map tells us exactly where those boundaries are.

Modification. Define a per-edge weight from $S_1$: $w(p,q)=1$ if $S_1(p)=S_1(q)$, else $w(p,q)=\varepsilon\approx 0$, and minimise the segmentation-aware energy $\iint (I_xu+I_yv+I_t)^2 + \alpha\,w(\mathbf{x})(|\nabla u|^2+|\nabla v|^2)$.

Effect on the solution. The Euler–Lagrange equations are unchanged except that the plain Laplacian becomes a weighted graph Laplacian, $(L_wu)(p)=\sum_q w(p,q)(u(p)-u(q))$: each neighbour contributes $-w(p,q)$ instead of $-1$. In the iterative scheme, the neighbourhood average $\bar u$ is replaced by the $w$-weighted average. Edges crossing a label change contribute nothing, so flow no longer leaks across silhouettes and sharp motion discontinuities appear there, while inside a segment the prior still fills in flow at textureless pixels exactly as before.

Using $S_2$. Optionally add a soft label-consistency data term $\lambda[S_2(x+u,y+v)\neq S_1(x,y)]$, penalising flow that sends a pixel onto a different class. It must be soft (and smoothed, e.g. via a distance transform or soft class probabilities) because all instances of a class share a label.

Limitations. Two adjacent cars share a label, so their mutual boundary is not cut; use $\varepsilon$ rather than $0$ so segments stay weakly coupled and the system stays well-posed; $w\equiv 1$ recovers standard HS.

8 · Exam drill

Q. In one sentence, what is wrong with plain Horn–Schunck here?

Its smoothness prior is isotropic — it penalises flow differences equally between every pair of neighbours, including pairs on opposite sides of an object boundary, so real motion discontinuities get smoothed away.

Q. Why not just drop the smoothness term near boundaries — or everywhere?

Because without it the data term alone gives $I_xu+I_yv+I_t=0$: one equation, two unknowns per pixel. That is the aperture problem, and it is unsolvable pointwise. The prior is what makes the system invertible and what fills in flow at textureless pixels. You only want to remove the coupling across boundaries, not within regions.

Q. Where exactly does $w$ enter the equations?

Only on the smoothness term. In the Euler–Lagrange equations the plain Laplacian $u_{xx}+u_{yy}$ becomes the weighted graph Laplacian $\sum_q w(p,q)(u(p)-u(q))$; equivalently, in the iterative update the plain neighbourhood average is replaced by the $w$-weighted average. The data term is untouched.

Q. Why $\varepsilon$ instead of exactly $0$?

Hard zeros decouple the linear system into one independent block per segment. A small or textureless segment then has nothing to borrow flow from and may be underdetermined. A tiny residual weight keeps the system globally connected and well-posed while still being weak enough that boundaries stay sharp.

Q. Two cars are driving side by side and touching in the image. Does your method separate them?

No — the question states that all instances of a class share a label, so both cars are one segment and $w=1$ between them. Their mutual motion boundary still blurs. Fixes: use instance segmentation if available, or multiply $w$ by an image-gradient-based term so strong intensity edges also reduce the weight.

Q. What is $S_2$ good for, and why must its term be soft?

It supports a label-consistency data term: a pixel's flow should land it on a pixel of the same class, penalised by $\lambda[S_2(x+u,y+v)\neq S_1(x,y)]$. It must be soft because the label is shared across instances (landing on a different car is fine) and because a hard indicator is non-differentiable — in practice use a distance transform of the class masks or the network's soft class probabilities.

Q. Does this change the computational structure of the solver?

No. It is still a sparse linear system with the same sparsity pattern — only the coefficient values change (each off-diagonal entry is scaled by $w$, and the diagonal becomes $\sum_q w$). Same iterative solver, same complexity, same $\alpha$ dial.
