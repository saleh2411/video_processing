Take the logarithm, and it is a Kalman filter

The measurement looks hopelessly nonlinear — the noise is inside an exponent. One logarithm later it is the most standard linear-Gaussian model in the course: constant velocity, position and velocity, predict seven steps. Nothing here is new; the trick is seeing which model it already is.

Exam 8/7/2020 · Question 6 · 12 points · COVID-19

The Covid-19 pandemic spreads at an exponential rate. The measured daily number of new cases, $Z_t$, can be written as $Z_t = e^{-(r_t+n)}$ for some daily growth rate $r_t$ and white Gaussian noise $n$. Experts tell us that $r_t$ can be modeled as $r_t = r_{t-1} + \Delta r_{t-1}$. Design an on-line model that at day $t$ predicts $Z_{t+7}$, the number of new cases a week ahead.

The 30-second version — read this first 1. Log the measurement: $y_t=-\ln Z_t=r_t+n$ — now linear, with additive Gaussian noise.
2. State $x_t=\begin{pmatrix}r_t\\\Delta r_t\end{pmatrix}$, $F=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, $H=(1\;\;0)$, $R=\sigma_n^2$. This is the constant-velocity model, with $r$ as “position” and $\Delta r$ as “velocity”.
3. Run the ordinary Kalman filter once per day (that is the “on-line” part).
4. Forecast: $x_{t+7|t}=F^7x_{t|t}$ and $F^7=\begin{pmatrix}1&7\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, so $\hat r_{t+7}=\hat r_t+7\,\widehat{\Delta r}_t$, and $$\hat Z_{t+7}=e^{-\hat r_{t+7}}=e^{-(\hat r_t+7\widehat{\Delta r}_t)}.$$

1 · The story (no math at all)

Guessing where the toy car will be

A toy car rolls across the floor. Every second you glance at it and shout out where it is. Your little brother writes the numbers down, but he is sloppy, so every number is a bit wrong.

Now somebody asks: where will the car be in seven seconds?

You do not need physics. You need two numbers: where it is now, and how fast it is moving now. Then: seven seconds ahead = where it is + seven × how fast. That is the whole forecast.

You already know the machine that takes noisy “where is it” readings and quietly maintains a clean estimate of both where-it-is and how-fast — the Kalman filter with a constant-velocity model. You met it tracking people.

Now the epidemic. Case counts do not add — they multiply. Twice as many people each week, then twice again. A picture of that curve is useless to the eye, and a “speed” is meaningless when the units keep changing.

But look at what a logarithm does: it turns multiplying into adding. Plot the log of the cases and an exponential outbreak becomes a straight line. Straight lines have a position and a slope. A rolling toy car has a position and a speed. They are the same problem.

So: take logs, track the line with a Kalman filter, extend the line seven days, and un-log at the end.

The second thing the log fixes In $Z_t=e^{-(r_t+n)}$ the noise sits inside the exponent, so in case-count units the noise is multiplicative ($Z$ is log-normal, and its spread grows with its size — realistic: reporting error is a percentage, not a fixed number of people). Taking the log converts multiplicative noise into additive, constant-variance, Gaussian noise, which is precisely the assumption the Kalman filter needs. You are not approximating anything: this transformation is exact.

2 · Building the model, line by line

Step 1 — the measurement equation

Given $Z_t = e^{-(r_t + n)}$, take the natural log of both sides and flip the sign:

$$ \ln Z_t = -(r_t+n) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\;y_t \;:=\; -\ln Z_t \;=\; r_t + n,\quad n\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_n^2)\;} $$

$y_t$ is an observed quantity (you compute it from the reported case count) which is a linear function of the unknown $r_t$ plus additive Gaussian noise. That is exactly the form $z = Hx+\eta$ that the Kalman filter is built for. No EKF, no linearisation, no approximation error.

Step 2 — the state and the dynamics

The experts give you $r_t=r_{t-1}+\Delta r_{t-1}$. That is one equation with two unknowns, so $\Delta r$ must be part of the state. Add the standard assumption that the trend itself drifts slowly (a random walk):

$$ x_t=\begin{pmatrix} r_t\\[2pt] \Delta r_t\end{pmatrix},\qquad \begin{cases} r_t = r_{t-1}+\Delta r_{t-1}\\[2pt] \Delta r_t=\Delta r_{t-1}+w_t\end{cases} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x_t = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}}_{F}x_{t-1}+w_t,\quad w_t\sim\mathcal N(0,Q) $$
$$ y_t = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&0\end{pmatrix}}_{H}x_t + n,\qquad R=\sigma_n^2 $$
Recognise it $F=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ with $H=(1\ 0)$ is the constant-velocity model from the tracking chapter, with the log-rate playing the part of position and $\Delta r$ the part of velocity. You are tracking a point moving along the log-cases axis. Everything you know about that model transfers directly.

A reasonable process noise is the piecewise-constant-acceleration choice $Q=\sigma_w^2\begin{pmatrix}1/3&1/2\\1/2&1\end{pmatrix}$, or simply $Q=\mathrm{diag}(q_r,q_{\Delta})$ with $q_{\Delta}\gg q_r$ — you believe the trend can change (a lockdown), not that the rate teleports.

Step 3 — the on-line loop (one pass per day, $O(1)$ work, no history stored)

  1. Predict. $\;\hat x_{t|t-1}=F\hat x_{t-1|t-1}$, $\;P_{t|t-1}=FP_{t-1|t-1}F^{\!\top}+Q$.
  2. Observe. Today's report $Z_t$ arrives → compute $y_t=-\ln Z_t$.
  3. Update. $\;\nu_t=y_t-H\hat x_{t|t-1}$, $\;S_t=HP_{t|t-1}H^{\!\top}+R$, $\;K_t=P_{t|t-1}H^{\!\top}S_t^{-1}$, $\;\hat x_{t|t}=\hat x_{t|t-1}+K_t\nu_t$, $\;P_{t|t}=(I-K_tH)P_{t|t-1}$.
  4. Forecast 7 days. Iterate the prediction with no measurements: $\;\hat x_{t+7|t}=F^{7}\hat x_{t|t}$, and since $F^{k}=\begin{pmatrix}1&k\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, $\;\hat r_{t+7}=\hat r_t+7\widehat{\Delta r}_t$.
  5. Un-log. $\;\hat Z_{t+7}=e^{-\hat r_{t+7}}$.

3 · The detail worth two extra marks: $e^{-\hat r}$ is not the mean

The filter gives you a full Gaussian for the future log-rate:

$$ r_{t+7}\mid \mathcal Z_{1:t} \;\sim\; \mathcal N\big(\mu,\ \sigma^2\big),\qquad \mu = \hat r_t + 7\widehat{\Delta r}_t,\qquad \sigma^2 = \big[\,F^7P_{t|t}(F^7)^{\!\top} + \textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{6}F^kQ(F^k)^{\!\top}\big]_{11} $$

Then $Z_{t+7}=e^{-r_{t+7}}$ (ignoring the extra $n$ of that future day, or folding it in as $\sigma^2+\sigma_n^2$) is log-normal, and for a log-normal the exponential of the mean is not the mean:

$$ \text{median}\big(Z_{t+7}\big)=e^{-\mu},\qquad \mathbb E\big[Z_{t+7}\big]=e^{-\mu+\sigma^2/2},\qquad \text{95\% interval}=\Big[e^{-\mu-1.96\sigma},\;e^{-\mu+1.96\sigma}\Big] $$
Which one should you report? Say it explicitly and you cannot be marked down: $e^{-\mu}$ is the median / MAP forecast (the usual thing to plot), $e^{-\mu+\sigma^2/2}$ is the expected number of cases (what you want if you are budgeting hospital beds and care about the average), and the interval is what any epidemiologist will ask for first. The interval is asymmetric in case-count units — a genuine feature of exponential processes, not an artefact.

4 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

Full-marks version — one page

Linearise by taking logs (exactly, not approximately). From $Z_t=e^{-(r_t+n)}$,

$$ y_t \;:=\; -\ln Z_t \;=\; r_t + n,\qquad n\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_n^2). $$

The observation $y_t$ is linear in the unknown rate with additive Gaussian noise, so a plain Kalman filter applies — no EKF needed. (Equivalently: the noise is multiplicative in case counts, and the log makes it additive with constant variance.)

State-space model. The dynamics $r_t=r_{t-1}+\Delta r_{t-1}$ require the trend in the state; let the trend follow a random walk:

$$ x_t=\begin{pmatrix}r_t\\\Delta r_t\end{pmatrix},\quad x_t=Fx_{t-1}+w_t,\ F=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\ w_t\sim\mathcal N(0,Q);\qquad y_t=Hx_t+n,\ H=(1\ \ 0),\ R=\sigma_n^2. $$

This is the constant-velocity model, with $r$ as position and $\Delta r$ as velocity. Take $Q=\sigma_w^2\begin{pmatrix}1/3&1/2\\1/2&1\end{pmatrix}$ (or $\mathrm{diag}(q_r,q_\Delta)$ with $q_\Delta\gg q_r$): the trend, not the rate, is what changes when policy changes.

On-line recursion (once per day, $O(1)$ memory).

Predict: $\hat x_{t|t-1}=F\hat x_{t-1|t-1}$, $P_{t|t-1}=FP_{t-1|t-1}F^{\!\top}+Q$.
Update with $y_t=-\ln Z_t$: $\nu_t=y_t-H\hat x_{t|t-1}$, $S_t=HP_{t|t-1}H^{\!\top}+R$, $K_t=P_{t|t-1}H^{\!\top}S_t^{-1}$, $\hat x_{t|t}=\hat x_{t|t-1}+K_t\nu_t$, $P_{t|t}=(I-K_tH)P_{t|t-1}$.

Seven-day forecast. Iterate the prediction 7 times with no measurements: $\hat x_{t+7|t}=F^{7}\hat x_{t|t}$, and $F^{k}=\begin{pmatrix}1&k\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, so

$$ \hat r_{t+7}=\hat r_t+7\,\widehat{\Delta r}_t,\qquad \hat Z_{t+7}=e^{-\hat r_{t+7}}=e^{-(\hat r_t+7\widehat{\Delta r}_t)}. $$

The forecast covariance is $P_{t+7|t}=F^7P_{t|t}(F^7)^{\!\top}+\sum_{k=0}^{6}F^kQ(F^k)^{\!\top}$; write $\sigma^2=[P_{t+7|t}]_{11}$ ($+\sigma_n^2$ if predicting the reported number rather than the true rate). Since $r_{t+7}\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ with $\mu=\hat r_{t+7}$, the predicted case count is log-normal: $e^{-\mu}$ is its median, $\mathbb E[Z_{t+7}]=e^{-\mu+\sigma^2/2}$, and a 95% interval is $[e^{-\mu-1.96\sigma},\,e^{-\mu+1.96\sigma}]$.

Initialisation and practical notes. Start with $\hat x_0=(-\ln Z_0,\,0)^{\!\top}$ and a large $P_0$ so the first days dominate; tune $\sigma_n^2$ from the residual variance of the reported series and $Q$ by maximising the innovation likelihood $\sum_t\big(-\tfrac12\ln S_t-\tfrac{\nu_t^2}{2S_t}\big)$. Reported counts have a strong day-of-week effect, so either smooth with a 7-day average, or extend the state with weekday offsets. Days with $Z_t=0$ must be handled (the log is undefined) — merge or clamp. Finally, monitor the normalised innovation $\nu_t^2/S_t$: a run of large values means the model has broken (a new variant, a policy change) and $Q$ should be temporarily inflated.

5 · Exam drill

Q. Why not run an EKF directly on $Z_t$?

You can: $h(x)=e^{-r}$ with Jacobian $H=\partial h/\partial r=-e^{-r}$. But it is strictly worse. The EKF linearises around the current estimate, so it carries linearisation error that grows exactly when the counts are large; the noise in $Z$ is not Gaussian (it is log-normal), violating the filter's assumption; and the innovation variance changes by orders of magnitude over the epidemic, making tuning miserable. The log transform is exact and leaves you with a textbook linear-Gaussian problem. Always prefer an exact change of variables to an approximation.

Q. Why must $\Delta r$ be in the state? Why not estimate it by differencing?

Differencing noisy measurements amplifies the noise: $\mathrm{Var}(y_t-y_{t-1})=2\sigma_n^2$, and you have no way to smooth it without hand-picking a window. Putting $\Delta r$ in the state makes the filter estimate it optimally — the Kalman gain automatically chooses how much of each new residual to attribute to a change in level versus a change in trend, based on $Q$ and $R$. It also gives you $P$, hence an uncertainty on the trend, hence a forecast interval. And it is what makes the 7-step forecast $F^7x$ a one-liner.

Q. Why does forecasting 7 days ahead cost you so much accuracy?

Because uncertainty compounds. Each prediction step multiplies the covariance by $F$ on both sides and adds $Q$: $P_{t+7|t}=F^7P(F^7)^\top+\sum_{k=0}^6F^kQ(F^k)^\top$. With $F^7=\begin{pmatrix}1&7\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, the trend variance enters the rate variance multiplied by $7^2=49$. So a small uncertainty in $\Delta r$ turns into a large uncertainty in $r$ a week out — and then the exponential magnifies it again. This is why the orange band in Demo 1 is wide, and why it should be.

Q. What breaks if there is a lockdown on day $t_0$?

$\Delta r$ jumps — exactly the thing the random-walk model says happens only slowly. The filter will lag: for several days the innovations $\nu_t$ are large and one-signed, and the forecast keeps extrapolating the old trend. Remedies: monitor the normalised innovation squared $\nu_t^2/S_t$ (it should be $\approx1$ under a correct model) and inflate $Q$ when a run of large values is detected (adaptive filtering); or use an interacting-multiple-model / switching filter with a "policy change" mode; or, if the date is known, simply reset $P$ on that day. This is the same problem as a tracked person suddenly changing direction.

Q. What is the interpretation of $r_t$ and its sign here?

With $Z_t=e^{-(r_t+n)}$, growing case counts mean $r_t$ decreasing. $-\Delta r_t$ is the daily log-growth rate, so the doubling time is $\ln2/|\Delta r|$ days, and the weekly multiplication factor is $e^{-7\Delta r}$ — which is precisely the quantity your forecast computes. $\Delta r=0$ is a plateau, and the sign of $\Delta r$ flips at the epidemic peak, so the filter's estimate of $\Delta r$ crossing zero is a peak detector.

Q. How would you handle the weekly reporting cycle?

Reported cases dip at weekends, which is a deterministic, periodic contamination of $y_t$ — not noise, so the filter will chase it. Three standard options: (a) pre-smooth with a centred 7-day moving average (simple, but it delays detection by 3 days); (b) add 6 weekday-offset terms to the state with $F$ acting as a cyclic permutation, letting the filter learn them online; (c) model it as a known additive regressor $c_{d(t)}$ so $y_t=Hx_t+c_{d(t)}+n$, estimated from historical data. Option (b) is the "proper" state-space answer.
