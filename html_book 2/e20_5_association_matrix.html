Companion page to Chapter 4 · Exam 8/7/2020, question 5 · 13 points

Not “which is closest”, but “which is closest in units of my own uncertainty”

The Kalman filter already hands you everything you need: a prediction and a covariance. The association score is just the Gaussian that those two define, evaluated at the measurement. One formula, plus three details that separate a 9 from a 13.

Exam 8/7/2020 · Question 5 · 13 points · TRACKING

Consider a multi-person tracking system. At each time step you have $n$ targets. Target $i$ is tracked using a Kalman Filter with state vector $X_i$ and covariance matrix $P_i$. At each time step you obtain $m$ measurements $Z_1,...,Z_m$ (say by running a person detector). Both the state $X$ and the measurement $Z$ are 2D vectors that represent the 2D position of a target. See Figure 4. Design an association matrix $\Omega$ of size $n\times m$, where $\Omega(i,j)$ determines how likely is measurement $j$ to belong to target $i$. Express $\Omega(i,j)$ in terms of the Kalman filter parameters.

The 30-second version — read this first $$\Omega(i,j)\;=\;\mathcal N\big(Z_j;\,H\hat X_i^-,\,S_i\big) \;=\;\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{|S_i|}}\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12\,\nu_{ij}^{\!\top}S_i^{-1}\nu_{ij}\Big)$$ with the innovation $\nu_{ij}=Z_j-H\hat X_i^-$ and the innovation covariance $S_i=HP_i^-H^{\!\top}+R$. Since here $X$ and $Z$ are both 2-D positions, $H=I$ and this is just $\nu_{ij}=Z_j-\hat X_i^-$, $\;S_i=P_i^-+R$.

Then: gate with $\nu^{\!\top}S^{-1}\nu\le\chi^2_{2,0.95}=5.99$, and assign by running Hungarian on the cost $C(i,j)=-\log\Omega(i,j)$.

1 · The story (no math at all)

Three kids and four footprints

It snowed last night. This morning you find four fresh footprints in the yard, and you know three kids live on your street. Which footprint belongs to which kid? (One footprint might belong to the postman — that's why there are four prints and three kids.)

The obvious rule is: give each kid the footprint nearest to where you last saw them. That is usually right, and sometimes badly wrong. Here is why.

Kid 1 — Noa. You watched her walk down the path one second ago. You know almost exactly where she should be. If a footprint is even one metre off, it is probably not hers.

Kid 2 — Adam. You last saw him ten minutes ago and he was running. He could be anywhere in a huge area. A footprint five metres from your guess is completely believable for him.

So “one metre away” is a big deal for Noa and a tiny deal for Adam. You must not measure distance in metres. You must measure distance in units of “how surprised am I?”

And there is a third wrinkle. Kid 3 — Maya is on a bicycle going fast down the road. You are confident about which side of the road she is on, but not at all confident about how far along it she got. Her cloud of uncertainty is not a circle — it is a long thin cigar pointing down the road. A footprint 3 m ahead of the guess is fine; a footprint 3 m sideways is not.

The Kalman filter has already computed that cloud for you. It is called $P$. The whole answer to this question is: score each footprint by how well it fits inside that kid's own cloud.

What “the cloud” actually is After the predict step, the Kalman filter believes the target's position is Gaussian with mean $\hat X_i^-$ and covariance $P_i^-$. The detector adds its own noise $R$. So the position of the measurement you expect to see is Gaussian with mean $H\hat X_i^-$ and covariance $S_i=HP_i^-H^\top+R$. Drawing that as an ellipse is exactly the cloud in the story — and it is exactly the blue ellipses in the demo below.

2 · Building $\Omega$ from the Kalman parameters

Step 1 — predict, because association happens before update

At time $t$ you first run the prediction step for every target:

$$ \hat X_i^- = F\hat X_i^{+}, \qquad P_i^- = F P_i^{+} F^{\!\top} + Q $$

You must associate against the predicted state — the previous corrected state is one time step stale, and the whole point of a motion model is that it tells you where to look.

Step 2 — what would the measurement look like?

Measurement model $Z = HX + \eta$, $\eta\sim\mathcal N(0,R)$. So, conditioned on target $i$, the measurement is Gaussian:

$$ Z \mid \text{target }i \;\sim\; \mathcal N\big(\underbrace{H\hat X_i^-}_{\text{predicted measurement}},\; \underbrace{HP_i^-H^{\!\top}+R}_{\textstyle S_i\;=\;\text{innovation covariance}}\big) $$

Note that $S_i$ adds two uncertainties: how unsure I am about the target ($P$, mapped into measurement space by $H$) and how unsure I am about the detector ($R$). Both matter, and both are quantities the Kalman filter already carries.

Step 3 — the entry

Define the innovation (residual) of the pair, and score the measurement by the density:

$$ \nu_{ij} \;=\; Z_j - H\hat X_i^-, \qquad d^2_{ij} \;=\; \nu_{ij}^{\!\top}S_i^{-1}\nu_{ij}\quad\text{(squared \textbf{Mahalanobis} distance)} $$
$$ \boxed{\;\Omega(i,j) \;=\; p(Z_j\mid \text{target }i) \;=\;\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\sqrt{|S_i|}}\;\exp\!\left(-\tfrac12\,d^2_{ij}\right)\;} \qquad d=2\ \Rightarrow\ (2\pi)^{d/2}=2\pi $$

For this question $X$ and $Z$ are both the 2-D position, so $H=I_{2\times2}$ and everything simplifies:

$$ \nu_{ij}=Z_j-\hat X_i^-,\qquad S_i=P_i^-+R,\qquad \Omega(i,j)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{|P_i^-+R|}}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac12(Z_j-\hat X_i^-)^{\!\top}(P_i^-+R)^{-1}(Z_j-\hat X_i^-)\right) $$
Why the $1/\sqrt{|S_i|}$ factor is not optional It is what makes entries comparable across different rows. A target with a huge uncertainty ellipse gives a broad, flat Gaussian: it accepts far-away measurements, but its peak value is low. Drop the normaliser and every measurement would look wonderfully likely for the most uncertain target, so a lost track would greedily steal detections from well-tracked ones. Keep it, and a confident target with a small ellipse correctly outbids a vague one for a measurement that fits them both.

3 · The three details that finish the answer

a. Gating — refuse the absurd matches

$d^2_{ij}$ is a sum of 2 squared standard normals, so under the correct-association hypothesis it is $\chi^2$ with $d=2$ degrees of freedom. Accept a pair only if

$$ d^2_{ij} \;\le\; \chi^2_{d,\,1-\alpha} \qquad(\,d=2,\ \alpha=0.05 \;\Rightarrow\; 5.991\,) $$

and set $\Omega(i,j)=0$ otherwise. This is not cosmetic: it is what lets the system say “this detection belongs to nobody” (a new person entering the scene, or a false detection) and “this target was not detected this frame” (occlusion) instead of always forcing $\min(n,m)$ matches. Gating also cuts the search cost.

b. Turning likelihoods into an assignment

$\Omega$ is a score matrix, not yet a decision. Two standard ways to finish:

  • Hard assignment (GNN). Build the cost matrix $C(i,j) = -\log\Omega(i,j)$ (and $+\infty$ for gated pairs), then solve the linear assignment problem $\min_\pi\sum_i C(i,\pi(i))$ with the Hungarian algorithm in $O(n^3)$. Maximising $\prod_j\Omega$ under a one-to-one constraint is exactly minimising $\sum -\log\Omega$. Pad the matrix with dummy rows/columns priced at the gate threshold so “no match” is a legal choice.
  • Soft assignment (PDA/JPDA). Do not commit: give target $i$ a weighted mixture of all the measurements in its gate, with normalised weights $\;\beta_{ij}= \dfrac{P_D\,\Omega(i,j)}{\lambda(1-P_D)\;+\;P_D\sum_{k}\Omega(i,k)}\;$ where $P_D$ is the detection probability and $\lambda$ the clutter density; $\beta_{i0}$ is the "missed-detection" weight. Then update the Kalman filter with the combined innovation $\sum_j\beta_{ij}\nu_{ij}$. Robust in clutter, at the price of a covariance inflation term.

c. Use more than position, if you have it

A person detector also gives you an appearance patch and a box size. Since the cues are conditionally independent given the association, the likelihoods simply multiply:

$$ \Omega(i,j) \;=\; \underbrace{\mathcal N(Z_j;H\hat X_i^-,S_i)}_{\text{motion}}\;\cdot\; \underbrace{\exp\!\big(-\gamma\,d_{\text{app}}(a_i,a_j)\big)}_{\text{appearance (colour histogram, embedding)}} $$

This is what saves you when two people cross: their motion gates overlap, and only appearance can break the tie. (Same trick as the observation model in the particle-filter chapter.)

4 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

Full-marks version — one page

Predict first. At time $t$, run the Kalman prediction for each target $i$: $\hat X_i^-=F\hat X_i^+$, $P_i^-=FP_i^+F^{\!\top}+Q$. Association is done against the predicted state.

Predicted measurement and its covariance. With measurement model $Z=HX+\eta$, $\eta\sim\mathcal N(0,R)$, the measurement expected from target $i$ is Gaussian with mean $H\hat X_i^-$ and innovation covariance

$$ S_i \;=\; H P_i^- H^{\!\top} + R. $$

Here $X,Z\in\mathbb R^2$ are both positions, so $H=I$ and $S_i = P_i^- + R$.

The association matrix. With the innovation $\nu_{ij}=Z_j-H\hat X_i^-$ and the squared Mahalanobis distance $d^2_{ij}=\nu_{ij}^{\!\top}S_i^{-1}\nu_{ij}$, define

$$ \Omega(i,j)\;=\;p(Z_j\mid i)\;=\;\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{|S_i|}} \exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12\,\nu_{ij}^{\!\top}S_i^{-1}\nu_{ij}\Big). $$

Every quantity is a Kalman filter parameter: $\hat X_i^-,P_i^-$ from the predict step, $H,R$ from the measurement model. The Mahalanobis form is essential — it measures the residual in units of the target's own uncertainty, so a confident track demands a close measurement while an uncertain one tolerates a distant one, and an elongated $P$ (e.g. along the direction of motion) tolerates error along that direction only. The $1/\sqrt{|S_i|}$ normaliser makes entries comparable between rows, preventing uncertain targets from capturing every measurement.

Gating. Under the correct hypothesis $d^2_{ij}\sim\chi^2_2$, so accept only pairs with $d^2_{ij}\le\chi^2_{2,0.95}=5.99$ and set $\Omega(i,j)=0$ otherwise. Gating lets a measurement be declared clutter / a new target, and a target be declared undetected (occluded), instead of forcing $\min(n,m)$ matches.

From $\Omega$ to a decision. Either (a) hard assignment: build $C(i,j)=-\log\Omega(i,j)$ ($\infty$ if gated) and solve the linear assignment problem with the Hungarian algorithm, which maximises $\prod\Omega$ under a one-to-one constraint, padding with dummy rows/columns for “no match”; or (b) soft assignment (PDA/JPDA): update target $i$ with the weighted innovation $\sum_j\beta_{ij}\nu_{ij}$ where $\beta_{ij}\propto P_D\,\Omega(i,j)$, normalised together with a missed-detection term $\propto\lambda(1-P_D)$. Finally, if the detector also supplies appearance, multiply in $\exp(-\gamma\,d_{\text{app}})$, which is what disambiguates targets whose gates overlap during a crossing.

5 · Exam drill

Q. Why $S=HPH^\top+R$ and not just $P$ or just $R$?

Two independent sources of error separate the prediction from the observation. $HPH^\top$ is the target's own positional uncertainty pushed through the measurement map — how wrong my prediction of the state may be, expressed in measurement units. $R$ is how wrong the detector is even about a perfectly known target. The residual $\nu = Z-H\hat X^-$ contains both errors, and for independent errors variances add. Using only $P$ would make a perfect tracker reject every noisy detection; using only $R$ would make a lost track reject everything.

Q. Why is a $\chi^2$ table the right thing for the gate?

If measurement $j$ really does come from target $i$, then $\nu\sim\mathcal N(0,S)$, so $S^{-1/2}\nu\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ and $d^2=\|S^{-1/2}\nu\|^2$ is the sum of $d$ independent squared standard normals — the definition of $\chi^2_d$. So $d^2\le\chi^2_{d,0.95}$ is a hypothesis test at the 5% level: it keeps 95% of true associations. For $d=2$ the threshold is $5.991$; the ellipse $d^2=5.991$ is exactly the one drawn in Demo 1.

Q. Give a concrete case where nearest-neighbour in Euclidean distance fails.

Target A is a pedestrian tracked for 50 frames, standing still: $P_A$ is a tiny circle of radius 0.2 m. Target B just entered the scene: $P_B$ is 3 m across. A detection lands 0.9 m from A and 0.8 m from B. Euclidean picks B. Mahalanobis: for A, $d^2=(0.9/0.2)^2\approx20$ — outside the gate; for B, $d^2=(0.8/1.5)^2\approx0.3$ — a fine match. Here they agree. Now move the detection to 0.25 m from A and 0.20 m from B: Euclidean still picks B, but Mahalanobis gives A a $d^2\approx1.6$ against B's $\approx0.02$ — B still wins on $d^2$, yet after the $1/\sqrt{|S|}$ factor A's density is far higher, because a tight Gaussian is tall. That factor is what encodes "a confident tracker's opinion counts for more".

Q. $n \ne m$. What do the leftover rows and columns mean?

An unmatched column (measurement) is either clutter (a false detection) or a person entering the scene — in the latter case you initialise a new Kalman filter from it, typically with a large initial $P$. An unmatched row (target) means a missed detection: skip the update step, keep only the prediction, so $P_i$ grows and the gate widens for next frame; delete the track after $k$ consecutive misses. Implement both by padding the cost matrix with dummy entries priced at the gate threshold before running Hungarian.

Q. Why Hungarian rather than just picking the max of each row?

Row-wise maxima are greedy and inconsistent: two targets can claim the same measurement, and the resolution order changes the outcome. Hungarian solves the global problem — the one-to-one assignment maximising the joint likelihood $\prod_i\Omega(i,\pi(i))$, i.e. minimising $\sum_i-\log\Omega(i,\pi(i))$ — in $O(n^3)$. The log turns the product into a sum, which is exactly the additive form the assignment algorithm needs.

Q. When should you prefer JPDA over a hard assignment?

In heavy clutter, or when targets are close enough that several measurements fall in several gates simultaneously — precisely the crossing case. A hard decision there is a coin flip that, if wrong, corrupts the state permanently (identity switch). JPDA never commits: it updates with the probability-weighted average innovation, so a wrong guess degrades gracefully rather than catastrophically. The cost is an inflated covariance (the spread-of-innovations term) and combinatorial complexity in the number of joint hypotheses, which is why practical systems gate aggressively first.
