The building vanished and the lawn grew into the sky

A hole over a dome gets filled with grass, trees and a chunk of crowd. Not a bug — the algorithm did exactly what it was told. The interesting part is that the correct answer and the wrong answer have almost the same cost, so nothing in the objective could have preferred the right one.

Exam 8/7/2020 · Question 3 · 13 points · IMAGE INPAINTING

Consider Figure 2. The user tried to inpaint the marked rectangle using the Bidirectional similarity measure and got this result. Explain how this can happen and propose a solution to fix this problem.
(Figure 2: left — the MIT dome with a blue rectangle covering the building and sky; right — the “filled” result, in which the hole now contains lawn, trees and people, and the roof line does not continue.)

The 30-second version — read this first Two causes, and the answer wants both.
(1) The wrong half of the measure. BDS is completeness + coherence. For hole filling only coherence is meaningful. Keeping completeness asks that every patch of the source appear somewhere in the output — and the only free canvas is the hole, so the lawn, the trees and the crowd get pushed into the hole. That is precisely what the figure shows.
(2) Coherence alone is degenerate + non-convex. A hole full of grass has essentially zero coherence cost, exactly like the correct building continuation. The objective cannot tell them apart, so the result is decided by initialisation, and the iterative EM optimisation is stuck in a local minimum.
Fix: coherence-only, with a hole-restricted search, coarse-to-fine with structure-aware fill order, and explicit structure/user constraints.

1 · The story (no math at all)

The jigsaw puzzle with a missing corner

Imagine a jigsaw of a castle with grass in front of it. Someone loses a few pieces from the middle of the castle. You hand the box to a robot and say: “fill the gap so that every piece you put down looks like some piece that already exists on the table.”

The robot looks around. There are 4 pieces of castle wall, and there are 500 pieces of grass, which all look the same as each other. So it fills the gap with grass. Every single piece it laid down does look like a piece that already exists. It obeyed you perfectly. Grass is the cheapest, safest, most repeatable thing in the box.

Now the second problem. You also told it: “and make sure nothing on the table gets left out of the gap.” Now it is not just allowed to shove grass and people into the hole — it is required to. The hole is the only empty space, so everything gets crammed in there.

The robot did not fail. The instructions were wrong. You never once said “the castle wall must keep going in a straight line,” because a rule about lines cannot be written as a rule about little squares.

2 · The measure, written out — and which half hurts

Bidirectional similarity (Simakov et al.)

For a source $S$ and a target $T$, over all patches $s\subset S$, $t\subset T$:

$$ d_{\text{BDS}}(S,T) \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{N_S}\sum_{s\subset S}\ \min_{t\subset T} D(s,t)}_{\textstyle d_{\text{complete}}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{N_T}\sum_{t\subset T}\ \min_{s\subset S} D(s,t)}_{\textstyle d_{\text{cohere}}} $$

Read each half in words:

  • Completeness — “nothing from the input is missing in the output.” Every source patch must find a home in $T$. This is what stops a summarisation from dropping objects.
  • Coherence — “nothing new was invented in the output.” Every output patch must come from somewhere in $S$. This is what stops the output from containing garbage.

Now apply that to hole filling

In inpainting, $S$ = the image outside the hole (known pixels), $T$ = the hole. What do you actually want? Only that the stuff you paint into the hole is real image content — that is coherence, and only coherence. You emphatically do not want “every patch of the surrounding image must appear inside the hole”: the hole is small, the image is large, and the demand is not just unnecessary, it is actively harmful — it turns the hole into a dumping ground for a summary of the whole photo.

This is the observable signature in the figure The filled region contains people and trees that were nowhere near the hole. Coherence alone would never invent that: it would happily copy the immediate surroundings, because those are the closest matches to the hole's boundary conditions. Content teleporting in from across the image is the fingerprint of the completeness term being included in an inpainting objective.

3 · The second cause: it is a non-convex optimisation

Minimising $d_{\text{cohere}}$ over the unknown pixels is solved by an EM-style alternation (the same loop as in the retargeting chapter):

  1. E-step (search). For every patch overlapping the hole, find its nearest neighbour in the source — with PatchMatch, because exhaustive search is hopeless.
  2. M-step (vote). Each hole pixel is covered by many patches; set it to the (weighted) average of the corresponding pixels of all the matched source patches.
  3. Repeat, inside a coarse-to-fine pyramid: solve at low resolution, upsample, refine.

Three consequences, all of them causes of the observed result:

  • Local minima. The objective is non-convex; the alternation only ever descends. Whatever content the hole is initialised with steers the whole thing. A random or mean-colour initialisation at the coarsest level essentially picks the semantics of the fill by coin flip.
  • The coarsest level decides everything. At the top of the pyramid the hole is a handful of pixels; a decision made there ("this region is greenish") is then merely refined — later levels can add detail but cannot revisit "it should have been a building".
  • Voting blurs and washes out structure. Averaging many candidate patches produces a smooth, texture-less fill, which then attracts even more smooth matches. Straight edges (the roof line) are the first casualty, because an edge in the wrong place costs only a few patches, while a whole region of the wrong texture costs almost nothing at all.

4 · The fixes

Ranked — the first two are the ones that must be in your answer

1. Use coherence only (drop completeness)

Minimise, over the unknown pixels $T_{\text{hole}}$,

$$ E(T) \;=\; \sum_{t \,\cap\, \text{hole} \neq \emptyset}\ \min_{s\subset S} D(s,t), \qquad S=\text{image}\setminus\text{hole} $$

Every patch that touches the hole must look like a real patch of the image; nothing forces source content into the hole. Patches straddling the hole boundary carry the known pixels, and that is what makes the fill agree with its surroundings.

2. Constrain the search space

Do not let the whole image compete. Options, all cheap: restrict candidate patches to a window around the hole; weight the patch distance by spatial distance, $D'(s,t)=D(s,t)+\mu\|pos(s)-pos(t)\|$; or forbid patches whose segment/label differs from the label continuing into the hole (sky patches for the sky part, facade patches for the facade part). This directly kills “people teleport into the dome”.

3. Better optimisation, not just a better objective

Coarse-to-fine with a sensible initialisation (e.g. onion-peel inward from the boundary, or diffusion/PDE inpainting as the coarse-level seed), several random restarts keeping the lowest energy, and a fill order driven by Criminisi-style priority $P=C(p)\,D(p)$ — confidence × the strength of the isophote hitting the boundary — so strong edges are continued first, before the flat texture takes over.

4. Give the objective a notion of structure

Since patches cannot express “lines continue”, add it explicitly: user-drawn structure curves that the algorithm must follow (structure propagation), line/vanishing-point detection to extend the roof line and the columns, or matching on gradients as well as colours. Allowing transformed patches (scale + rotation, and a gain/bias on brightness) also helps a perspective facade match itself.

5. Cosmetics that stop the giveaway artefacts

Use a robust vote (weighted median / EM with a Gaussian weight $e^{-D/2\sigma^2}$) instead of a plain mean, so the fill does not blur; composite the result in the gradient domain (Poisson blending) so the hole boundary is seamless.

5 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

Full-marks version — one page

What BDS says. $d_{\text{BDS}}(S,T)=\frac{1}{N_S}\sum_{s\subset S}\min_{t\subset T}D(s,t)+\frac{1}{N_T}\sum_{t\subset T}\min_{s\subset S}D(s,t)$: completeness (nothing from the input is lost) plus coherence (nothing new is invented).

Why this happened — reason 1: the wrong term. For hole filling, $S$ is the known image and $T$ is the filled hole, and only coherence is desired. Keeping the completeness term demands that every patch of the surrounding image be represented inside the output; since the hole is the only region free to change, the optimiser satisfies it by packing unrelated source content — lawn, trees, people — into the hole. That is exactly the artefact in Figure 2.

Why this happened — reason 2: the objective is degenerate, and the optimisation is non-convex. Coherence is a purely local, patch-level statistic with no notion of long-range structure or semantics. A hole filled entirely with the image's most repetitive texture (grass) has near-zero coherence cost — as low as, or lower than, the correct continuation of the building, because grass patches match each other exactly. So the objective cannot prefer the correct answer. The minimisation is an EM alternation (PatchMatch NN search ↔ voting) inside a coarse-to-fine pyramid; it only descends, so the semantics of the fill are effectively chosen by the initialisation at the coarsest level and can never be revisited. Averaging in the vote step additionally smooths away edges, so the roof line is not continued.

Proposed fix.

(i) Objective: minimise coherence only, $E(T)=\sum_{t\cap\text{hole}\neq\emptyset}\min_{s\subset S}D(s,t)$, with $S$ the image outside the hole; patches straddling the boundary enforce agreement with the surroundings.

(ii) Search space: restrict candidate patches to a neighbourhood of the hole, or add a spatial penalty $D'(s,t)=D(s,t)+\mu\|pos(s)-pos(t)\|$, or forbid matches across segmentation labels — so distant lawn/crowd patches cannot win.

(iii) Optimisation: coarse-to-fine with a sensible initialisation (onion-peel from the boundary or a diffusion-based seed) rather than random; several restarts keeping the lowest energy; a Criminisi-style priority fill order $P(p)=C(p)D(p)$ so strong isophotes are propagated before flat texture; robust (weighted-median) voting instead of the mean.

(iv) Structure: because patches cannot encode “this line continues”, add it explicitly — user-drawn structure curves / detected lines that the fill must follow, matching on gradients as well as colour, and allowing scaled and rotated patches so a perspective facade can match itself. Composite with Poisson blending to remove seams.

6 · Exam drill

Q. In one sentence: what is completeness for, if it is wrong here?

Summarisation and retargeting. When you shrink an image, the danger is that whole objects silently disappear, and completeness is precisely the term that forbids that. Inpainting has the opposite geometry: the output is a subset of the scene, not a summary of it, so demanding that everything be represented is a category error. Same measure, different task, different half.

Q. Would a bigger patch size fix it?

It helps with structure — a larger patch spans more of a straight line, so breaking the roof line becomes more expensive — but it does not fix the degeneracy: a big patch of lawn still matches another big patch of lawn at zero cost. It also costs you: large patches match fewer places, the fill becomes a visible copy-paste of one large region, and the search gets slower. Multi-scale patches (or the pyramid, which is the same idea) is the usable version of “bigger patches”.

Q. Why does the vote step blur, and why does that matter?

Each hole pixel sits under $p^2$ overlapping patches, each proposing its own colour; the M-step averages them. Early on those proposals disagree, so the average is a smear. A smeared region has weak gradients, and weak gradients match other smooth regions best — so the blur is self-reinforcing and the fill drifts towards the flattest texture available. Fixes: weight the vote by match quality, $w=e^{-D/2\sigma^2}$, or take a weighted median; both let a strong minority of confident patches win over a mushy majority.

Q. The hole boundary is the only real constraint. Make that precise.

Patches centred inside the hole but overlapping known pixels have part of their cost determined by pixels you cannot change. Those partial matches are the entire link between the fill and the rest of the photo — and their influence reaches only $p/2$ pixels inward. So for a hole much larger than the patch, the interior is essentially unconstrained: it only has to be self-consistent, which is why big holes fail much worse than thin scratches, and why the pyramid matters (at coarse scale, $p/2$ covers a bigger fraction of the hole).

Q. Does PatchMatch cause this failure?

No — PatchMatch is only the accelerator for the E-step, an approximate nearest-neighbour field found by random initialisation, propagation and random search. It can add its own randomness to which local minimum you land in, but the failure in the figure is a property of the objective, not of the search: an exhaustive, exact nearest-neighbour search would give the lawn fill an equally good score.

Q. How would you evaluate whether your fix worked, without eyeballing it?

Hold-out: take intact images, punch synthetic holes, inpaint, and measure against the known ground truth — PSNR/SSIM inside the hole, plus a structural check such as the fraction of ground-truth edge pixels recovered. Report the final coherence energy too: if your “better” result has a higher energy than the lawn fill, that is direct evidence that the objective, not the optimiser, was the problem — which is the whole point of this question.
