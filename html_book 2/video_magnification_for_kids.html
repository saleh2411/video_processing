Eulerian Video Magnification, explained like you are five

A pulse in someone's neck. A skyscraper swaying by a millimetre. A crisp packet vibrating to the sound of a voice. All invisible — all already recorded in an ordinary video. This page builds the whole method from zero: why it never estimates motion, why a slope is what makes it possible, what the band-pass is really doing, and exactly where it falls apart. Six demos you can press.

What goes in
  • A video from a steady camera (tripod).
  • An amplification factor $\alpha$ (typically 5–50).
  • A temporal frequency band $[f_{lo}, f_{hi}]$ — the speed of the thing you want to see.
  • Optionally: a spatial pyramid, if motions exceed about a pixel.
What comes out
  • The same video, same length, same size — with motions in that band amplified by $\alpha$.
  • Nothing else changes: the picture, the colours and the lighting stay put.
  • No motion vectors, no tracks, no segmentation. Those are never computed.

That is the entire algorithm. Four steps, no iteration, no optimisation, no linear system. The rest of this page is why each step is what it is.

1 · The story — two ways to watch a river

The leaf and the bridge

You want to know how a river moves. Two options.

Option A — be a leaf. Drop a leaf in the water and run along the bank following it. You now know exactly where that piece of water went. To describe the whole river you need to follow thousands of leaves and never lose one. This is the Lagrangian view — and in video it means optical flow: track every pixel, chapter 2, hard and fragile.

Option B — stand on the bridge. Do not follow anything. Stare at one fixed spot in the water and watch how the water at that spot changes. Do the same at a thousand fixed spots and you know the river just as well. This is the Eulerian view — you never track anything, you only watch things change under a fixed window.

Eulerian magnification stands on the bridge. Every pixel is one fixed spot. It never asks “where did this go?”, only “how did the value here change?” — and that question is answered by subtraction, not by search.

The one sentence to remember Lagrangian = follow the pixel (needs motion estimation). Eulerian = stand still and watch the value (needs only a subtraction). Eulerian magnification amplifies motion without ever computing motion.

But the probe at the bottom still records a real change, because a 0.15-pixel shift across a steep edge still changes that pixel's brightness by a few grey levels. The motion was always in the data. Only the Eulerian view can reach it.

2 · The core trick — a slope turns motion into brightness

The single idea the whole method rests on

Take one pixel sitting on the edge of a dark object against a bright wall. The image there is a ramp: brightness climbs as you move right.

Now shift the whole object right by a tiny $\delta$. The pixel does not move — but the ramp slid under it, so the pixel now reports a different height on the ramp. How much different? $\delta$ times the steepness of the ramp.

That is it. That is the whole conversion: a small motion becomes a small brightness change, and the exchange rate is the spatial gradient.

Demo 2 — where does the brightness change come from?

drag δ and the probe

The curve is a 1-D slice of an image: a dark region, a soft edge, a bright region. The vertical line is your probe pixel. Slide $\delta$ to shift the picture, and watch the two readings on the probe. Then drag the probe into the flat area and slide $\delta$ again.

Two consequences you can state in an exam
  • Flat regions cannot be magnified. Gradient zero means brightness change zero, no matter how much the object moved. Eulerian magnification only ever works at edges and texture. A blank wall that is genuinely swaying produces literally nothing.
  • The exchange rate is a constant per pixel. $\partial f/\partial x$ does not change over time (the picture is still). So filtering the brightness signal in time is the same as filtering the displacement in time — which is why you can clean up $\delta$ without ever knowing $\delta$.

The derivation, symbol by symbol

Model the video as one fixed picture $f$ being shifted by a tiny time-varying amount:

$$I(x,t)=f\big(x+\delta(t)\big),\qquad I(x,0)=f(x)$$

First-order Taylor expansion around $x$:

$$I(x,t)\;\approx\; f(x)\;+\;\underbrace{\delta(t)\,\frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x}}_{B(x,t)}$$

where:

$f(x)$the still picture — the DC part, constant in time. This is what the video looks like when nothing moves.
$\delta(t)$the invisible sub-pixel displacement. The thing you want, and the thing you will never compute.
$\partial f(x)/\partial x$the spatial gradient at this pixel — the ramp's steepness. Fixed per pixel; zero on flat regions.
$B(x,t)$everything that varies in time at this pixel. In words: the pixel's brightness signal with its average removed. This is the object the algorithm actually manipulates.

Now add $\alpha$ extra copies of $B$ back onto the video:

$$\tilde I(x,t)=I(x,t)+\alpha B(x,t)\;\approx\;f(x)+(1+\alpha)\,\delta(t)\frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x} \;\approx\;f\Big(x+(1+\alpha)\delta(t)\Big)$$

Read the last step backwards: a signal equal to picture plus $(1{+}\alpha)\delta$ times slope is, by the very same Taylor expansion, what you would have recorded if the object had moved $(1{+}\alpha)$ times further. The motion has been multiplied by $\alpha$ and $\delta$ was never estimated, searched for, or even named in the computation.

Why this feels like cheating (and why it is not) You did not create information. The 0.15-pixel motion was already encoded in the pixel as a few-grey-level change. All you did was rescale that change so a human eye can see it. That is why the method is called magnification and not reconstruction — and it is also why it amplifies noise just as happily, which is what the band-pass exists to prevent.

3 · Adding it back — and the limit of $\alpha$

The approximation is first-order. It holds while the shift is small compared to how fast the picture changes in space. Push $(1{+}\alpha)\delta$ past about one pixel and $I+\alpha B$ stops being a shifted copy of $f$ — it starts overshooting near edges. That overshoot is the ringing / halo artefact.

Demo 3 — push α until it breaks

watch the halo appear

White = what the algorithm outputs, $I+\alpha B$. Dashed teal = the truth, the picture genuinely shifted by $(1{+}\alpha)\delta$. While they sit on top of each other the magnification is honest. Raise $\alpha$ (or $\delta$, or sharpen the edge) and watch them separate — the white curve grows ears the truth does not have.

The rule of thumb, derived Keep $(1+\alpha)\,\delta \lesssim 1$ pixel — or, more precisely, small compared to the width over which the image changes. Two knobs give you room: lower $\alpha$, or blur the image so the edge is wider and the linear approximation stays valid further. That second option is exactly what the pyramid does.

The pyramid fix

Build a spatial pyramid (repeatedly blur and downsample). At a coarse level the picture is smooth, so its gradients are gentle and Taylor stays valid for much larger shifts. So:

  1. Decompose each frame into spatial scales — a Laplacian/Gaussian pyramid.
  2. Filter and amplify each scale separately, with its own $\alpha$: large $\alpha$ for the coarse, smooth levels where the approximation is safe; small (or zero) $\alpha$ for the finest level where edges are sharpest and ringing appears first.
  3. Collapse the pyramid back into a frame.

Same trick, same reason as the coarse-to-fine pyramid in Lucas–Kanade (chapter 2): a linear approximation to motion is only valid at a scale where the image is smooth relative to the displacement.

4 · The missing ingredient — band-passing the temporal signal

Everything so far assumed $B$ contains only the motion you care about. It does not. Step 2 of the pipeline is where that gets fixed, and it is the step that decides whether the method produces a heartbeat or a strobing mess.

The pixel keeps a diary

Pick one pixel, say $(120,87)$ on someone's forehead. Every frame it writes one number in its diary: “my brightness was 141.” Next frame: “142.” After 300 frames it has 300 numbers.

Plot them left to right and you get a wiggly line. That line is the per-pixel temporal signal — that is the whole term. A pixel's diary, drawn. It is exactly $B$ plus a constant.

The heartbeat is inside that wiggle. But so are three other things, and they are all bigger.

The average level (DC) The pixel's base colour, $f(x)$. Speed: zero. This is the picture itself and must never be amplified.
Slow drift A cloud passes, the sun moves, auto-exposure breathes. Speed: one cycle per 10–60 s.
The thing you want Heartbeat ≈ 1 Hz. Breathing ≈ 0.2 Hz. A swaying building ≈ 0.5 Hz. An engine, tens of Hz.
Noise Sensor noise, compression flicker. Speed: as fast as possible — completely different every frame.
Why separating them is possible at all The target is the smallest of the four, so you cannot separate them by size. But each one lives at its own speed, with nothing else nearby: drift is ~10× slower, noise ~15× faster. Separation by speed is exactly what a band-pass filter does.

Frequency, in three sentences

A frequency is how many times per second something repeats, in hertz. A heart at 66 bpm is $66/60=1.1$ Hz. Any wiggly line can be written as a sum of pure sines at different frequencies — Demo 4 is literally that sum, built by hand. A band-pass filter keeps the sines between $f_{lo}$ and $f_{hi}$ and deletes the rest.

Nyquist — free exam points To see a wiggle at frequency $f$ you need a frame rate of at least $2f$: $$f_{\max}=\frac{\text{fps}}{2}$$ At 30 fps nothing above 15 Hz is reachable. A 20 Hz engine vibration is not merely missed — it aliases, appearing as a completely fake slow wiggle. Always check the target frequency against half the frame rate first.

Demo 5 — move the band, watch what survives

Left: the spectrum — one bar per frequency, showing how much of that speed is in the diary. Three lumps: drift far left, the pulse as a spike, noise smeared across everything. The lit region is your band. Right: the diary, what survives the filter ($B$), and the final output $I+\alpha B$.

What the three buttons prove
  • No filter → the output is dominated by drift and noise. The pulse is present and still invisible. This is the naive $B \approx I_2-I_1$ failure mode.
  • Tight band → $B$ becomes a clean sine at the pulse rate. Amplifying by 30 is now safe, because nothing else is left to amplify.
  • Wrong band → match with the true pulse falls to ~0%. The output is not empty (which would be harmless) — it is pure noise, amplified 20×.

Three real implementations of step 2

Way 1 — ideal band-pass by DFT along the time axis. Per pixel, DFT down the time axis, zero every bin outside the band, inverse. This is what Demo 5 computes.

# X has shape (T frames, H, W) — one diary per pixel, down axis 0
F     = np.fft.rfft(X, axis=0)              # DFT along TIME, not space
freqs = np.fft.rfftfreq(T, d=1.0/fps)       # which Hz each bin means
F[(freqs < f_lo) | (freqs > f_hi)] = 0      # kill everything outside the band
B     = np.fft.irfft(F, n=T, axis=0)        # B = the band-passed change signal
out   = X + alpha * B                       # amplify and add back

Sharpest possible band, but offline: you must hold all $T$ frames before emitting frame 1.

Way 2 — difference of two running averages (the online one). A moving average is a low-pass. Subtract a slower one from a faster one and what remains is the middle band.

$$L_1[n]=L_1[n-1]+a_1\big(x[n]-L_1[n-1]\big),\qquad L_2[n]=L_2[n-1]+a_2\big(x[n]-L_2[n-1]\big),\qquad B[n]=L_1[n]-L_2[n]$$

where: $x[n]$ = this frame's pixel value; $L_1$ = fast-following average, cutoff $\approx f_{hi}$; $L_2$ = slow-following average, cutoff $\approx f_{lo}$, so $a_2between. Two numbers of state per pixel, $O(1)$ per frame, no history stored — the same “replace storage with a running statistic” move as Weiss's 256-bin histogram and the GMM's incremental update.

Way 3 — the plain frame difference $B\approx I_2-I_1$. What the chapter writes. It is a filter too: the crudest temporal high-pass. It removes anything constant (the DC picture drops to exactly zero) and passes fast changes. It has no upper edge, so noise — the fastest component — comes through at full gain.

How to say this in an exam $B=I_2-I_1$ is the minimal temporal high-pass: it kills the DC term, which is the one thing the Taylor derivation strictly requires (you must not amplify the still picture). It is not a band-pass — it rejects neither drift's neighbours nor noise — so a real implementation replaces it with an actual band-pass around the target frequency.

5 · The whole algorithm, written out

INPUT : frames I[0..T-1], amplification α, band [f_lo, f_hi], fps
OUTPUT: magnified frames Ĩ[0..T-1]

# optional but recommended: work per spatial scale
for each pyramid level ℓ (coarse → fine):
    L[ℓ] = spatial_decompose(I, ℓ)

    for each pixel x independently:                 # no neighbours are ever consulted
        diary = L[ℓ][0..T-1][x]                     # the per-pixel temporal signal
        B[x]  = bandpass(diary, f_lo, f_hi, fps)    # step 2 — removes DC, drift and noise

    Ĩ[ℓ] = L[ℓ] + α(ℓ) · B                          # steps 3+4; α smaller on fine levels

Ĩ = collapse_pyramid(Ĩ[·])

Properties worth naming out loud:

  • Embarrassingly parallel. Every pixel is an independent 1-D filter. A million of them, no communication.
  • No optimisation. No least squares, no linear system, no iteration to convergence. Compare with Horn–Schunck, which solves a $2N\times2N$ system for the same physical phenomenon.
  • Linear. The output is a linear function of the input, which is why it can be pushed through a pyramid and recombined safely.

Four ways the whole thing fails
  • Camera shake. Shake sits in the same frequency band as most targets, so no filter removes it — and $\alpha$ multiplies it. Tripod, always.
  • Motion beyond ~1 pixel. The Taylor step, not the filter, is what breaks. Fix with the pyramid or a smaller $\alpha$.
  • Flat regions. Zero gradient, zero output, however much the surface moved.
  • Target drifts out of the band. The pulse moves from 1.1 to 1.9 Hz, the band stays at 0.9–1.3, and the output silently becomes nothing.

Where it is actually used

  • Medicine — contactless pulse and respiration from a webcam.
  • Civil engineering — micro-vibrations of bridges, dams and buildings with no attached sensors.
  • Manufacturing — diagnosing machine faults from video of a housing.
  • The visual microphone — recovering sound from the vibration of a crisp packet.

7 · Drill

Lagrangian vs Eulerian in one sentence each.

Lagrangian: follow each pixel through the image plane, which requires motion estimation (LK/HS). Eulerian: fix a location and observe how its value changes over time, which requires only temporal filtering. The chapter's phrasing: Lagrangian works in the image plane, Eulerian works in the time domain.

Where does the amplification actually come from? Why does adding $\alpha B$ move anything?

Because $B=\delta(t)\,\partial f/\partial x$ is a brightness change that is proportional to the displacement. Adding $\alpha$ copies gives $(1+\alpha)\delta\,\partial f/\partial x$, which by the same Taylor expansion is what the pixel would have read if the object had moved $(1+\alpha)\delta$. The pixel never moves; its value is set to what a bigger motion would have produced.

A perfectly uniform wall is swaying. What does the method output?

Nothing. $\partial f/\partial x = 0$ there, so $B=0$ regardless of $\delta$. Eulerian magnification only produces signal at edges and texture. Whatever appears on the wall in the output is amplified noise.

Why band-pass instead of amplifying all temporal change?

The diary holds four things at different speeds: DC (the picture), slow lighting drift, the target, and fast sensor noise — and the target is the smallest. Multiplying the whole signal by $\alpha$ multiplies drift and noise too, so the target stays buried and the output strobes. The band-pass deletes the competition so the amplification is spent entirely on the wanted signal.

Why does the DC term have to be removed, specifically?

$f(x)$ is the still picture, not motion. Amplifying it would multiply the brightness of the whole image by $(1+\alpha)$ and blow out the exposure, while adding nothing. Removing DC is the one thing even the crude $I_2-I_1$ gets right.

Your target is a 20 Hz engine vibration at 30 fps. What happens?

$f_{\max}=\text{fps}/2=15$ Hz, so 20 Hz is above Nyquist: it aliases and shows up as a fake ~10 Hz wiggle. No band recovers it. You need a camera faster than 40 fps.

Give an online implementation with $O(1)$ memory per pixel.

Two exponential moving averages with rates $a_1>a_2$: $L_i[n]=L_i[n-1]+a_i(x[n]-L_i[n-1])$, output $B[n]=L_1[n]-L_2[n]$. Each average is a low-pass; their difference is a band-pass. Two numbers of state per pixel, $O(1)$ work per frame, no stored frames.

You band-pass perfectly and still get halos around edges. Cause and fix?

Not a filtering problem: the motion exceeded the first-order Taylor validity of about a pixel, so $I+\alpha B$ is no longer a shifted copy of $f$. Fix by lowering $\alpha$, or by decomposing into a spatial pyramid and amplifying each scale with its own smaller $\alpha$ — coarse levels are smooth, so large shifts stay linear there.

Why is filtering the brightness in time the same as filtering the displacement in time?

Because $B(x,t)=\delta(t)\,\partial f(x)/\partial x$ and, for a fixed pixel, the spatial gradient is a constant. A linear filter along $t$ passes straight through a constant factor, so filtering $B$ equals filtering $\delta$ and re-multiplying. This is precisely why $\delta$ never has to be computed.

Compare the cost of this with Horn–Schunck for the same goal.

Horn–Schunck estimates dense flow by solving a sparse $2N\times2N$ system iteratively over the whole image, coupling every pixel to its neighbours. Eulerian magnification runs one independent 1-D filter per pixel with no coupling, no system and no iteration — and it succeeds on sub-pixel motion, where HS's own brightness constancy linearisation is swamped by noise.

Take it away in one line A shift of $\delta$ across a slope changes a pixel's value by $\delta\times$slope. Clean that change with a band-pass, multiply it by $\alpha$, add it back — and the picture moves as if by $(1+\alpha)\delta$. Motion amplified, motion never estimated.
