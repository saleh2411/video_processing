Paper deep-dive · Yair Weiss, ICCV 2001

Deriving Intrinsic Images from Image Sequences

Every photo is two invisible pictures multiplied together: what things are painted and how the light fell. Pulling them apart from one photo is impossible. Weiss showed that from a stack of photos it is still impossible — and then found the one extra belief about the world that makes it work anyway. The answer turns out to be four words long: take the median of the edges.

1 · The story: the photograph is lying to you

The painted wall

Imagine a long wall in a schoolyard. Somebody painted it: a red stripe, a blue square, a big white patch. That painting never changes. It is what the wall is.

Now it is a windy day with clouds. All morning, cloud shadows slide across the wall. Sometimes the red stripe is in bright sun and looks pink. Sometimes it is in shade and looks almost brown. The paint did not change — the light changed.

You take a photo every ten minutes. Every photo is a mix of two things:

  • the paint — the same in all photos
  • the light — different in every photo

The question of the whole paper: from your stack of photos, can you draw a picture of the paint alone, with all the cloud shadows erased?

The two invisible pictures have names

NameKid versionSymbol
Reflectancethe paint. What fraction of light a surface bounces back. A white shirt bounces ~0.9, black asphalt ~0.05. It belongs to the object.$R(x,y)$ — one picture, never changes
Illuminationthe light. How much light is landing at that spot right now — sun, shade, cloud shadow, a lamp. It belongs to the situation.$L(x,y,t)$ — one picture per frame
The photolight arriving × fraction bounced. So the camera records the product. $I(x,y,t) = R(x,y)\,L(x,y,t)$

These two are what Barrow and Tenenbaum (1978) called intrinsic images. The paper calls the split a midlevel description: it is not a full 3-D model of the scene (it never says why a spot is dark — cast shadow? object turned away? — and it depends on where you stood), but it is already enormously more useful than raw pixels.

Why anybody cares

  • Segmentation stops being fooled. The paper's Figure 1: a cylinder, evenly painted, gets chopped into pieces by any segmentation algorithm because shading makes one side dark. On the reflectance image "even primitive segmentation algorithms" get it as one object.
  • Recognition stops being brittle. Template matching and shape-from-shading break when the lighting changes. Run them on reflectance and the lighting is already gone.
  • Editing becomes physically believable. The paper's last figure: paste a logo onto a plaza photo with ordinary $\alpha$-blending and it looks stuck-on and fake. Paste it onto the reflectance image, then re-multiply by the recovered illumination, and the logo picks up the plaza's own shadows and lighting. It looks painted on the ground.

2 · Why this is impossible (and stays impossible)

The 12 = ? × ? game

I tell you two numbers multiply to 12. What are they?

$3 \times 4$. Or $2 \times 6$. Or $1 \times 12$. Or $12 \times 1$. Or $0.5 \times 24$.

You cannot know. One equation, two unknowns. Not hard — impossible. Every photo hands you exactly this puzzle at every single pixel.

Formally: one image gives one number per pixel and asks for two, so the unknowns are twice the equations. And there is always a cheating answer sitting right there:

$$ R = I, \qquad L = 1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad R\,L = I \;\;\checkmark $$

"Everything is paint, the light was perfectly even everywhere." Absurd, but it fits the data perfectly. This is what ill-posed means: not "difficult", but "the data does not contain the answer."

Surely a whole stack of photos fixes it?

It does not. Count again, at one pixel, with $T$ frames:

Equations$I_1 = R\,L_1,\; I_2 = R\,L_2,\; \dots,\; I_T = R\,L_T$  →  $T$
Unknowns$R$, plus $L_1,\dots,L_T$  →  $T+1$
Verdictalways exactly one short, forever

And the cheat still works: set $R = 1$ and $L_t = I_t$. Adding frames does not close the gap — it opens a new unknown every time you add an equation. This is the paper's own opening move, and it is the reason a prior is unavoidable.

The freedom, stated exactly Pick any non-zero picture $\alpha(x,y)$ you like. Then $R' = R\cdot\alpha$ and $L'_t = L_t/\alpha$ reproduce every frame perfectly, because $R'L'_t = R\alpha L_t/\alpha = R L_t = I_t$. There is an infinite family of perfect answers, and the data cannot rank them. Play with it below.

3 · The ideas that came before, and exactly how each one breaks

The paper's introduction is a graveyard. Worth knowing, because every wrong answer here is a tempting exam answer.

AttemptThe assumption it makesWhere it dies
Retinex
Land & McCann '71		 Reflectance is piecewise constant (a "Mondrian" of flat patches), and illumination is low contrast — soft and gradual. So: sharp edge = paint, soft gradient = light. Weiss checked it on outdoor scenes and says it plainly: illumination edges are often just as sharp and high-contrast as reflectance edges. The crisp black boundary of a cast shadow on a sunny day destroys the whole premise.
Freeman & Viola '98A wavelet prior used to classify an image as all-reflectance or all-illumination.Answers a yes/no question, does not produce the decomposition.

Yuille et al., Hayakawa		 The scene is a single convex Lambertian surface, so $L_t = \max(\mathbf n\cdot \mathbf s_t, 0)$ — only attached shadows, no cast shadows. Solvable by SVD. Real scenes are not one convex blob. Also leaves a Generalized-Bas-Relief ambiguity.
Farid & Adelson '99All illumination images are the same picture times a number: $L_t = c_t L$. Solved with ICA.A moving cloud shadow is not a global brightness knob. Fails the moment the shape of the lighting changes.

The common thread of every failure Min, mean, and median-of-images all ask "what is this pixel's honest brightness?" — and they all need the pixel to be honestly lit at some point. Weiss's move is to stop asking about brightness at all.

4 · Trick one: take a logarithm and multiplication becomes addition

Products are awkward. Logarithms turn them into sums, so the paper works in the log domain throughout. Write lowercase for logs:

$$ i = \log I, \quad r = \log R, \quad l = \log L \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \underbrace{I = R\cdot L}_{\text{product}} \;\;\longrightarrow\;\; \underbrace{i = r + l}_{\text{sum}} $$

So at every pixel, over time:

$$ i(x,y,t) \;=\; \underbrace{r(x,y)}_{\text{same in every frame}} \;+\; \underbrace{l(x,y,t)}_{\text{changes every frame}} $$

This is now a signal-plus-noise problem, which is a shape we know how to attack: a constant we want, buried under something that varies. "Get rid of the light" has become "get rid of the time-varying part". (Logs also match how cameras and eyes behave, and the paper notes the sparse statistics it relies on survive the log — the histograms in its Figure 3 are computed on log images.)

5 · Trick two: the one belief about the world that saves everything

We are one equation short, so we must add a belief. Weiss's first instinct was Retinex's belief ("illumination is low contrast") and he rejected it — see the table above. He needed something weaker and more generic. He took it from the statistics of natural images.

The kid version of "sparse derivative outputs"

Walk along one row of any real photograph and ask at every step: "did the brightness change from the pixel before?"

The answer is almost always "no, basically not at all." Sky next to sky. Wall next to wall. Cheek next to cheek. Then very occasionally — bang — you cross an edge and the answer is "enormously".

So a picture of "changes" is mostly zeros with rare big spikes. That is what sparse means. It is one of the most reliably true facts about real images, and it is not a fact about paint or about light — it is a fact about pictures.

The paper's Figure 3 is exactly this: a face and an outdoor scene, wildly different pictures, produce almost the same histogram of horizontal-derivative outputs — a huge spike at zero with tails that fall off far faster than a Gaussian. That shape is well fit by a Laplacian density:

$$ P(x) = \tfrac{1}{Z} e^{-\alpha |x|} $$

Compare with a Gaussian $\propto e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}$: the Gaussian's $x^2$ punishes big values so brutally that it essentially forbids them, which forces everything to be middling. The Laplacian's $|x|$ is gentle about outliers, so it is happy with "almost all exactly zero, a few enormous". Laplacian = the maths of sparse.

Now read the assumption in the paper's exact words

Take $N$ filters $\{f_n\}$ (Weiss uses exactly two: horizontal and vertical derivative). Define the filtered input and filtered reflectance:

$$ o_n(x,y,t) = i \star f_n, \qquad r_n(x,y) = r \star f_n $$

The assumption is: the filter outputs applied to $l$ are Laplacian and independent over space and time. Not $l$ itself — the filtered $l$. Hold on to that distinction; §7 is entirely about it.

6 · Claim 1 — and now the median falls out of the algebra

Filtering is linear, so applying $f_n$ to $i = r + l$ just splits:

$$ o_n(x,y,t) \;=\; \underbrace{r_n(x,y)}_{\text{constant in }t} \;+\; \underbrace{(f_n \star l)(x,y,t)}_{\text{sparse, changes with }t} $$

Read that as a measurement model: at each pixel, over time, you get $T$ noisy readings of the one number $r_n(x,y)$, and the noise is Laplacian. The whole problem has collapsed into: estimate a constant from noisy samples.

The likelihood (paper eq. 3–4)

Laplacian noise, independent over space and time, so the probability of everything you saw is a product of per-sample Laplacians:

$$ P(o_n \mid r_n) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z}\prod_{x,y,t} e^{-\beta\,|o_n(x,y,t) - r_n(x,y)|} \;=\; \frac{1}{Z}\, e^{-\beta \sum_{x,y,t} |o_n(x,y,t) - r_n(x,y)|} $$

Maximising that is minimising the exponent. The $Z$ and $\beta$ are constants, so:

$$ \hat r_n(x,y) \;=\; \arg\min_{c} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \bigl| o_n(x,y,t) - c \bigr| $$

A sum of absolute deviations — an $\ell_1$ problem. And the minimiser of a sum of absolute deviations is the median. That is Claim 1, paper equation 2:

$$ \boxed{\;\hat r_n(x,y) \;=\; \operatorname{median}_t \; o_n(x,y,t)\;} $$
Why does $\ell_1$ give the median (and $\ell_2$ the mean)? — one-line proof

Differentiate $g(c) = \sum_t |o_t - c|$. Each term contributes $-\operatorname{sign}(o_t - c)$, so

$$ g'(c) = \#\{t : o_t < c\} - \#\{t : o_t > c\} $$

This is zero exactly when the same number of samples lie below $c$ as above it — the definition of the median. Note the derivative only counts how many samples are on each side, never how far they are. That is precisely why the median ignores outliers: a sample that is wrong by a mile counts the same as one that is wrong by a hair.

Contrast $\ell_2$: $\frac{d}{dc}\sum (o_t - c)^2 = -2\sum(o_t - c) = 0 \Rightarrow c = \bar o$, the mean — where distance does count, so one wild sample drags the answer with it. Gaussian prior → mean → fragile. Laplacian prior → median → robust. The choice of prior is the choice of estimator.

Why the median is the perfect tool here A cloud shadow's edge passes over a given pixel during only a few of the frames. In those frames the filtered value is wildly off; in most frames it is clean. The median simply does not care about a minority of wild values — it asks "what did most frames agree on?" The moving shadow is outvoted.

7 · The one paragraph that separates this paper from a temporal median filter

Taking a temporal median of an image sequence is an old trick for building clean mosaics. So what is new? This is the crux, and it is the most likely thing to be asked about.

Two bricks, P and Q

Two bricks in the wall are painted exactly the same colour. But brick P sits in the open and is sunlit in every single photo. Brick Q is under the eaves and is shaded in every single photo.

Median of brightness: P's readings are all bright, so its median is bright. Q's readings are all dark, so its median is dark. Two different answers. Wrong — they are the same paint. And no amount of extra frames fixes it, because every frame is biased the same way.

Median of changes: now ask about edges instead. Between Q and the brick next to it there is no paint edge, so the reading is ≈ 0 in every frame. Between P and its neighbour, also ≈ 0. Same answer — correct. Being permanently dark is invisible to an edge detector, because a constant offset has zero derivative.

The paper states it precisely: pixels $P$ and $Q$ "are estimated as having the same reflectance even though one was always lighter than the other. This is because the ML estimate performs a temporal median on the filtered images, not the original images."

And then it names the assumption each choice secretly makes — this is the sentence to memorise:

EstimatorIs the ML estimate if you assume…True for real images?
temporal median of images $l(x,y,t)$ itself is sparse — i.e. most pixels are unshadowed, $l \approx 0$ Rarely. Whole regions sit in permanent shade; global light level drifts all day.
temporal median of filtered images $f_n \star l$ is sparse — i.e. the illumination is mostly smooth, with rare sharp edges Very often. This is the natural-image statistic of Figure 3.

Same operator, one filter apart, and a completely different assumption about the world. The contribution of the paper is which signal you median, not the median.

8 · Trick three: putting the picture back together from its edges

Claim 1 gives us $\hat r_n$ — the estimated filtered reflectance. Two of them: an estimated horizontal-derivative image and an estimated vertical-derivative image. But we wanted $r$ itself. We have the edges of the answer and need the answer.

The staircase

Someone tells you the height of every step of a staircase: +3, +3, −1, +5… Can you draw the staircase? Yes — start somewhere and add them up.

What you cannot know is how high the whole staircase sits above sea level. Steps tell you shape, never absolute height. Remember this — it comes back in a moment as the one ambiguity that survives.

In 2-D "adding up" is not so simple, because the horizontal and vertical estimates are computed independently and will not agree perfectly — there may be no image whose derivatives are exactly $\hat r_x$ and $\hat r_y$. So the paper sets it up as an over-constrained linear system (eq. 5) and takes the least-squares solution:

$$ f_n \star \hat r \;=\; \hat r_n \qquad \text{for all } n \qquad\qquad (5) $$

The pseudo-inverse solution is (eq. 6):

$$ \hat r \;=\; g \star \Bigl( \sum_n f^{\,r}_n \star \hat r_n \Bigr) \qquad\qquad (6) $$

where $f^{\,r}_n(x,y) = f_n(-x,-y)$ is the flipped filter, and $g$ solves (eq. 7):

$$ g \star \Bigl( \sum_n f^{\,r}_n \star f_n \Bigr) \;=\; \delta \qquad\qquad (7) $$
The line the paper puts right after eq. 7 — and why it matters "Note that $g$ is independent of the image sequence being considered and can be computed in advance." $g$ depends only on which filters you chose. Compute it once, ever, and reuse it on every video for the rest of time. This is what makes an online/real-time version possible at all — hold that thought for §12.
What is $g$, concretely? (It's the Poisson solver — this is what Demo 3 actually runs)

With $f_1 = $ horizontal derivative and $f_2 = $ vertical derivative, the operator $\sum_n f^{\,r}_n \star f_n$ is precisely the negative discrete Laplacian (the 5-point stencil). So eq. 7 says $g$ is the inverse Laplacian — the Green's function — and eq. 6 is exactly the statement

$$ \nabla^2 \hat r \;=\; \operatorname{div}\bigl(\hat r_x, \hat r_y\bigr) $$

i.e. solve a Poisson equation whose right-hand side is the divergence of the median gradient field. Exactly the same machinery as Poisson image editing / gradient-domain blending. Demo 3 solves it with Gauss–Seidel iterations in your browser.

The surviving ambiguity. The Laplacian has a null space: adding any constant to $\hat r$ changes nothing. So $r$ is recovered only up to an additive constant, which after $R = e^{r}$ means reflectance is recovered up to one global multiplicative constant — one unknown number for the whole image, instead of one unknown number per pixel. That is the staircase's sea level, and it is all that remains of the infinite ambiguity of Demo 1. In practice you fix it by a convention (e.g. force the brightest reflectance to 1).

And once you have $R$, illumination is free

$$ \hat l(x,y,t) = i(x,y,t) - \hat r(x,y) \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \hat L_t = I_t / \hat R $$

One division per frame. You get a reflectance image and a full stack of illumination images — the complete decomposition, which is what makes the relighting/compositing application possible.

9 · The whole algorithm, running

The recipe in five lines (this is the entire paper)
  1. Take logs: $i_t = \log I_t$.
  2. Filter every frame with horizontal and vertical derivatives: $o_n(\cdot,t) = i_t \star f_n$.
  3. At every pixel, take the median over time of each filter output → $\hat r_x, \hat r_y$.
  4. Reconstruct $\hat r$ from those two gradient images (eq. 6 — a Poisson solve with a precomputed $g$).
  5. $\hat R = e^{\hat r}$, and $\hat L_t = I_t/\hat R$.

10 · Claim 2 — you do not actually need the Laplacian to be true

A fair objection: what if $f_n \star l$ is not really Laplacian? Real shadows are not drawn from a textbook distribution. Claim 2 answers this, and it is the reason the method works in practice rather than only on paper.

Let $p_\epsilon = P\bigl(|f_i \star l(x,y,t)| < \epsilon\bigr)$ — plain English: "the chance that, at a random pixel in a random frame, the illumination's edge-response is tiny." That is a direct measure of sparseness, with no distribution assumed at all. Then the estimate is within $\epsilon$ of the truth with probability at least

$$ \sum_{k} \binom{T}{k} \, p_\epsilon^{\,k}\,(1-p_\epsilon)^{\,T-k} \qquad\text{summed over } k > T/2 $$

The proof is one sentence, and it is pure logic about medians: if more than 50% of the samples are within $\epsilon$ of some value, then by the definition of the median, the median must be within $\epsilon$ of that value too. The rest is just the binomial formula for $T$ independent coin flips.

Reading note on the printed formula In the paper the summation is typeset as $\sum_{k=1}^{T/2}\binom{T}{k}(1-p_\epsilon)^{T-k}p_\epsilon^{k}$ — the limits are transposed relative to the argument in the proof, which is about more than half the samples being close. Summing the upper tail ($k$ from just above $T/2$ to $T$) is what reproduces the paper's own quoted numbers: with $p_\epsilon = 0.85$ you get $\approx 0.94$ at $T=3$ and $\approx 0.973$ at $T=5$, matching its "at least 0.93 … at least 0.97". Use the upper tail.

The paper's worked example: the illumination image of its Figure 1c is not Laplacian, but it is very sparse — 85% of its filter outputs have magnitude below 1% of the maximum. Plugging $p_\epsilon = 0.85$ in: 93% confidence from three frames, 97% from five.

Why Claim 2 is the practical punchline It replaces a fragile assumption ("the noise is Laplacian") with a robust one ("the noise is mostly small"), and it converts sparseness directly into how many frames you need. The sparser the illumination changes, the faster the median converges. This is why a webcam sequence of 35 frames — or in the synthetic case, three — is enough.

What the paper itself lists as unfinished

  • Linear camera assumed. $I = R\cdot L$ only holds for a linear response. The webcam's auto-gain is named as the reason those results are weaker. The ML estimator can be re-derived for a known non-linear response.
  • Independence assumed across space and time, which is plainly false — shadow edges are long and connected, and consecutive frames are similar. Modelling the dependency is left open.
  • Still needs a sequence. Barrow and Tenenbaum's original question — one image, one decomposition — remains open. (And note the built-in blind spot: illumination that never changes across the whole sequence is mathematically indistinguishable from paint. A shadow that never moves stays baked into the reflectance.)

12 · Using all of this: exam Q3

Question 3 · Intrinsic Images · 13 points

Suggest an online algorithm for computing the intrinsic reflectance image $R_t$ from a sequence of images $I_1, \ldots, I_t$. The algorithm can store as many as 256 input images (or their equivalent in terms of memory footprint).

Hint: the intensity values of a pixel in images $I_1,\ldots,I_t$ are in the range $[0,255]$.

Step 1 — recognise the question

"Intrinsic reflectance image from a sequence" is Weiss, verbatim. So the offline answer is already known and is §9 above. The exam is not asking you to invent anything: it is asking you to make Weiss's algorithm streaming.

Step 2 — find the one part that is not online

Walk the five steps and audit each one for whether it needs the whole sequence:

StepOnline?Why
$i_t = \log I_t$yesper-frame, pointwise
filter: $o_n = i_t \star f_n$yesper-frame, local
$\operatorname{median}_t o_n$NOtextbook median needs every sample kept and sorted — memory grows with $t$, forever
reconstruct with $g$ (eq. 6)yesthe paper's own note: $g$ is independent of the sequence and precomputed once
$\hat R = e^{\hat r}$yespointwise

Exactly one blocker. The entire question reduces to: compute a running median in constant memory.

Step 3 — read the hint, because it is the answer

"Values are in $[0,255]$" + "you may store 256 images" is not a coincidence and not a red herring. It is telling you: 256 slots per pixel. The only classic structure with 256 slots over a 256-value alphabet is a histogram. And a histogram gives you a median without keeping any samples: sort order comes free from the bin index.

Why a histogram beats a list

To find the middle of a class's heights you could write down all 30 heights and sort them. Or you could keep 256 tally counters — one per centimetre — and shout "hands up if you are 152 cm". Then walk up the counters adding as you go, and stop when you have counted half the class. That is the median, and you never stored a single height. Works for 30 kids or 30 million, with the same 256 counters.

Demo 5 — the running median, one pixel, streaming

feed frames

One pixel's filter response arrives frame by frame — mostly a constant (its true $r_n$) with occasional wild values when a shadow edge sweeps over it. Bars are the 256-bin histogram; the teal line is the running cumulative count; the marker is where it crosses $t/2$ — that is the running median, readable at any moment. Watch the two memory counters: the naive one grows forever, the histogram one never moves.

Step 4 — the algorithm, written properly

State kept (fixed forever, independent of $t$): for every pixel and every filter $n$, a 256-bin histogram $H_n[x,y,\cdot]$ of the quantised filter response, plus a frame counter $t$.

  1. Receive frame $I_t$. Compute $i_t = \log I_t$ and the responses $o_n = i_t \star f_n$ for the horizontal and vertical derivative filters.
  2. Quantise and tally. Map $o_n(x,y)$ to a bin and increment $H_n[x,y,\text{bin}] \mathrel{+}= 1$. One increment per pixel per filter — $O(1)$ per pixel.
  3. Running median. $\hat r_n(x,y) = $ the value of the smallest bin whose cumulative count reaches $t/2$. (Keep a per-pixel cursor and it moves by ~1 bin per frame — amortised $O(1)$; or scan 256 bins, still constant work.)
  4. Reconstruct on demand. Feed the current $\hat r_x, \hat r_y$ through the precomputed $g$ (eq. 6–7) to get $\hat r$, then output $R_t = e^{\hat r}$. Because $g$ never changes, this is a fixed convolution / Poisson solve, available at any frame.
  5. Optionally emit $L_t = I_t / R_t$ as well, then discard $I_t$ — no frame is ever stored.

Step 5 — the wrinkles that separate a full mark from a partial one

1. The hint says images are in $[0,255]$ — but you histogram filter responses. Isn't that a mismatch?

Good catch, and saying so earns credit. The median is taken on $o_n = i \star f_n$, not on $I$. A difference of two log-intensities drawn from a 256-value alphabet takes on ~511 possible values, so a 512-bin histogram would be exact and 256 bins costs you a half-step of quantisation — utterly negligible against the sensor noise, and the quantisation error is bounded and known.

Two clean ways to answer: (a) use 256 bins over the response range and state the quantisation error is ≤ half a bin; or (b) note that since $I \in \{0,\dots,255\}$ you can equivalently keep the 256-bin histogram of the log-intensity itself per pixel and form differences of medians — but be careful, that is not the same estimator (median of differences ≠ difference of medians), so (a) is the faithful one. Prefer (a).

2. Two filters means two histograms — does that blow the 256-image budget?

Worth one sentence. A bin counter is not a pixel: pixels need 8 bits, counters need $\lceil \log_2 T \rceil$ bits. With 8-bit-equivalent accounting, 256 bins/pixel/filter = 256 images' worth, so two filters = 512 — twice the stated budget. Ways to stay inside it, any one of which is a fine answer: use 128 bins per filter (half a bin of extra quantisation); or note that counters saturating at 255 are enough if you down-weight old counts; or simply observe that the budget is stated as "or equivalent memory footprint" and show the arithmetic. Showing you counted the bytes is the point.

3. Why not just keep the last 256 frames in a ring buffer and median those?

It is a legal reading of the budget and worth mentioning — it gives a sliding-window median, which actually adapts to slow scene changes, a genuine advantage. But it is strictly worse on the exam's own terms: it costs 256 full frames instead of 256 counters per pixel, it re-sorts a 256-long list per pixel per frame instead of $O(1)$, and it caps your evidence at 256 frames instead of all of history. Mention it as a variant, choose the histogram.

4. Why does an online version even make sense here — doesn't the median need to see the shadows move?

Yes, and that is Claim 2 doing the work: the bound says confidence grows with $T$ and grows faster the sparser the illumination is, reaching ~93% at just three frames in the paper's example. So the streaming estimate is poor for the first couple of frames and good very quickly after. Saying this explicitly connects the algorithm to the theory and is exactly the kind of remark that reads as understanding rather than recall.

5. Anything else worth one line?

Three cheap ones: (i) $g$ is precomputed once and depends only on the filters — say it, it is the reason step 4 is online. (ii) $\hat r$ is recovered only up to an additive constant, so fix the gauge by a convention (e.g. $\max \hat R = 1$) and say so. (iii) Guard $\log 0$ — clamp $I$ to $[1,255]$ before the log.

The answer, as you would write it in the box

By Weiss (Ch. 12), with $I = R\cdot L$ and $i = r + l$, the ML filtered reflectance under a sparse prior on the filtered illumination is the per-pixel temporal median of the filtered log-frames, $\hat r_n(x,y) = \operatorname{median}_t (f_n \star i)(x,y,t)$, and $r$ is then recovered by the fixed pseudo-inverse deconvolution $\hat r = g \star \sum_n f^{\,r}_n \star \hat r_n$ with $g \star (\sum_n f^{\,r}_n \star f_n) = \delta$ precomputed once. Every step is per-frame except the median, so the median is the only obstacle to an online algorithm.

Since responses live in a 256-value range and we may store 256 images' worth, keep a 256-bin histogram per pixel per filter (Weiss uses two filters: horizontal and vertical derivatives). For each incoming frame: take the log, filter, increment one bin per pixel per filter ($O(1)$), and read the running median as the smallest bin whose cumulative count reaches $t/2$. Then apply the precomputed $g$ to the current medians and output $R_t = e^{\hat r}$; optionally $L_t = I_t/R_t$. Discard the frame.

Memory $256 \times \#\text{filters}$ counters per pixel, constant in $t$; work $O(1)$ per pixel per frame; $R_t$ emitted every frame. Fix the global scale by convention, and clamp before the log.

13 · Exam drill

Q. In one sentence, what is the contribution of this paper?

That taking the temporal median of derivative-filtered log-frames — rather than of the frames themselves — is the maximum-likelihood reflectance estimate under a sparse (Laplacian) prior on the filtered illumination, which is a prior that actually holds for natural images.

Q. Why is the median, rather than the mean, the right average here?

The prior is Laplacian ($e^{-\alpha|x|}$), so maximising the likelihood minimises a sum of absolute deviations, and the $\ell_1$ minimiser is the median. Physically: a shadow edge crosses a given pixel in only a minority of frames, so it is an outlier — and the median ignores outliers because its optimality condition counts samples on each side rather than measuring their distance. A Gaussian prior would give the mean, which the shadow would drag along with it.

Q. A pixel is in shadow in every frame. What do min, mean, median-of-images, and Weiss report?

The first three all report reflectance that is too dark, because every sample they average is biased the same way and no amount of extra frames helps. Weiss gets it right: a permanent constant offset in $l$ has zero derivative, so it is invisible to the filters and never enters the median at all. Verify it yourself on markers P and Q in Demo 3.

Q. What is the difference between assuming "$l$ is sparse" and "$f_n \star l$ is sparse"?

"$l$ is sparse" means most pixels are unshadowed, $l \approx 0$ — rarely true, and it is precisely the assumption under which a plain temporal median of the images is the ML estimate. "$f_n \star l$ is sparse" means the illumination is mostly smooth, with rare sharp edges — usually true, and it makes the median of the filtered images the ML estimate. One filter apart; totally different claim about the world.

Q. Why do we need the reconstruction step at all, and what does $g$ depend on?

The median gives us the reflectance's derivatives, not the reflectance. Recovering an image from its gradients is an over-constrained least-squares problem (the horizontal and vertical estimates need not be consistent), whose pseudo-inverse solution is eq. 6. $g$ depends only on the chosen filters — not on the data — so it is computed once in advance; with derivative filters $\sum_n f^{\,r}_n\star f_n$ is the negative Laplacian and the step is a Poisson solve.

Q. How much of the original ambiguity survives, and where does it live?

Almost none. We started with a free multiplier $\alpha(x,y)$ at every pixel (Demo 1). We end with a single global constant, because the Laplacian's null space is the constants: $\hat r$ is determined up to an additive constant, so $\hat R$ is determined up to one multiplicative constant for the whole image. Fixed by convention, not by data.

Q. Name a scene where this method must fail.

Any scene where the illumination never changes — a static shadow across the whole sequence is mathematically identical to dark paint, and it will be baked into the reflectance. Equally: a camera with a strongly non-linear response, which breaks $I = R\cdot L$ (the paper blames exactly this for its weaker webcam results), or a scene where the shadows are so dense that $p_\epsilon < 0.5$, in which case the median locks onto the illumination instead.
