Why tracking only ever needs yesterday

This is the single most important equation in the tracking chapter, and it is a three-line proof once you know which two assumptions to invoke. The whole point: without them, tracking would need the entire history at every frame; with them, it becomes a recursion you can run forever in constant memory. Story, the two assumptions stated precisely, the derivation line by line, and a live filter you can step.

Moed B · 1/8/2022 · Question 2 · 12 points · TRACKING

Write down the Markov model assumption and the Conditional Independence of observations assumption and show that:

$$ p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t}\mid \mathbf{z}_{1:t}) \;\propto\; p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_t)\, p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1})\, p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1}\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}) \tag{1} $$

1 · The story

The kid with the diary and the terrible eyes

A puppy is running around the garden. You want to know where it is. You have two things:

  • $\mathbf{x}_t$ — the truth. Where the puppy really is at time $t$. You never see this. It is hidden.
  • $\mathbf{z}_t$ — what you saw. A blurry glance through a dirty window at time $t$. You do see this.

By evening you have a whole diary of glances $\mathbf{z}_1,\dots,\mathbf{z}_t$, and you want the whole story of the puppy's day, $\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_t$. That is exactly what $p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t}\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t})$ means: how likely is this whole story, given everything I saw?

Now the two beliefs that make life easy:

Belief 1 — the puppy has no memory (Markov). To guess where the puppy will be next, you only need to know where it is now. Knowing where it was at breakfast adds nothing once you know it is standing by the tree right now. A puppy standing by the tree will do the same thing whether it arrived from the left or from the right.

Belief 2 — each glance only reports the present (conditional independence). The blurriness of the glance you take right now depends only on where the puppy is right now. The dirty window doesn't remember yesterday's glances, and it doesn't know the puppy's plans. So given the true position now, this glance tells you nothing extra about any other glance.

Put them together and something wonderful happens: you never have to re-read your diary. Yesterday's belief plus one motion guess plus one new glance = today's belief. That is equation (1), and it is why a tracker can run for a million frames on a phone.

Read equation (1) out loud new belief about the whole story = how well the new glance fits ($p(\mathbf{z}_t|\mathbf{x}_t)$) × how plausible the step from yesterday to today is ($p(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})$) × yesterday's belief about the story so far ($p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})$). Three factors, one new frame each. Nothing else survives.

Assumption 1 — the Markov (state transition) model

The state at time $t$ depends on the past only through the immediately preceding state:

$$ p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{1:t-1}) = p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1}) \tag{2} $$

and, in the form actually needed below (which also follows from the graph, since the past observations influence $\mathbf{x}_t$ only through $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$, which we are conditioning on):

$$ p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{1:t-1},\,\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}) = p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1}) \tag{2'} $$
Assumption 2 — conditional independence of observations

Given the states, observations are mutually independent and each depends only on its own state:

$$ p(\mathbf{z}_{1:t}\mid \mathbf{x}_{1:t}) = \prod_{k=1}^{t} p(\mathbf{z}_k\mid\mathbf{x}_k) \tag{3} $$

whose one-step consequence — the form used in the proof — is

$$ p(\mathbf{z}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{1:t},\,\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}) = p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_t) \tag{3'} $$

3 · The derivation

Start from the definition of conditional probability and split off the newest frame. Everything below is just Bayes' rule plus the chain rule; the two assumptions are used exactly once each, and they are flagged.

  1. Bayes. Write the posterior as a joint over a normaliser:
    $$ p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t}\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t}) = \frac{p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t},\,\mathbf{z}_{1:t})}{p(\mathbf{z}_{1:t})} $$
  2. Chain rule on the numerator, peeling off $\mathbf{z}_t$ then $\mathbf{x}_t$:
    $$ p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t},\mathbf{z}_{1:t}) = p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid \mathbf{x}_{1:t},\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})\; p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid \mathbf{x}_{1:t-1},\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})\; p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1},\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}) $$
    This step is exact — no assumptions yet.
  3. Apply the two assumptions. The first factor collapses by (3′), the second by (2′):
    $$ p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t},\mathbf{z}_{1:t}) = \underbrace{p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_t)}_{\text{cond. indep. (3')}}\;\underbrace{p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1})}_{\text{Markov (2')}}\; p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1},\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}) $$
  4. Re-expand the tail as a posterior times an evidence:
    $$ p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1},\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}) = p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1}\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})\; p(\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}) $$
  5. Factor the normaliser the same way:
    $$ p(\mathbf{z}_{1:t}) = p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})\, p(\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}) $$
  6. Divide and cancel $p(\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})$:
    $$ p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t}\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t}) = \frac{p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_t)\,p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1})\,p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1}\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})}{p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})} $$
  7. Drop the denominator. $p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})$ does not depend on $\mathbf{x}_{1:t}$ — it is a constant as far as the distribution over trajectories is concerned (it is the integral of the numerator over all $\mathbf{x}_{1:t}$). Hence
    $$ \boxed{\;p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t}\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t}) \;\propto\; p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_t)\,p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1})\,p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1}\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})\;} \qquad \blacksquare $$
Where each assumption did its job Without (3′) the first factor would stay $p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{1:t},\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})$ — you would need every past frame to evaluate the new one. Without (2′) the second factor would stay $p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1},\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})$ — you would need the whole trajectory to predict one step. Each assumption deletes one growing history. Together they turn an $O(t)$ evaluation into an $O(1)$ one.

4 · The corollary everyone actually uses

Equation (1) is about the whole trajectory. Marginalising $\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1}$ out of it gives the filtering recursion — the two-line predict/update loop that Kalman and particle filters implement:

$$ \underbrace{p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}) = \int p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1})\, p(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})\, d\mathbf{x}_{t-1}}_{\textbf{predict} \;\text{(Chapman–Kolmogorov)}} \tag{4} $$
$$ \underbrace{p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t}) = \frac{p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_t)\,p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})}{p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})}}_{\textbf{update} \;\text{(Bayes)}} \tag{5} $$

5 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

One page, as the exam asks

Assumption 1 (Markov model / first-order state transition). The state is a complete summary of the past: the next state depends on the history only through the current state,

$$ p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1},\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}) = p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1}). $$

Assumption 2 (conditional independence of observations). Given the states, observations are independent of one another and each depends only on the state at its own time,

$$ p(\mathbf{z}_{1:t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{1:t}) = \prod_{k=1}^{t} p(\mathbf{z}_k\mid\mathbf{x}_k) \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{1:t},\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}) = p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_t). $$

Derivation. By Bayes' rule and the chain rule, splitting off the newest frame,

$$ p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t}\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t}) = \frac{p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t},\mathbf{z}_{1:t})}{p(\mathbf{z}_{1:t})} = \frac{p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{1:t},\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})\,p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1},\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})\,p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1},\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})}{p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})\,p(\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})}. $$

Apply Assumption 2 to the first numerator factor and Assumption 1 to the second, and write $p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1},\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}) = p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1}\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})\,p(\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})$. The factor $p(\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})$ cancels top and bottom:

$$ p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t}\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t}) = \frac{p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_t)\,p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1})\,p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1}\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})}{p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})}. $$

The remaining denominator is independent of $\mathbf{x}_{1:t}$ (it is the normalising evidence, obtained by integrating the numerator over all trajectories), therefore

$$ p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t}\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t}) \propto p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_t)\,p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1})\,p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1}\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}). \qquad\blacksquare $$

Significance. The posterior over the whole trajectory is obtained from the previous posterior by multiplying two local factors only. Nothing depends on $\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}$ explicitly, so the tracker needs constant memory and constant work per frame. Marginalising $\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1}$ gives the standard predict/update filtering recursion $p(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}) = \int p(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})p(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})d\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ and $p(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{z}_{1:t}) \propto p(\mathbf{z}_t|\mathbf{x}_t)p(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})$, which the Kalman filter solves in closed form (linear-Gaussian case) and Condensation approximates with weighted particles.

6 · Exam drill

Q. Why $\propto$ and not $=$? What exactly was thrown away?

The evidence $p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}) = \int p(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_t)p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})d\mathbf{x}_t$. It is a number, not a function of $\mathbf{x}$, so it rescales every trajectory equally and cannot change which trajectory is most probable. It is not useless, though: it is the likelihood of the new measurement under the model, and its running product $\prod_t p(\mathbf{z}_t|\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})$ is the model evidence — used for failure detection ("this frame is very surprising, the target may be occluded or lost") and for comparing motion models.

Q. In the particle filter, which factor does each of the three stages implement?

Condensation's three stages line up one-to-one with the recursion. Select (resample from the weighted set) realises $p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})$ — it turns last frame's weighted representation into an unweighted sample of it. Predict (push each particle through the dynamics plus noise) applies $p(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})$. Measure (weight each particle by the image evidence, $\pi^{(n)} \propto p(\mathbf{z}_t|\mathbf{x}_t^{(n)})$, then normalise) applies $p(\mathbf{z}_t|\mathbf{x}_t)$ — and the normalisation is exactly the $\propto$ we dropped.

Q. What if the motion is second order, e.g. constant acceleration?

Then $p(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})$ is false as stated — position alone doesn't predict the next position. The fix is never to abandon the Markov assumption but to enlarge the state: take $\mathbf{x}_t = (\text{position},\text{velocity})^{\!\top}$, or $(\text{position},\text{velocity},\text{acceleration})$. In the new state the process is first-order Markov again and equation (1) holds verbatim. This is the standard trick: "the state is whatever you must know about the past to predict the future", and any finite-order dependence can be absorbed by stacking. In the Kalman filter this is the constant-velocity model $A=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&\Delta t\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]$.

Q. Give a real case where the observation independence assumption fails.

A fixed occluder — a lamp post. When the target passes behind it, several consecutive measurements are bad, and they are bad for a common reason that is not the target's state. Given $\mathbf{x}_t$, $\mathbf{z}_t$ and $\mathbf{z}_{t+1}$ are then still correlated, violating (3). Same for a mis-calibrated sensor, a static background clutter patch that looks like the target, or JPEG blocking artefacts. Consequences: the filter becomes overconfident, because it treats $k$ correlated errors as $k$ independent votes. Remedies: model the occluder in the state (add a visibility flag, making the model Markov again in the bigger state), inflate $R$, or use a robust/heavy-tailed likelihood so a run of bad frames cannot dominate.

Q. The trajectory posterior vs the filtering posterior — what's the difference, and why do particle filters degrade on the first?

$p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t})$ lives in a space whose dimension grows with $t$; $p(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{z}_{1:t})$ does not. A particle filter technically samples the trajectory version (each particle carries an ancestral path), and repeated resampling means all particles eventually share one common ancestor — so the early part of the trajectory posterior collapses to a single sample. This is path degeneracy. It doesn't hurt the filtering marginal at recent times, which is what tracking needs, but it means you cannot trust a particle filter's estimate of where the target was 200 frames ago. Fixing that requires smoothing (forward–backward), not filtering.

Q. Prove the predict equation (4) from (1).

Marginalise (1) over $\mathbf{x}_{1:t-2}$ to get the joint of the last two states, $p(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}) = p(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})p(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})$ (using the pre-measurement version of the recursion), then marginalise $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$: $p(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}) = \int p(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})p(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})\,d\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$. That is Chapman–Kolmogorov. Then multiply by $p(\mathbf{z}_t|\mathbf{x}_t)$ and normalise for (5).

Q. Is the initial condition part of the assumptions?

Yes — the recursion needs a starting point $p(\mathbf{x}_1)$ (the prior), otherwise there is nothing to recurse from. Formally the model is the triple $\big(p(\mathbf{x}_1),\, p(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{t-1}),\, p(\mathbf{z}_t|\mathbf{x}_t)\big)$, and at $t=1$ equation (1) degenerates to $p(\mathbf{x}_1|\mathbf{z}_1) \propto p(\mathbf{z}_1|\mathbf{x}_1)p(\mathbf{x}_1)$, plain Bayes. In practice this is the detection that initialises the tracker; a broad prior means "I don't know where it is", and the filter concentrates it over the first few frames.
