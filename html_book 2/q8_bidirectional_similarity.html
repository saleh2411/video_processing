Companion page to Chapter 11 · Moed A, question 8

"How far is A from B?" is not the same question as "how far is B from A?"

Twelve points for one two-point counter-example — as long as you find it. Story, a demo where you drag the points yourself, then the proof and the reason the course cares.

Moed A · 19/6/2022 · Question 8 · 12 points · BIDIRECTIONAL SIMILARITY

Given point sets $P = \{p_1,\dots,p_n\}$ and $Q = \{q_1,\dots,q_m\}$ in some Euclidean space, define the "distance" $D(P,Q)$:

$$ D(P,Q) = \max_{p \in P} d(p, Q), \quad\text{with}\tag{3} $$
$$ d(p,Q) = \min_{q \in Q} d(p,q) \tag{4} $$

and $d(p,q)$ is the usual Euclidean distance between points $p$ and $q$. Is $D$ symmetric? That is, show that $D(P,Q) = D(Q,P)$ or give a counter-example.

1 · First, read the definition out loud

Two lines, two different words: min and max

Inner (4): $d(p,Q) = \min_q d(p,q)$ — "from this one point $p$, how far is the nearest member of $Q$?" That's one number per point of $P$.

Outer (3): $D(P,Q) = \max_p d(p,Q)$ — "over all points of $P$, which one is the worst off?" i.e. which point of $P$ is most abandoned by $Q$.

Put together in one sentence:

$D(P,Q)$ in plain words "How far do I have to travel from the loneliest point of $P$ to reach anything in $Q$?"
Or equivalently: how big does a ball around each point of $Q$ have to be before those balls cover all of $P$?

2 · The story

The village and the country

$P$ is one tiny village. $Q$ is a whole country — and the village happens to sit inside the country's borders.

Ask the villager: "how far to the nearest bit of the country?" Answer: zero. You are standing in it. So $D(\text{village}, \text{country}) = 0$.

Now ask the country: "over all your citizens, who is furthest from the village?" That's somebody way out at the far border, a thousand kilometres away. So $D(\text{country}, \text{village}) = 1000$.

Same two sets. Two completely different numbers. Nothing was computed wrongly — the two questions were simply never the same question.

Where the asymmetry comes from, structurally The $\max$ runs over the first argument and the $\min$ over the second. Swapping $P$ and $Q$ swaps which set gets to nominate the worst case and which set gets to be the target. An expression that treats its two arguments differently has no reason to be symmetric — that observation alone is most of the answer, and the counter-example just proves it.

Try to make them equal on purpose Press "A symmetric case". Equality can happen — e.g. two identical sets, or two sets whose worst-case gaps coincide. That is exactly why the question says "show that $D(P,Q)=D(Q,P)$ or give a counter-example": occasional equality proves nothing. One counter-example kills symmetry for good.

3 · The answer

The smallest counter-example there is

Work on the real line. Take

$$ P = \{0\}, \qquad Q = \{0, 10\} $$

Direction 1. $P$ has one point, $p = 0$. Its nearest neighbour in $Q$:

$$ d(0, Q) = \min\{\,d(0,0),\, d(0,10)\,\} = \min\{0, 10\} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; D(P,Q) = \max\{0\} = 0 $$

Direction 2. $Q$ has two points, each measured to the only point of $P$:

$$ d(0,P) = 0, \qquad d(10,P) = 10 \;\Longrightarrow\; D(Q,P) = \max\{0, 10\} = 10 $$
$$ \boxed{\;D(P,Q) = 0 \;\neq\; 10 = D(Q,P)\;} $$

So $D$ is not symmetric. $\blacksquare$

Note how extreme this is: $D(P,Q)=0$ while $D(Q,P)$ can be made as large as you like by moving the second point of $Q$ further away. The two directions aren't just occasionally different — they can disagree arbitrarily.

Bonus observations, cheap to add and they read well

• $D(P,Q) = 0 \iff P \subseteq Q$ (for finite sets). So $D(P,Q)=0$ says "$P$ is contained in $Q$", which is a one-directional statement by nature — containment is not symmetric, so $D$ cannot be either.

• $D$ is the directed Hausdorff distance. Symmetrising it by $D_H(P,Q) = \max\{D(P,Q),\,D(Q,P)\}$ gives the Hausdorff distance, which is a genuine metric on compact sets. That is the standard repair.

• $D$ also fails to be a metric for a second reason: $D(P,Q)=0$ does not imply $P=Q$ (take $P=\{0\}, Q=\{0,10\}$ again).

4 · Why the course asks this

This is completeness vs coherence, in disguise

The asymmetry you just proved is exactly the reason Chapter 11's retargeting needs two terms instead of one. With $S$ the source image and $T$ the target, patches playing the role of points:

$$ D_{com}(S,T) = \frac{1}{N_S}\sum_{P \in S}\ \min_{Q \in T} d^2(P,Q), \qquad D_{coh}(S,T) = \frac{1}{N_T}\sum_{Q \in T}\ \min_{P \in S} d^2(P,Q) $$

Same shape as this question — an outer aggregation over one set, an inner $\min$ over the other — and the two directions mean genuinely different things:

  • Completeness ($S \to T$): every patch of the original should appear somewhere in the output. Violated ⇒ you lost content.
  • Coherence ($T \to S$): every patch of the output should have come from somewhere in the original. Violated ⇒ you invented content (artefacts).

Neither alone is enough: minimising only completeness lets you duplicate everything; minimising only coherence lets you output a patch of sky and call it a day. So Chapter 11 symmetrises them exactly the way you'd repair $D$:

$$ D(S,T) = \alpha\, D_{com}(S,T) + (1-\alpha)\, D_{coh}(S,T) $$

If $D$ had been symmetric, one term would have sufficed and the whole bidirectional-similarity idea would not exist. Saying this in the exam turns a two-line answer into a complete one.

(One honest difference worth noting: Chapter 11 aggregates the inner distances with a sum/average, whereas this question uses a $\max$. The asymmetry argument is identical either way — it comes from the outer aggregation ranging over one set and the inner $\min$ over the other, not from which aggregator you pick.)

5 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

Short — but give the structural reason, not just the numbers

No, $D$ is not symmetric.

Why to expect it. In $D(P,Q) = \max_{p\in P}\min_{q\in Q} d(p,q)$ the outer $\max$ ranges over the first argument while the inner $\min$ ranges over the second. The two arguments play different roles, so there is no reason for the value to be preserved under swapping them.

Counter-example. On $\mathbb{R}$, let $P = \{0\}$ and $Q = \{0,10\}$. Then $d(0,Q) = \min\{0,10\} = 0$, so $D(P,Q) = 0$; while $d(0,P)=0$ and $d(10,P)=10$, so $D(Q,P) = \max\{0,10\} = 10$. Since $0 \neq 10$, $D(P,Q) \neq D(Q,P)$. $\blacksquare$

Remarks. $D$ is the directed Hausdorff distance; $D(P,Q)=0$ iff $P \subseteq Q$, which is inherently one-directional. It is symmetrised by $D_H(P,Q) = \max\{D(P,Q), D(Q,P)\}$, the Hausdorff distance, which is a true metric.

Relevance. This is precisely the completeness/coherence asymmetry of Chapter 11: $D_{com}(S,T)$ penalises losing content from the source, $D_{coh}(S,T)$ penalises inventing content in the target, and they are different quantities. Retargeting therefore optimises the symmetrised combination $\alpha D_{com} + (1-\alpha)D_{coh}$.

6 · Exam drill

Q. Give the counter-example from memory, with both numbers.

$P=\{0\}$, $Q=\{0,10\}$ on the real line. $D(P,Q)=\max\{\min\{0,10\}\}=0$. $D(Q,P)=\max\{d(0,P),d(10,P)\}=\max\{0,10\}=10$. Not equal, so not symmetric.

Q. Explain the asymmetry without using numbers.

$D(P,Q)$ asks "how abandoned is the loneliest point of $P$ by the set $Q$". The $\max$ is taken over the first set and the $\min$ over the second, so the first set nominates the worst case and the second merely acts as a target. Swapping the arguments swaps those two roles, which is a genuinely different question — like asking "how far is the village from the country" versus "how far is the country from the village".

Q. When is $D(P,Q) = D(Q,P)$?

Trivially when $P = Q$ (both are 0), and more generally whenever the two worst-case gaps happen to coincide — e.g. two sets that are reflections of one another about a common configuration. Such cases exist, which is why the question offers "prove it or find a counter-example": equality in particular instances is not a proof of symmetry, while one counter-example is a disproof.

Q. What does $D(P,Q) = 0$ tell you?

That every point of $P$ has distance 0 to $Q$, i.e. $P \subseteq Q$ for finite sets (more generally, $P$ is contained in the closure of $Q$). Containment is a one-directional relation, which is another way of seeing immediately that $D$ cannot be symmetric — and also shows $D$ is not a metric, since $D(P,Q)=0$ does not force $P = Q$.

Q. How do you repair it into a real distance?

Take the larger of the two directions: $D_H(P,Q) = \max\{D(P,Q), D(Q,P)\}$ — the Hausdorff distance. It is symmetric by construction, is zero only when the sets are equal (for compact sets), and satisfies the triangle inequality, so it is a genuine metric. Chapter 11's retargeting uses the analogous repair, a weighted sum of the two directions.

Q. Which of the two directions corresponds to completeness, and which to coherence?

Measuring from source patches to the target ($S \to T$) is completeness: every patch of the original should still be found in the output, so violating it means content was lost. Measuring from target patches to the source ($T \to S$) is coherence: every patch of the output should come from the original, so violating it means content was invented — visible as artefacts.

Q. What goes wrong if a retargeting method optimises only one of them?

Only completeness: the output is rewarded for containing everything from the source, so it can duplicate content repeatedly and produce incoherent junk that nonetheless "covers" the source. Only coherence: the output is rewarded for having every patch explainable by the source, which a uniform patch of sky satisfies perfectly — so entire objects can vanish. Both terms are needed, weighted by $\alpha$.
