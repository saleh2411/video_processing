Companion page to Chapter 9 · built around Moed A, question 7

Colouring a cloud of dots

First: what the question is even asking, in ordinary words. Then a story, then three demos you can press, then the math. By the end this should feel like the easiest 13 points on the page.

Moed A · 19/6/2022 · Question 7 · 13 points · SEGMENTATION

Consider Figure 5. The left part shows two 3D point clouds (a plane and a motorcycle) with user scribbles superimposed (diamond-shaped points with different colours). A 3D point cloud is simply an unordered list of 3D points. Suggest an algorithm that takes a 3D point cloud and user scribbles and produces the segmentation results seen on the right side of the figure.

1 · What is the question even asking?

Three nouns to unpack. None of them are hard, they just sound hard.

WordWhat it actually is
3D point cloudA bag of dots. Literally a list: (x,y,z), (x,y,z), … That's it. If you scanned a motorcycle with a laser, you'd get a few thousand dots floating in space in the shape of a motorcycle. No pixels, no grid, no order, and — crucially — nobody tells you which dot is next to which.
User scribblesA person clicked on a handful of dots and marked them: "these few are wing", "these few are body", "these few are tail". The diamonds in the figure. Maybe 20 dots out of 5000. They are seeds.
The expected outputGive every single dot a label. The right side of the figure is the same cloud with every dot coloured by which part it belongs to. Nothing is left grey.
So the task in one sentence A few dots are labelled. Spread those labels to all the other dots, sensibly. That's the entire question. "Sensibly" is where the 13 points live.

2 · The story

The playground with no map

Imagine thousands of little stepping stones floating in the air, arranged in the shape of a motorcycle. You are standing on one of them. Somebody has painted a few stones red and a few stones blue. Every other stone is grey.

Your job: paint every grey stone either red or blue.

The rule a five-year-old would invent, and it's the right one: "paint me the colour of whichever painted stone I can walk to fastest."

Walk. Not fly. That word is the entire answer.

Why "walk" and not "fly"

Think of a motorcycle. The front wheel and the handlebar are very close together as the crow flies — a few centimetres of empty air. But to actually travel from one to the other along the metal, you go down the fork, along the frame, and back up. They are near in space and far along the object.

If you measure "fastest" by straight-line distance, the wheel's colour leaks onto the handlebar through empty air. If you measure it by walking along the object, it doesn't. That is the whole difference between the wrong answer and the right one.

Straight-line distance has a name: Euclidean. Walking-along-the-object distance has a name: geodesic. Chapter 9 is entirely about the second one.

A horseshoe-shaped cloud of dots (think: a bent piece of a motorcycle frame). Two scribbles: red at the top tip, blue on the far side. The two tips are close through the air but very far along the shape. Watch what each rule does to the bottom tip.

Read the left panel again Euclidean labelling paints the bottom tip red — because red is only a short hop away across the gap. But you cannot get there without leaving the object. Geodesic labelling walks the long way round and correctly calls it blue. Same dots, same scribbles, one word changed.

3 · But wait — how do you "walk" on a bag of dots?

Building the bridges yourself

Here is the catch, and it is the one genuinely new thing in this question. In Chapter 9 you propagated colour across an image. An image is a grid — every pixel already knows its four neighbours. Walking is obvious: step left, right, up, down.

A point cloud is an unordered list. Dot #417 has no idea which dot is beside it. There are no neighbours. There is nothing to walk along.

So you build the walkways yourself: for each dot, find the handful of dots physically nearest to it, and lay a little bridge to each. Do that for every dot and suddenly the bag of dots is a connected network you can walk on.

That's a k-nearest-neighbour graph. It is the only step in this whole question that isn't already in Chapter 9 — and it is the step that earns you the points, because it is exactly what the examiner is checking you noticed.

And k matters — a lot

k too small → the network falls apart into disconnected islands. A dot in an island with no scribble can never be reached: its distance is infinite and it gets no label.

k too big → you start building bridges across empty air, from the wheel straight to the handlebar. Now the "walk" can cheat through the gap and you're back to the Euclidean mistake, just slower.

4 · Now spread the paint

Two floods racing

Pour red water onto the red stones and blue water onto the blue stones at the same moment. Both floods spread outwards along the bridges, one step at a time, always going to the nearest unflooded stone first.

Every stone gets the colour of whichever flood reaches it first. Where the two floods meet is the boundary between the parts.

That flood is Dijkstra's algorithm. Nothing more mysterious than that.

Demo 3 — the two floods (Dijkstra), one step at a time

step · auto

Bright dots = already settled (their distance is final). The pale ring = the current frontier, the dots the floods are about to reach. Press step slowly at the start and you'll see both floods creeping outward from their scribbles.

Why "nearest unflooded stone first" is not arbitrary That ordering is what makes Dijkstra correct. Once you pick the closest remaining dot, no later path can ever reach it more cheaply — every other route would have to pass through something already further away, and weights are never negative. So its distance is final the moment you pick it, and you never revisit it. That's the Bellman principle from Chapter 9: any piece of a shortest path is itself a shortest path.

5 · Only now, the math

Every symbol is a thing you already saw

Chapter 9 defined a path, its length, and then a distance. Same three definitions here, just on a graph instead of a pixel grid.

$$ L(P) \;=\; \sum_{e \in P} W_e \qquad\qquad d_L(x,y) \;=\; \min_{P:\,x \to y} L(P) $$

Then the point-to-set distance — the distance from a dot to a whole scribble, which is just the distance to its closest member:

$$ d_L(x, \Omega_k) \;=\; \min_{y \in \Omega_k} d_L(x,y) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad D_k(x) = d_L(x, \Omega_k) $$

$D_k$ is the distance map for scribble $k$: zero on the scribble, growing as you walk away from it. One map per scribble colour. Then the label is simply whoever is closest:

$$ \mathrm{label}(x) \;=\; \arg\min_k \; D_k(x) $$

Ties — where two maps are equal — are the boundary between parts. (In Chapter 9's colorization you did not take the $\arg\min$; you blended with $c(x) = \sum_k W(D_k)c_k / \sum_k W(D_k)$, $W(D)=1/D^b$, because colour should fade smoothly. Segmentation wants a hard decision, so $\arg\min$. Worth one sentence in the exam — it shows you know why the two chapters differ.)

The only genuinely new part: what is $W_e$ here?

In Chapter 9 the graph was the pixel grid and the weight was the brightness jump, $W_e = |Y(x) - Y(x')|$ — crossing an intensity edge is expensive. On a point cloud there is no brightness, so you pick weights from geometry:

  • Base: $W_e = \|p - q\|$, the plain 3D distance. Already enough to produce "walk along the object, don't fly", because there simply are no edges across empty air.
  • Better: add a term that makes crossing a sharp geometric boundary expensive, e.g. $W_e = \|p - q\| \cdot \big(1 + \lambda(1 - \langle n_p, n_q\rangle)\big)$ where $n_p$ is the surface normal at $p$ (estimated by fitting a plane to $p$'s neighbours). Two dots on the same flat panel have parallel normals → cheap. Two dots across a crease → normals disagree → expensive. That is the exact analogue of "crossing an image edge costs a lot".
  • Colour, if the scan has it, joins the same product.

Everything else — Bellman, Dijkstra, $D_k$, the $O(N\log N)$ with a priority queue — transfers unchanged from Chapter 9.

6 · The algorithm, written properly

  1. Build a graph $G=(V,E)$. Nodes = the points. Since the list is unordered, connect each point to its k nearest neighbours in 3D (k ≈ 6–10), found with a k-d tree. Optionally cap the edge length so no bridge crosses empty space.
  2. Weight the edges so distance grows across boundaries: $W_e = \|p-q\|$, optionally $\times \big(1+\lambda(1-\langle n_p,n_q\rangle)\big)$ using estimated surface normals (and colour, if available).
  3. Seeds. $\Omega_k$ = the set of points carrying scribble colour $k$. Set $D_k = 0$ on them.
  4. Distance maps. For each label $k$, run multi-source Dijkstra from $\Omega_k$ to get $D_k(v) = d_L(v, \Omega_k)$ for every point. (Or run one Dijkstra with all seeds at once, carrying an owner label — same result, one pass.)
  5. Assign. $\mathrm{label}(v) = \arg\min_k D_k(v)$. Ties mark the boundary between segments.
  6. Output. Colour each point by its label — the right-hand side of Figure 5.

7 · The traps

1 · Do not answer "nearest scribble by Euclidean distance" That's demo 1's left panel. It leaks labels across empty air between parts that are spatially close but structurally far — precisely the motorcycle wheel/handlebar case. Saying the word geodesic and explaining why is most of the grade.
2 · Do not forget that you have to CREATE the neighbourhood "An unordered list of 3D points" is in the question for a reason. If your answer says "run Dijkstra on the point cloud" without saying what the edges are, you have skipped the only step that differs from the lecture. Say k-nearest-neighbour graph, built with a k-d tree.
3 · Handle disconnection If a component of the graph contains no scribble at all, every point in it has $D_k=\infty$ for all $k$ and is unlabelled. Say what you'd do: increase $k$, or use a radius-graph with a larger radius, or assign that component by its nearest labelled component. Mentioning this failure mode reads as engineering maturity and is cheap.
4 · Nice-to-have extras (one line each, if you have room)

• One Dijkstra, not k of them — push all seeds into the queue at once, each carrying its label; the first flood to settle a node owns it. Same answer, one pass instead of $k$.

• Soft version — keep the $D_k$ maps and blend as in Chapter 9 if you want soft boundaries or a confidence value per point.

• Fast sweeping — the GPU-friendly alternative to Dijkstra from Chapter 9. It needs a grid-like sweep order, so on an unordered cloud Dijkstra is the natural pick; saying so shows you know the difference.

8 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

One page, in order

Idea. This is Chapter 9's scribble propagation by geodesic distance, moved from a pixel grid to a point cloud. The only new step is that the cloud is unordered, so the neighbourhood structure has to be constructed before any distance can be computed.

1 · Graph. $G=(V,E)$ with $V$ = the points; connect each point to its $k$ nearest neighbours in 3D ($k\approx 6\text{–}10$, via a k-d tree), optionally discarding edges longer than a cap so no edge bridges empty space.

2 · Weights. $W_e = \|p-q\|$, optionally multiplied by $\big(1+\lambda(1-\langle n_p,n_q\rangle)\big)$ with $n_p$ the local surface normal, so that crossing a sharp geometric boundary is expensive — the analogue of the intensity-gradient weight used for images.

3 · Seeds. $\Omega_k$ = points carrying scribble label $k$, with $D_k|_{\Omega_k}=0$.

4 · Distance maps. Run multi-source Dijkstra to obtain $D_k(v)=d_L(v,\Omega_k)=\min_{y\in\Omega_k} \min_P \sum_{e\in P} W_e$ for every point and every label ($O(N\log N)$, justified by the Bellman principle).

5 · Label. $\mathrm{label}(v)=\arg\min_k D_k(v)$; ties are the segment boundaries. (Chapter 9 blends with $W(D)=1/D^b$ instead, because colorization wants soft transitions; segmentation wants a hard decision.)

Notes. Euclidean nearest-scribble would fail: parts that are close in space but far along the object (motorcycle wheel vs handlebar) would leak labels across empty air. $k$ must be large enough to keep the graph connected but small enough not to bridge distinct parts; a component containing no scribble stays unlabelled and must be handled separately.

9 · Exam drill

Q. Why not simply label each point by its nearest scribble in Euclidean distance?

Because Euclidean distance travels through empty space. Two parts of an object can be centimetres apart in space while being far apart along the object — a motorcycle's front wheel and handlebar, or the two tips of a horseshoe. Euclidean labelling leaks a label across that gap. Geodesic distance is constrained to the graph, which only contains edges between genuinely adjacent points, so it must travel along the object.

Q. What is the single thing that makes this different from Chapter 9's colorization?

The domain has no built-in neighbourhood. An image is a grid where every pixel's four neighbours are known for free; a point cloud is an unordered list. So you must construct the graph — k-nearest neighbours in 3D — before any of the Chapter 9 machinery applies. Everything after that (Bellman, Dijkstra, $D_k$, $\arg\min$) is unchanged.

Q. How would you choose the edge weights, and why does that choice matter?

Minimum: $W_e = \|p-q\|$ so distance measures travel along the surface. Better: multiply by a term that grows when the two surface normals disagree, $1+\lambda(1-\langle n_p,n_q\rangle)$, so crossing a crease or a part boundary is expensive. This is the direct analogue of weighting by the intensity gradient in an image: you want the distance to grow fast exactly where a segment boundary should be, so the two floods meet there.

Q. What happens if k is too small? Too large?

Too small: the graph fragments into disconnected components; any component with no scribble gets $D_k=\infty$ everywhere and is left unlabelled. Too large: edges start bridging genuinely separate parts across empty space, which reintroduces the Euclidean shortcut and lets labels leak — the exact failure you built the graph to avoid. A length cap on edges is the standard guard.

Q. Do you need one Dijkstra run per scribble colour?

No. Push every seed of every colour into one priority queue at distance 0, each carrying its label; when a node is settled it inherits the label of whichever flood reached it. One pass gives the $\arg\min$ directly. Running $k$ separate Dijkstras and taking the $\arg\min$ afterwards gives the identical answer, and is worth doing if you also want the individual $D_k$ maps (e.g. for soft blending or confidence).

Q. Why is Dijkstra correct here — what property is it relying on?

Non-negative edge weights plus the Bellman principle: any sub-path of a shortest path is itself a shortest path. When you extract the minimum-distance unsettled node, no alternative route can improve it, because any such route would have to pass through a node that is already further away, and adding non-negative weights can only increase the total. So its distance is final on extraction and it never needs revisiting.

Q. Complexity?

Building the kNN graph with a k-d tree: $O(N \log N)$. Dijkstra with a binary-heap priority queue: $O(|E| \log N) = O(kN \log N)$, which is $O(N \log N)$ for constant $k$. Total $O(N \log N)$ — with a small $k$ the graph is sparse, exactly like the 7-nonzeros-per-row sparsity that makes Horn–Schunck's system fast.

Q. Where else in this course have you seen this exact pattern?

Chapter 9 colorization (scribbles → geodesic distance → blend), Chapter 10 matting (trimap → geodesic distance maps → opacity), and here. The recurring recipe is: define a graph, define a cost that is small within a region and large across boundaries, run Dijkstra from seeds, decide by comparing the resulting distance maps. If a question gives you seeds and a domain, this is almost always the answer.
