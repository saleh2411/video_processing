Companion page to the light-field material · Moed B 2022, question 4 · 12 points

Don't render the scene. Just find the ray you already have.

This is the concentric mosaics setup (Shum & He, SIGGRAPH 1999). The entire answer rests on one idea: a photograph is not a picture, it is a bucket of rays. If the ray your virtual camera needs is already sitting in one of the recorded frames, you don't need geometry, depth, or matching — you need a lookup table. The work is all in the change of coordinates.

Moed B · 1/8/2022 · Question 4 · 12 points · LIGHT FIELDS

Consider Figure 2. On the left you see a camera attached to an arm that is rotating around an axis. The camera is recording a video sequence as it is rotating. On the right, you see a schematic top view of the setup. Suggest a way to synthesize a novel view from the virtual camera inside the circle, using the images taken by the rotating camera.

1 · The story

The kid with the straw and the merry-go-round

You are standing in a field, and you want to know what the world looks like from a spot ten steps away — but you can't walk there.

Here is the thing: light doesn't care who is looking. A beam of light travelling from the far mountain to that spot doesn't stop there — it keeps going, in a straight line, forever. If you stand somewhere further along that same straight line, and you look back along it through a straw, you see the exact same colour that a person standing at the spot would have seen.

So a photograph is not really "a picture from a place". It is a bundle of straws, one per pixel, each one labelled with which straight line it looked down and what colour came back.

Now: the camera goes round and round on the arm. Each turn, it collects thousands of straws. After a full rotation, you have millions of straws, all labelled. To make the picture the virtual camera would take, you don't need to understand the scene at all. For each pixel of the imaginary photo, you work out which straight line that pixel looks down — and then you go rummaging in your pile for the straw with the same label, and copy its colour. Done.

The only real question left is: is the straw you need actually in the pile? And the beautiful answer for this setup is: yes, for every ray that starts inside a certain disc. That is why the virtual camera in the figure is drawn inside the circle, and not outside it.

The whole answer, in one sentence Re-index every recorded pixel not by (frame, column) but by the ray it measured; for a 2-D circular capture a ray is fully described by two numbers — its direction $\psi$ and its signed distance to the rotation axis $r$. The captured set is exactly $\{|r| \le R\sin\beta_{\max}\}$, so any virtual camera inside the disc of radius $R\sin\beta_{\max}$ can be rendered by pure table lookup, with no depth and no correspondence.

2 · Setting up the coordinates

The plenoptic function, and how much of it we have

Everything you could ever see is the 7-D plenoptic function $P(x,y,z,\theta,\phi,\lambda,t)$ — the radiance at every point, in every direction, at every wavelength and time. Assume a static scene, fixed wavelengths, and radiance constant along a ray in free space (no fog): the position along the ray becomes irrelevant, and 5-D drops to the 4-D light field $L(u,v,s,t)$ — a ray needs only 4 numbers. Restrict further to one horizontal plane and a ray needs only 2. The arm setup is exactly this 2-D case (plus a vertical index for the image rows, which we'll return to).

Notation for the top view

  • $O$ = the rotation axis, $R$ = the arm length, so the camera centre is $\mathbf{C}(\theta) = R(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$.
  • The camera looks radially outward, i.e. its optical axis is $\hat{\mathbf{n}}(\theta) = (\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$, with horizontal half-field-of-view $\beta_{\max}$.
  • A pixel column is indexed by the angle $\beta \in [-\beta_{\max},\beta_{\max}]$ it makes with the optical axis. (For an equiangular/cylindrical camera $\beta$ is linear in the column index; for a pinhole, column $u = f\tan\beta$.)
  • A ray is written $(\psi, r)$: $\psi$ = its direction angle, $r$ = the signed perpendicular distance from $O$ to the line. For a ray through a point $\mathbf{X}$ with unit direction $\mathbf{d}=(\cos\psi,\sin\psi)$, $\;r = X_x\sin\psi - X_y\cos\psi$.
The one computation that solves the question

Which ray does pixel $\beta$ of frame $\theta$ measure? Its direction is $\psi = \theta + \beta$, and its distance from the axis is

$$ r \;=\; C_x\sin\psi - C_y\cos\psi \;=\; R\cos\theta\,\sin(\theta+\beta) - R\sin\theta\,\cos(\theta+\beta) \;=\; \boxed{\,R\sin\beta\,} \tag{1} $$
Read what equation (1) says — it is the whole question The distance of a ray from the rotation axis depends only on the pixel column $\beta$, not on the frame $\theta$. So all the pixels in column $\beta$, gathered across the whole rotation, are exactly the set of rays tangent to the circle of radius $R\sin\beta$ — one full $360°$ panorama of them. That collection is a concentric mosaic $\mathrm{CM}_{r}$, and the recorded video, re-sorted, is a stack of them, nested like tree rings.

Rebinning: $\;\mathrm{CM}_{R\sin\beta}(\psi) \;=\; \text{frame}(\psi-\beta)\big[\text{column }\beta\big]$.
Rendering: invert equation (1)

Put the virtual camera at $\mathbf{P}$ (inside the circle) looking along $\psi_0$. For each of its pixels, with viewing direction $\psi$:

  1. Which ray do I need? $\;r = P_x\sin\psi - P_y\cos\psi$. Together with $\psi$ this labels the ray completely.
  2. Do I have it? Available iff $|r| \le R\sin\beta_{\max}$. Since $|r|\le\|\mathbf{P}\|$ for any ray through $\mathbf{P}$, every pixel is available whenever $\|\mathbf{P}\| \le R\sin\beta_{\max}$.
  3. Where is it? Invert (1): $\;\beta = \arcsin(r/R)$, and then $\theta = \psi - \beta$.
  4. Fetch. Read the pixel of frame $\theta$ at column $\beta$ — i.e. read $\mathrm{CM}_{r}(\psi)$. Nearest neighbour if you are lazy; bilinear in $(\theta,\beta)$ — equivalently in $(\psi, r)$ — if you are not.
  5. Repeat for every row of the image (see the vertical caveat below), and you have the novel view.
Why the arcsin branch is automatically the right one A line through $\mathbf{P}$ meets the circle twice, so there are two candidate cameras. The branch $\beta = \arcsin(r/R) \in [-\pi/2,\pi/2]$ picks the exit point — the intersection ahead of $\mathbf{P}$ along $\mathbf{d}$. That is the correct one: an outward-facing camera sitting there sees the same scene, further down the same line. The other intersection, behind $\mathbf{P}$, would need $\beta' = \pi-\beta > \pi/2$, i.e. a camera looking backwards through its own arm — impossible for any real field of view, which is exactly why the equation rejects it.

4 · The parts nobody gets right the first time

Caveats you should mention
IssueWhyFix
Vertical parallax is not captured The camera only ever moves in one horizontal plane, so the light field is sampled in 3-D $(\theta,\beta,v)$, not 4-D. A virtual ray at height $v$ is fetched from a camera at a different horizontal position, which is correct in-plane but wrong out-of-plane: the vertical scale of an object depends on its distance from the fetching camera, not from the virtual one. Rescale the column vertically by the depth ratio, $v' = v\cdot d_{\rm virtual}/d_{\rm captured}$. Requires a rough depth estimate — a constant "average scene depth" is the standard cheap version, and it is why concentric mosaics show mild vertical distortion on very near objects.
Depth-dependent aliasing Adjacent captured rays diverge; two rays that are 1 frame apart hit points that are further apart the deeper the scene is. Under-sampling in $\theta$ shows up as ghosting/banding on near objects. Sample $\theta$ finely enough (the plenoptic sampling bound: required $\Delta\theta$ scales with the closest scene depth), or pre-filter — blur along $\theta$ before rebinning — or use depth-corrected interpolation.
You cannot leave the disc $|r|\le R\sin\beta_{\max}$ is a hard limit: rays passing further from the axis were never recorded. Nothing to do but accept it — or use a wider-FOV lens, which enlarges the disc. $\beta_{\max}\to90°$ (a tangentially-looking camera) gives the largest possible disc, radius $R$.
The scene must be static The rotation takes real time, so "frame $\theta$" is also "time $t$". A moving object appears at inconsistent positions in different mosaics. Capture fast, or accept ghosting. (This is the same static-scene assumption as every light-field capture rig.)
Non-Lambertian surfaces Actually fine — arguably a feature. Because you copy the ray rather than shading a surface, specular highlights move correctly with the viewpoint, which geometry-based rendering struggles with. —
Storage and cost

The video is $N_\theta$ frames of $W\times H$; rebinning does not change the amount of data, it only re-sorts it — $W$ concentric mosaics, each $N_\theta \times H$. Rendering one novel view costs $O(W_v H_v)$ table lookups with $O(1)$ arithmetic each (one $\arcsin$, one subtraction), so it is real-time and independent of scene complexity. Compression exploits the huge redundancy between adjacent mosaics (they differ by a small parallax) — vector quantisation or a video codec along $\psi$.

5 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

One page, as the exam asks

Principle. Treat each recorded pixel as a measurement of the radiance along a ray, not as part of a picture (the light-field / plenoptic view: radiance is constant along a ray in free space, so 5-D reduces to 4-D and, restricted to the rotation plane, to 2-D). Rendering a novel view is then not reconstruction but resampling: for each pixel of the virtual camera, identify the ray it needs and look up the recorded pixel that measured the same ray. No depth, no correspondence, no geometry.

Parametrisation. Camera centre $\mathbf{C}(\theta)=R(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$, optical axis radially outward, pixel column at angle $\beta$ from the axis, $|\beta|\le\beta_{\max}$. The ray of pixel $(\theta,\beta)$ has direction $\psi=\theta+\beta$ and signed distance from the rotation axis

$$ r = C_x\sin\psi - C_y\cos\psi = R\sin\beta. $$

$r$ is independent of $\theta$: for a fixed column $\beta$, sweeping $\theta$ through $360°$ produces a complete panorama of rays tangent to the circle of radius $R\sin\beta$ — a concentric mosaic $\mathrm{CM}_{R\sin\beta}(\psi)=\text{frame}(\psi-\beta)[\beta]$. Rebin the video into these mosaics once, offline.

Rendering algorithm. Place the virtual camera at $\mathbf{P}$ inside the circle, looking along $\psi_0$. For each pixel, with direction $\psi$:

  1. $r = P_x\sin\psi - P_y\cos\psi$;
  2. if $|r|>R\sin\beta_{\max}$ the ray was never captured → leave black (or extrapolate);
  3. otherwise $\beta=\arcsin(r/R)$ and $\theta=\psi-\beta$ (this branch selects the intersection of the ray with the circle ahead of $\mathbf{P}$, which is the camera that saw the same scene);
  4. output $=\mathrm{CM}_{r}(\psi)$, i.e. frame $\theta$, column $\beta$, bilinearly interpolated in $(\theta,\beta)$.

Since $|r|\le\|\mathbf{P}\|$ for every ray through $\mathbf{P}$, all pixels are available whenever $\|\mathbf{P}\|\le R\sin\beta_{\max}$ — this is precisely why the virtual camera in the figure must be inside the circle, and it defines the exact region of free viewpoint motion. Rotating the virtual camera in place is always free (it only changes which $\psi$ range you sample).

Limitations to state. (i) Only horizontal parallax is captured; the vertical dimension is fetched from a camera at a different position, so the column must be rescaled vertically by an approximate depth ratio, otherwise near objects show vertical distortion. (ii) Angular sampling $\Delta\theta$ must be fine enough for the closest scene depth, else ghosting/banding (plenoptic sampling bound); pre-filtering or depth-corrected interpolation mitigates it. (iii) The scene must be static, since $\theta$ is also time. Cost is $O(1)$ per output pixel and independent of scene complexity, and view-dependent effects such as specularities are reproduced correctly for free.

6 · Exam drill

Q. Why is $r$ independent of $\theta$? Give the intuition, not just the trigonometry.

Because the whole rig is rotationally symmetric about $O$. Rotating the arm by $\Delta$ rotates every ray it captures by $\Delta$ about $O$ — and rotation about $O$ preserves distance to $O$. So $r$ can only depend on the quantity that is internal to the camera, namely which column you are reading, and $r=R\sin\beta$ is just "opposite over hypotenuse" in the right triangle formed by $O$, the camera centre, and the foot of the perpendicular onto the ray. This symmetry is exactly why the natural storage unit is a set of concentric (co-axial) mosaics.

Q. What if the camera points tangentially instead of radially outward?

Nothing changes structurally — you only add a constant. If the optical axis makes a fixed angle $\gamma$ with the outward radius, then $\psi = \theta+\gamma+\beta$ and $r = R\sin(\gamma+\beta)$, so the accessible band becomes $R\sin(\gamma-\beta_{\max}) \le r \le R\sin(\gamma+\beta_{\max})$. With $\gamma=90°$ (fully tangential) the band is centred on $r=R$: you capture rays that graze the circle, which is ideal for a wide outward panorama but bad for interior virtual cameras, since a virtual camera at $\mathbf{P}$ needs $|r|\le\|\mathbf{P}\| < R$. The radial-outward mounting drawn in the figure is the one that makes the interior renderable, and the band is symmetric, $|r|\le R\sin\beta_{\max}$.

Q. Can you synthesise a view from outside the circle?

Only partially. A viewpoint $\mathbf{P}$ with $\|\mathbf{P}\|>R$ still has many rays with small $|r|$ — those pointing roughly toward the axis — and those are available. But the rays looking away from the circle, and the rays grazing past it, have $|r| > R\sin\beta_{\max}$ and were never recorded, so the view is incomplete. Worse, a ray from an outside viewpoint through the disc is looking at the scene from the wrong side: the recorded ray with the same $(\psi,r)$ label carries radiance travelling in the same direction, so it is still correct for the far half of the scene, but anything between $\mathbf{P}$ and the circle was never imaged at all. The honest answer: the guaranteed free-viewpoint region is the disc of radius $R\sin\beta_{\max}$, and everything else needs extrapolation with geometry.

Q. Where does this differ from stitching a panorama or from image-based morphing?

A cylindrical panorama fixes the viewpoint: it captures all directions from one point, so it gives you rotation but no parallax — near and far objects never shift relative to each other. The concentric-mosaic capture varies $r$ as well as $\psi$, and $r$ is exactly the parameter that encodes viewpoint translation, which is why parallax appears. Compared with view morphing / depth-based warping, the difference is that no correspondence is estimated: instead of solving an ill-posed vision problem you took enough pictures that the answer is already recorded. That is the entire philosophy of image-based rendering — trade storage for the hard problem.

Q. How finely must you sample $\theta$?

By the plenoptic sampling analysis, the maximum disparity between adjacent captured rays must stay below one pixel. Two neighbouring camera positions are $R\,\Delta\theta$ apart; an object at depth $z$ from the circle shifts by roughly $f\,R\,\Delta\theta / z$ pixels between them. Requiring $\le 1$ gives $\Delta\theta \lesssim z_{\min}/(fR)$ — the sampling rate is set by the closest object, not the average one. If you cannot sample that finely, you must either pre-filter (blur along $\theta$, trading aliasing for blur) or reintroduce a coarse depth map and use depth-corrected interpolation, which is the standard "a little geometry goes a long way" result.

Q. How is the data stored, and how big is it?

Unchanged in size — rebinning is a permutation. $N_\theta$ frames of $W\times H$ become $W$ mosaics of $N_\theta \times H$: mosaic $k$ collects column $k$ from every frame. The rebinned layout is what makes rendering cache-friendly, because a virtual view with a fixed $\|\mathbf{P}\|$ reads mostly along a single mosaic. Redundancy between adjacent mosaics is enormous (they differ only by parallax), so compress across the mosaic index with any inter-frame video codec; Shum & He report large ratios with vector quantisation while keeping random access, which matters because rendering touches pixels in an unpredictable order.

Q. In the two-plane light field $L(u,v,s,t)$, what is the analogue of equation (1)?

The same change of variables. In the two-plane parametrisation a ray is labelled by where it pierces a camera plane $(s,t)$ and a focal plane $(u,v)$; rendering a novel view means, for each virtual pixel, computing the ray's intersections with those two planes and reading $L$ there — again a pure lookup with quadrilinear interpolation. The circular capture here is just a different, one-parameter slice of the same 4-D function: instead of a planar $(s,t)$ grid of viewpoints you have a circle of them, so the natural coordinates become $(\psi,r)$ rather than $(s,u)$. The epipolar-plane-image structure is identical: a scene point traces a line in the planar case and a sinusoid in the circular case, with amplitude set by its distance — which is exactly the green curve in Demo 2.
