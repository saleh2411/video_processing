Catching a copy-paste

Someone erased a bin from a photo by pasting grass over it. Find the paste — with nothing but the doctored photo. Story, a demo that actually runs the detector, then the math.

Moed A · 19/6/2022 · Question 5 · 13 points · CLONE DETECTION

Consider Figure 3. The user took the original image and used inpainting to remove the garbage can, resulting in the "Doctored" image in the middle. Suggest a clone detection algorithm that takes an image as input (i.e., the middle image of the panel) and automatically detects cloned regions (i.e., the mask on the right that shows that the two dark regions are clones). The original image is shown on the left for reference only. The algorithm has no access to it.

1 · The story

Two students, one homework

A teacher gets 30 essays and wants to find the cheaters. She doesn't have the internet, she doesn't have last year's essays — she has only the 30 papers in front of her.

How does she catch them? She looks for two paragraphs that are word-for-word identical. Not similar. Identical. Real writing is never identical by accident.

And she checks one more thing before accusing anyone: the copied part must be a whole run — a full paragraph copied in order, not one shared sentence here and an unrelated one there. A long, ordered, exact-match run is the fingerprint of copying.

Same for the photo

Inpainting removed the bin by copying grass from somewhere else in the same photo and pasting it over the bin. So the doctored image contains two regions that are near-identical copies of each other. Nothing came from outside.

So: match the image against itself, and look for a big region where every patch has an almost-perfect twin somewhere else — and where all those twins sit at the same offset.

You already own the tool that matches an image against another image, fast: PatchMatch (Ch.11). Here you feed it the same image twice.

2 · The two clues, and why you need both

ClueWhat it meansWhy it alone is not enough
near-zero patch distancethis patch has an almost identical twin elsewhere flat regions (sky, uniform grass, a blank wall) match everything with distance ≈ 0. You'd flag the whole sky.
one shared offseta whole neighbourhood was moved by the same vector a single patch with a coherent offset proves nothing; noise produces some by chance. You need a large connected region agreeing on it.
And one more thing you must exclude Every patch's best match is itself, at distance exactly 0 and offset $(0,0)$. So you must forbid the trivial match: reject any candidate whose offset is smaller than a few patch widths. Forget this and your algorithm reports that the entire image is a clone of itself. Graders check for exactly this line.

3 · Why the offsets come in a $\pm v$ pair

A small thing that reads as real understanding

Say region $B$ (the paste, over the bin) was copied from region $A$ (the donor grass), and $A$ sits at offset $-v$ from $B$. Then:

  • every patch in $B$ finds its twin in $A$, so it reports offset $\;-v$;
  • every patch in $A$ finds its twin in $B$, so it reports offset $\;+v$.

So a genuine copy-move shows up as two blobs of equal size and shape, one voting $+v$ and the other voting $-v$. That is exactly the two dark regions in the exam figure's right panel — the algorithm cannot tell you which of the pair is the forgery and which is the donor, and it doesn't need to.

Practical consequence: in the offset histogram, look for a peak at $v$ and its mirror at $-v$. A mirrored pair of peaks is much stronger evidence than a single peak.

4 · Only now, the math

It is Chapter 11's nearest-neighbour field, with source = target

PatchMatch computes a matching field. For every pixel $x$ it stores the offset to its best matching patch:

$$ f(x) \;=\; \arg\min_{y} \;\big\| P_x - Q_{x+y} \big\|_2^2 , \qquad d(x) \;=\; \big\|P_x - Q_{x+f(x)}\big\|_2^2 $$

with $P_x$ the patch centred at $x$. Chapter 11 ran this between two different images $S$ and $T$. Here we set $S = T = I$, the single doctored image, and add the constraint that kills the trivial answer:

$$ f(x) \;=\; \arg\min_{\|y\| \,>\, \rho} \; \big\| P_x - P_{x+y} \big\|_2^2 $$

where $\rho$ is a few patch widths. Then the clone mask is the set of pixels satisfying both clues:

$$ \mathcal{M} \;=\; \Big\{\, x \;:\; d(x) < \tau \;\;\wedge\;\; \big\| f(x) - v^\ast \big\| < \delta \,\Big\}, \qquad v^\ast = \arg\max_v \#\{x : f(x) \approx v\} $$

$v^\ast$ is the dominant offset, found as the peak of a 2-D histogram of the offset field. Finally keep only large connected components of $\mathcal{M}$ — a genuine paste is a contiguous blob, and this step throws away scattered accidental matches.

Why PatchMatch and not brute force

Brute force is $O(N^2)$ patch comparisons — every patch against every other. PatchMatch gets an approximate nearest-neighbour field in roughly $O(N \log N)$ by exploiting spatial coherence: if patch $x$ matched at offset $f(x)$, its neighbour $x+1$ almost certainly matches at the same offset, so it just borrows it (propagation) and then tries a few shrinking random perturbations (random search). And note the happy accident: a copy-moved region is the most coherent offset field imaginable, so PatchMatch converges on it especially fast.

5 · The algorithm, written properly

  1. Self-PatchMatch. Run PatchMatch with source = target = the input image, obtaining the offset field $f(x)$ and the patch distance $d(x)$ for every pixel.
  2. Forbid the trivial match. Reject any candidate with $\|f(x)\| \le \rho$ (a few patch widths), so a patch cannot match itself or its immediate neighbourhood.
  3. Clone candidates. Keep pixels with $d(x) < \tau$ — patches that have an almost-exact twin elsewhere.
  4. Find the dominant offset. Histogram the offsets $f(x)$ of the candidates; take the peak $v^\ast$. A true copy-move yields a mirrored pair of peaks at $\pm v^\ast$.
  5. Keep coherent regions. Retain candidates whose offset is within $\delta$ of $\pm v^\ast$, then keep only large connected components. Optionally clean up with a morphological open/close.
  6. Output those components as the clone mask — the matched pair $A$ (donor) and $B$ (paste).

6 · The traps and the extras

1 · Excluding the trivial match is not optional Without $\|f(x)\| > \rho$, every pixel matches itself perfectly and the whole image is flagged. Demo 1's checkbox shows it. One sentence in the exam.
2 · Flat regions match everything Uniform sky or smooth grass has near-zero distance to countless other patches, so clue 1 alone floods with false positives. The offset-coherence requirement is what saves you: random flat matches scatter across all offsets and never form a peak. If you want a belt-and-braces answer, also discard patches whose variance is below a threshold (no texture, no evidence).
3 · What if the paste was rotated, scaled or blended? Plain PatchMatch searches translations only, so a rotated or rescaled copy breaks the single-offset assumption. Say the fix in one line: Generalized PatchMatch searches over rotation and scale as well, so the "constant offset" test becomes "a consistent similarity transform" — estimated with RANSAC (Ch.3) over the match correspondences. Feathered/blended edges are handled by loosening $\tau$ and relying on the region's interior.
4 · Why this is a fair question It is Chapter 11 used backwards. Retargeting uses the nearest-neighbour field to synthesize; here you read the field as evidence. Same PatchMatch, same offset field — different question asked of it.

7 · The answer, as you would write it in the box

One page, in order

Observation. Cloning/inpainting fills a region by copying content from the same image. So the doctored image contains two regions that are near-identical copies of one another, related by a single translation. Detecting the forgery therefore means matching the image against itself.

1 · Self-PatchMatch. Run PatchMatch with source = target = the input image, giving the offset field $f(x)$ and the patch distance $d(x) = \|P_x - P_{x+f(x)}\|_2^2$. Forbid the trivial match by restricting the search to $\|f(x)\| > \rho$ (a few patch widths); otherwise every patch matches itself at distance 0.

2 · Candidates. Mark pixels with $d(x) < \tau$: these have an almost exact twin elsewhere.

3 · Coherence. Near-zero distance alone is not enough — flat regions match everything. So histogram the candidates' offsets and find the dominant offset $v^\ast$; keep only candidates whose offset is within $\delta$ of it. A genuine copy-move appears as a matched pair: region $A$ voting $+v^\ast$ and region $B$ voting $-v^\ast$.

4 · Regions. Keep only large connected components of the surviving set (optionally with a morphological clean-up) and output them as the clone mask.

Notes. PatchMatch gives the field in ~$O(N\log N)$ instead of the $O(N^2)$ of brute force, and copy-moved regions are exactly the highly coherent offset fields it converges on fastest. If the paste may have been rotated or scaled, use Generalized PatchMatch and fit a similarity transform with RANSAC instead of a single constant offset.

8 · Exam drill

Q. What is the one observation the whole algorithm rests on?

Inpainting/cloning fills a hole with content taken from the same image. So the evidence of tampering is already inside the single image you were given: two near-identical regions related by a translation. Nothing external is needed, which is why the algorithm can work with no access to the original.

Q. Why must you exclude the trivial match, and how?

Every patch is a perfect match for itself: distance 0 at offset $(0,0)$. Without exclusion, every pixel is a "clone" and the mask covers the image. Fix: restrict the search to offsets with $\|f(x)\| > \rho$, where $\rho$ is a few patch widths, so neither a patch nor its overlapping neighbours can be the answer.

Q. Why is a small patch distance not sufficient evidence?

Because textureless regions — sky, smooth grass, a plain wall — are near-identical to very many other patches. They would all pass a distance threshold. The distinguishing property of a real copy-move is that a whole contiguous region shares one offset; random flat matches scatter over all offsets and form no histogram peak.

Q. Why do the detected regions come in pairs with opposite offsets?

If $B$ was copied from $A$ with $A$ at offset $-v$ from $B$, then patches in $B$ point to $A$ with offset $-v$, and patches in $A$ point back to $B$ with offset $+v$. So the mask contains two blobs of equal shape voting $\pm v$. The algorithm cannot say which is the donor and which is the paste — and does not need to.

Q. Why use PatchMatch rather than brute-force search?

Brute force compares every patch with every other: $O(N^2)$. PatchMatch exploits spatial coherence — propagate a good offset to neighbours, then refine with shrinking random search — reaching a good approximate nearest-neighbour field in about $O(N\log N)$. Conveniently, a cloned region has the most coherent offset field possible, which is precisely the case PatchMatch handles best.

Q. What breaks if the forger rotated or resized the pasted region?

The "single constant offset" assumption. Plain PatchMatch searches translations only, so patches in the pasted region would fail to find good matches, or would find them at inconsistent offsets. Use Generalized PatchMatch (searching rotation and scale too) and replace the offset-histogram step with fitting a similarity transform to the correspondences, robustly, with RANSAC.

Q. How does this relate to what Chapter 11 normally does with the offset field?

Retargeting, reshuffling and inpainting use the nearest-neighbour field to synthesize new content. Here you use the identical field as forensic evidence — you read it rather than act on it. Same algorithm, opposite direction.
