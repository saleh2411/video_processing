1   The one idea the lecture never says out loud

Start here — a pixel is a bucket, not a point

You probably imagine a pixel as the colour of the scene at one exact location. It is not. A camera sensor is a grid of tiny square wells. Each well collects all the light that lands anywhere inside its square and reports the average. A pixel is a bucket, and its value is the average colour of everything that fell in.

Now put a person in front of the camera. Take a pixel deep inside his jacket: everything that fell in the bucket was jacket, so the value is pure jacket brown. Take a pixel far away on the wall: pure wall grey. Fine. Now take a pixel exactly on the silhouette — the little square straddles the edge. Say 70% of it saw jacket and 30% saw wall. The bucket reports

$$ \text{observed colour} = 0.7 \times (\text{jacket brown}) + 0.3 \times (\text{wall grey}) $$

That colour is neither jacket nor wall. It is a physically real, muddy in-between colour, and there is nothing wrong with your camera. It happens to every pixel on the outline of every object in every image ever taken. It happens even worse for hair, smoke, glass, motion blur and out-of-focus edges, where a single pixel can be a mix across many strands.

This is the entire reason matting exists

If you segment with a binary mask, you are forced to answer "jacket or wall?" for that pixel. Both answers are wrong. Say "jacket" and you glue a fringe of wall-grey onto the person. Say "wall" and you shave a pixel off his shoulder. Either way, when you drop him onto a new background you get a visible, ugly outline. Matting refuses to answer the question. Instead it says: this pixel is 70% person, and it calls that number $\alpha$ (alpha).

So: $\alpha$ is the fraction of a pixel that belongs to the foreground. $\alpha = 1$ deep inside the person, $\alpha = 0$ out on the wall, and something in between along the boundary. The opacity map is just the image of all the $\alpha$ values. That's it. That's the whole vocabulary.

If you remember one sentence Binary segmentation asks "is this pixel the person?" — matting asks "how much of this pixel is the person, and what colour would the person have been if the background hadn't leaked in?"

2   The compositing equation, and why matting is impossible

Building the equation

Write down what the bucket does, as algebra. For a pixel at position $x$:

$$ \underbrace{c(x)}_{\substack{\text{what the camera} \\ \text{recorded}}} \;=\; \alpha(x)\,\underbrace{f(x)}_{\substack{\text{the pure} \\ \text{foreground colour}}} \;+\; \big(1-\alpha(x)\big)\,\underbrace{b(x)}_{\substack{\text{the pure} \\ \text{background colour}}} $$

This is called the compositing equation. It is the single most important line in the whole chapter, and the lecture only writes it down on page 72, three sections in. Read it as: the recorded colour is a weighted average of the true foreground colour and the true background colour, and $\alpha$ is the weight.

Now count

The pixel is a colour, so $c(x)$ is 3 numbers (R, G, B). That gives you 3 equations. What don't you know? $f(x)$ is 3 numbers. $b(x)$ is 3 numbers. $\alpha(x)$ is 1 number. That's 7 unknowns.

$$ \underbrace{3 \text{ equations}}_{\text{R, G, B}} \quad\text{vs.}\quad \underbrace{7 \text{ unknowns}}_{f_R, f_G, f_B,\; b_R, b_G, b_B,\; \alpha} $$

Three equations cannot pin down seven unknowns. Matting is fundamentally under-determined, at every single pixel, forever. No algorithm will ever "solve" it. This is not a limitation of the course's method — it is arithmetic.

So what is the rest of the chapter?

Once you accept the counting argument, everything that follows becomes obvious in purpose. Every stage of the lecture is a way of injecting extra information to close the 4-unknown gap:

  • The trimap declares that for most pixels $\alpha$ is already known (exactly 0 or exactly 1). Now you only have to solve the thin uncertain band — maybe 2% of the image.
  • The colour models (KDE) add a prior: "foreground colours tend to look like this, background colours like that." That constrains $f$ and $b$.
  • The distance maps add a spatial prior: "a pixel hugging the confident foreground is probably mostly foreground," independent of its colour.
  • The window search (Section 10.3) adds a locality prior: "$f(x)$ is probably equal to the colour of some confidently-foreground pixel a few pixels away."

Four sources of side information, one for each missing degree of freedom. That is the architecture of the entire chapter, and now you can read it as a story instead of a list of formulas.

2.1   Why you must not just blend the pixel you recorded

The halo, derived

Suppose you have a perfect $\alpha$. You want to place the person on a new background $B$. The obvious thing to write is

$$ J(x) = \alpha(x)\,c(x) + (1-\alpha(x))\,B(x) \qquad \text{(the naive blend)} $$

and the lecture says, flatly, "we won't do that." Here is exactly why. Substitute the compositing equation, $c = \alpha f + (1-\alpha) b$, into the naive blend:

$$ J_{\text{naive}} = \alpha\big[\alpha f + (1-\alpha) b\big] + (1-\alpha) B = \alpha^2 f + \underbrace{\alpha(1-\alpha)\, b}_{\textbf{the old background!}} + (1-\alpha) B $$

Compare with what you actually wanted, which is the person's true colour mixed with the new background:

$$ J_{\text{correct}} = \alpha f + (1-\alpha) B $$

Subtract them:

$$ J_{\text{naive}} - J_{\text{correct}} = \alpha(1-\alpha)\,\big(b - f\big) $$

So the naive blend smears the old background colour $b$ into your result, with strength $\alpha(1-\alpha)$. That coefficient is zero where $\alpha=0$ or $\alpha=1$ (good — the confident regions are untouched) and largest exactly at $\alpha = 0.5$, where it equals $0.25$. In other words the error is concentrated in a thin ring hugging the person's outline, which is precisely what a halo is.

And note the error scales with $\|b - f\|$: the more the old background differs from the person, the worse it gets. Light hair on a red wall is the worst case in the lecture because $b - f$ is enormous there.

Notation trap #1 — the letter f means two different things

In the compositing equation, $f(x)$ is the pure foreground colour at pixel $x$ — a 3-vector of R,G,B.

Two pages earlier, $f(c|F)$ is a probability density over colours — a scalar function that takes a colour and returns "how likely". Totally unrelated. The lecture reuses the letter. Tell them apart by the argument: $f(x)$ takes a position, $f(c|F)$ takes a colour.

4   The framework (lecture p. 67)

Original text (PDF page 67)

The purpose of video matting is taking the foreground of an image and insert it to a new background. The entire framework of video matting compromises:

Input: A 3-channel image ($I : \Omega \to R^3$ or denoted as $c: \Omega \to R^3$) and scribbles of the foreground ($\Omega_F \in \Omega$) and the background ($\Omega_F \in \Omega$). We can even add more information to the input (such as optical flow for better segmentation), so the input image does not necessarily belong to $R^3$.

Trimap: This is essentially a map of only 3 values (hence the name): 1 - we confidently know the pixel is foreground. 0 - we confidently know the pixel is background. Undecided value $\phi$ - we are not sure if the pixel is foreground or background.

Opacity map: It will fill the undecided region with values which are between 0 and 1.

Matting: This process takes as input an opacity map and a new background, and performs the final matting between the foreground of the original image and the new background. In regions where the opacity map is 0, the new background is inserted, while in regions where the opacity map is 1, the original foreground is preserved.

The question remains on what to insert to pixels where the opacity map was brtween 0 and 1. One possible way is to implement $\alpha I + (1 - \alpha)B$, but we won't do that. The reason is because in all those semi-transparent areas we will have a mix between the colors of the foreground and the background, which is not desired. For example, if we had a light hair on a red background, the color of the hair will be mixed with red.

Translated, with the "why" attached

Three stages, and now you know what each is for:

image + scribbles │ ▼ Stage 1: TRIMAP "which pixels can I answer instantly?" │ output: a map of 1 / 0 / φ ▼ Stage 2: OPACITY MAP "for the φ pixels, what is the real α?" │ output: α(x) ∈ [0,1] on the band ▼ Stage 3: MATTING "now composite — without dragging the old bg along" │ output: J(x), the final frame ▼ new frame

Why three stages and not one

Solving 7 unknowns per pixel over a million pixels is hopeless. Stage 1's job is to shrink the problem: it declares 98% of pixels solved ($\alpha = 0$ or $1$, and $f = c$ or $b = c$) and hands stage 2 only the thin ribbon where the answer is genuinely ambiguous. Stage 2 spends its entire budget on that ribbon. Stage 3 then does the actual pasting, and is the only stage that needs the new background at all.

"Scribbles"?

In the classic setup a human drags a few strokes with a mouse — a green stroke on the person, a red stroke on the wall — and that's all the algorithm is told. Everything else is inferred. The scribbles exist only because the lecture assumes no other source of segmentation.

We do not have this problem. Our background-subtraction stage already produced a full binary mask of the person for every frame. So in our project the binary mask replaces the scribbles — it is a vastly richer input than a couple of mouse strokes. Keep this in mind: it lets us skip most of Stage 1.

"so the input image does not necessarily belong to $R^3$"

Meaning: a pixel doesn't have to be described by 3 numbers. You can staple extra channels onto it — for example the 2 numbers of its optical-flow vector, giving a 5-channel pixel. Then the "colour" model also knows about motion, and a person walking in front of a static wall is separable even if he's wearing wall-coloured clothes. All the maths below is unchanged; only the dimensionality of $c$ changes.

5   Stage 1 — Trimap

What we're trying to produce

A picture the same size as the frame, where every pixel is stamped with one of three things: 1 ("definitely person"), 0 ("definitely not"), or $\phi$ ("ask me later"). The $\phi$ pixels form a ribbon around the person's outline.

The lecture builds this ribbon from scratch, starting from nothing but a few scribbles. That takes three sub-steps — a colour model (5a), Bayes (5b), and distance maps (5c) — and it's the longest part of the chapter. Follow it, because the same machinery gets reused in Stage 2 where you genuinely need it.

5a   KDE — what "the probability of a colour" even means

Original text (PDF pages 68–70, first part)

If we had a light foreground and dark background, the most simple approach would be creating a histogram, dividing the values to bins and finding a threshold for which bins belong to the background. However, we are working with at least 3-channel images, so making for example 10 bins in each dimention, we will have in total 1000 bins which is a lot.

We will, therefore, work with what is called Kernel Density Estimation (KDE). This is a method to estimate a distribution from a finite sample set. What we have is the color value $c \in R^3$ for every pixel, and we want to find the color distribution of foreground and background. The scribles themselves indicate we have conditional probability: we have a color given the scribble is foreground or background. More formally: $\forall x \in \Omega_F : c(x) \in f_{C|F}$ and $\forall x \in \Omega_B : c(x) \in f_{C|B}$.

We will start with an example in 1-D. Usually, if we want to create a histogram, we would divide the axis to bins and count how many pixels we had at every bin. Instead, we would draw around each pixel a "bell".

We estimate:

$$ f(c|F) \propto \sum_{x \in \Omega_F} K(c, c(x)) $$

The same will be done with the background. This is sometimes called Parzen window. Usually the function K is: $K(c, c') \propto e^{-\frac{\|c - c'\|_2^2}{2\sigma^2}}$. An ideal histogram would look like: [two non-overlapping bumps]

However, in most cases, we will have a very large overlap. The complexity would be for calculating $f(c|F)$ is $O(|\Omega| \cdot |\Omega_F|)$ since K gets two parameters c and c'. This is too large, and as a result we will do something else. We will assume for now we have calculated them, and later introduce a practical approach for them.

From zero — you have 40 sample colours. Now what?

The user scribbled on the person. You now hold a bag of, say, 400 colours that you know are foreground. You want to turn that bag into a function that answers, for any colour anyone hands you: "how foreground-ish is this colour?" That function is $f(c|F)$.

Attempt 1: a histogram

Chop the colour axis into bins, count how many samples fall in each, done. Two problems, and the lecture names the second one:

  • Hard edges. Two colours a hair's breadth apart can land in different bins and get wildly different answers. The estimate is a staircase, and reality isn't.
  • Emptiness in 3-D. Colour is 3-dimensional. 10 bins per channel = $10^3 = 1000$ bins. With 400 samples, at least 600 bins are empty. A histogram assigns probability exactly zero to every empty bin. So a colour you plausibly should accept — one you just happened not to sample — gets $f(c|F) = 0$, and later, when you divide by it, everything explodes. And 10 bins per channel is already coarse; at a realistic 32 bins you have 32 768 bins and 400 samples. The histogram is basically all zeros.

This is the curse of dimensionality, and it's why the lecture waves the histogram away in one sentence.

Attempt 2: put a bell on every sample

Instead of dropping each sample into a rigid bin, let each sample deposit a small smooth Gaussian bump — a "bell" — centred on its own colour. Then add up all the bells. Where samples cluster, many bells overlap and the sum is tall. Where you had no samples but you're near some, the tails of nearby bells still give you a small positive value instead of zero. Far away from everything, it decays smoothly to near-zero.

$$ f(c|F) \;\propto\; \sum_{x \in \Omega_F} K\big(c,\, c(x)\big), \qquad K(c,c') \propto e^{-\frac{\|c-c'\|^2}{2\sigma^2}} $$

Read the sum literally: "to score the colour $c$, walk over every foreground sample $c(x)$, ask how close it is to $c$, and add up the votes — where a vote decays like a Gaussian with distance." That's it. That's Kernel Density Estimation. It is also called a Parzen window, after the guy who published it; the two names mean the same thing and the lecture uses both.

The $\propto$ ("proportional to") signs

A probability density has to integrate to 1. Neither the kernel nor the sum does, out of the box, so you'd have to divide by a normalising constant. The lecture writes $\propto$ to say "and there's a constant here I'm not going to bother writing." It genuinely doesn't matter, because in the next step the constant cancels in a ratio. (Our code does normalise, one line: lut /= lut.sum().)

What $\sigma$ controls

$\sigma$ is the width of each bell. Too small, and each bell is a needle: your density is a spiky mess that has memorised your samples and generalises to nothing (overfitting). Too large, and everything smears into one featureless mound that can't tell foreground colours from background ones (underfitting). It is the same bias–variance dial as every other smoothing parameter you've ever met.

The complexity remark

To evaluate $f(c|F)$ at one colour you sum over all $|\Omega_F|$ samples. To evaluate it at every pixel of the image you do that $|\Omega|$ times: $O(|\Omega|\cdot|\Omega_F|)$. For a 1-megapixel image and 10 000 scribble pixels that's $10^{10}$ kernel evaluations, per frame. That's the "too large" the lecture mentions, and Section 10.4 (our §8) is entirely about fixing it.

Worked example — KDE by hand, in 1-D

Foreground samples (say, the L channel of three scribble pixels): $\{100,\ 102,\ 140\}$. Take $\sigma = 5$. What is $f(c|F)$ at $c = 101$, and at $c = 120$?

$$ f(101|F) \propto e^{-\frac{(101-100)^2}{2\cdot 25}} + e^{-\frac{(101-102)^2}{2\cdot 25}} + e^{-\frac{(101-140)^2}{2\cdot 25}} = 0.980 + 0.980 + \approx 0 = 1.960 $$
$$ f(120|F) \propto e^{-\frac{400}{50}} + e^{-\frac{324}{50}} + e^{-\frac{400}{50}} = 0.00034 + 0.0016 + 0.00034 = 0.0023 $$

The colour 101 scores ~850× higher than 120 — as it should, since two samples sit right on top of it. But notice that 120 is not zero. A histogram with bins of width 10 would have put 120 in an empty bin and returned a hard 0. The bells' tails keep the estimate alive everywhere, which is exactly what saves you when you later divide by it.

The identity that makes our code work

Summing a bell centred on every sample is mathematically identical to taking the histogram of the samples and blurring it with that bell. Formally, a histogram is a sum of spikes, $h(c) = \sum_i \delta(c - c_i)$, and convolution with a kernel slides the kernel onto every spike:

$$ (K * h)(c) = \sum_i K(c - c_i) = f(c|F) $$

So histogram + Gaussian blur = KDE. Exactly, not approximately. Look at Demo 2: the teal curve is literally the grey bars blurred. This one line is why matting.py can compute KDE in two calls (np.bincount, then cv2.GaussianBlur).

5b   Bayes — flipping the question around

Original text (PDF pages 68–70, continued)

We will develop the equations:

$$ P(F|c) = \frac{f(c|F) P(F)}{f(c)} = \frac{f(c|F) P(F)}{f(c|F) P(F) + f(c|B) P(B)} $$
$$ P(B|c) = \frac{f(c|B) P(B)}{f(c)} = \frac{f(c|B) P(B)}{f(c|F) P(F) + f(c|B) P(B)} $$

The main parameters unkown to us here are P(F) and P(B). In worst case, we can assume uniform distribution: $P(F) = P(B) = \frac{1}{2}$, $\implies P(F|c) = \frac{f(c|F)}{f(c|F) + f(c|B)}$.

From zero — two different questions that look the same

KDE gave you $f(c|F)$: given that a pixel is foreground, how likely is its colour to be $c$?

But standing at a pixel, that is not your question. You are looking at the colour. You want: given that the colour is $c$, how likely is the pixel to be foreground? That is $P(F|c)$. Different question. Bayes' rule is the machine that turns one into the other.

Why they're different — the classic illustration

$P(\text{has four legs} \mid \text{is a dog}) \approx 1$. $P(\text{is a dog} \mid \text{has four legs})$ is small — most four-legged things are not dogs. The conditional bar is not symmetric, and confusing the two directions is the single most common probability error there is.

Bayes, assembled

The rule itself: $P(F|c) = \dfrac{f(c|F)\,P(F)}{f(c)}$. Every piece has a name and a job:

  • $f(c|F)$ — the likelihood. From KDE. "Does this colour fit the foreground model?"
  • $P(F)$ — the prior. Before looking at any colour, what fraction of pixels are foreground?
  • $f(c)$ — the evidence. How likely is this colour at all, from any source? Its only job is to make the numbers sum to 1.
  • $P(F|c)$ — the posterior. The answer.

You never compute $f(c)$ directly. A pixel is either foreground or background, so its colour came from one of the two: $f(c) = f(c|F)P(F) + f(c|B)P(B)$. That is the "law of total probability", and substituting it is the only step between the first equality and the second one in the teal block.

The uniform prior

Setting $P(F) = P(B) = \tfrac12$ means "I refuse to assume anything about how much of the image is person." Then the halves cancel top and bottom and you get the clean form the lecture ends on:

$$ P(F|c) = \frac{f(c|F)}{f(c|F) + f(c|B)} $$

Look at the shape of it: each side gets a vote whose loudness is how well the colour matches its model, and $P(F|c)$ is the foreground's share of the total vote. Because it's a share, it's automatically in $[0,1]$ and you never have to clamp it. Remember this shape — the opacity-map formula in Stage 2 is the exact same shape with the votes made louder or quieter by distance.

Also note this is where the $\propto$ from before stops mattering: any constant multiplying both $f(c|F)$ and $f(c|B)$ cancels in the ratio.

Worked example — Bayes with real numbers

A pixel on the person's shoulder has colour $c$. Your foreground KDE returns $f(c|F) = 0.030$; your background KDE returns $f(c|B) = 0.010$. Uniform priors.

$$ P(F|c) = \frac{0.030}{0.030 + 0.010} = 0.75, \qquad P(B|c) = \frac{0.010}{0.040} = 0.25 $$

Three-to-one in favour of foreground — and they sum to 1, for free. Now a pixel on a brown jacket in front of a brown sofa: $f(c|F) = 0.021$, $f(c|B) = 0.019$. Then $P(F|c) = 0.525$. The colour model has essentially shrugged. That coin-flip is exactly the situation the distance maps in the next section exist to break.

Notation trap #2 — lowercase f vs uppercase P

The lecture writes $f(\cdot)$ for things over colours (continuous — these are densities, they can exceed 1 and only integrate to 1) and $P(\cdot)$ for things over labels F/B (discrete — genuine probabilities in $[0,1]$ that sum to 1). It's a real convention, consistently applied, and once you see it the equations stop looking arbitrary.

5c   Distance maps and the narrow band

Original text (PDF pages 68–70, continued)

Now we will do distance maps similar to video colorization. We had a path $p \in \Omega$ but now:

$$ L(p) = \int_p |\langle W(p(t)), \dot{p}(t) \rangle| dl, \qquad W(x) = \nabla_x p(F|c(x)) $$

We have taken this function because it will be consistently larger in the foreground and smaller in the background. Next:

$$ x, y \in \Omega: d_L(x, y) = \min_{p \in [x, y]} L(p) $$

Where we go over all the points p from x to y and find the minimum. Next:

$$ D_F(x) = d_L(x, \Omega_F) = \min_{y \in \Omega_F} d_L(x, y), \qquad D_B(x) = d_L(x, \Omega_B) = \min_{y \in \Omega_B} d_L(x, y) $$

This will eventually create two regions: the first region would be for the foreground and the second would be for the background.

Within each region, pixels will have similar distance values, whereas crossing between regions leads to a sharp change in distance. Consequently, in each distance map, one region will exhibit relatively small values while the other will have larger values. By comparing the two distance maps, we can determine whether a given pixel belongs to the foreground or the background:

$$ V_F = (x \in \Omega: D_F(x) < D_B(x)), \quad V_B = (x \in \Omega: D_F(x) > D_B(x)) $$

And we can also define the boundary: $\Delta = (x \in \Omega: D_F(x) = D_B(x))$.

Usually, this set will be empty because the probabilty for the distance to be equal is very low. As a result, the boundary can be found using interpolation. Now, we will create a narrow band around the boundary:

$$ B_p(\Delta) = \bigcup_{x \in \Delta} B_\rho(x) $$

Where $B_\rho(x)$ is a ball around x with radius $\rho$. We can also write this as:

$$ x \in B_\rho(\Delta) \iff \exists y \in \Delta : \|x - y\| <= \rho $$

Finally, the Trimap will be:

$$ \forall x \in V_F \setminus B_\rho(\Delta): \alpha_T(x) = 1 $$
$$ \forall x \in V_B \setminus B_\rho(\Delta): \alpha_T(x) = 0 $$
$$ \forall x \in B_\rho(\Delta): \alpha_T(x) = \phi $$
From zero — why colour alone is not enough

End of §5b you had $P(F|c)$ at every pixel. Why not just threshold it at 0.5 and call it a trimap?

Because of the brown jacket on the brown sofa. When foreground and background share colours, the two KDE bumps overlap and $P(F|c) \approx 0.5$ over huge regions — the lecture's own remark, "in most cases we will have a very large overlap." Colour has told you everything it knows and it isn't enough. You need a second, independent kind of evidence, and the obvious one is where the pixel is. A pixel in the middle of the person's chest is foreground even if its colour is ambiguous, simply because it's surrounded by foreground.

Ordinary distance vs geodesic distance

The naive spatial evidence is "how many pixels away is the nearest scribble?" — straight-line, ruler distance. But a ruler happily walks straight through the person's silhouette, as if the edge weren't there. We want a distance that says: going around the edge is cheap, crossing it is expensive.

So define a cost of walking instead. Picture $P(F|c)$ as a landscape: high plateau over the person, low plain over the wall, and a cliff at the silhouette. Now charge yourself for altitude change. Walking along the plateau is free (no altitude change). Walking off the cliff costs a fortune. That is precisely what the integral says:

$$ L(p) = \int_p \big|\langle \underbrace{W(p(t))}_{\nabla P(F|c)},\ \underbrace{\dot{p}(t)}_{\text{your direction}} \rangle\big|\, dl $$

Piece by piece: $p$ is a path you walk. $\dot p$ is the direction you're stepping. $W = \nabla P(F|c)$ is the gradient — it points uphill, and is big only at the cliff, near zero on flat plateau or plain. The inner product $\langle W, \dot p\rangle$ is the altitude change of your step. Absolute value, because climbing and descending both cost. Integrate along the path: $L(p)$ = total altitude you gained and lost.

Then $d_L(x,y) = \min_p L(p)$ is the cheapest route from $x$ to $y$ — the smart route that detours around the cliff if it can. That's a geodesic distance: shortest path in a world where some ground is expensive.

Turning it into the two maps

$D_F(x)$ = cheapest walk from $x$ to any foreground scribble. $D_B(x)$ = same to any background scribble. Two full images of numbers.

Here's the payoff. If $x$ is on the person, you can reach the FG scribble without ever crossing the cliff: $D_F(x)$ is small. To reach a BG scribble you must cross: $D_B(x)$ is large. On the wall, the reverse. So the comparison is trivially informative:

$$ V_F = \{x : D_F(x) < D_B(x)\}, \qquad V_B = \{x : D_F(x) > D_B(x)\}, \qquad \Delta = \{x : D_F(x) = D_B(x)\} $$

Every pixel is now assigned. $\Delta$ is the tie: the ridge line between the two, which is the silhouette.

The lecture's aside — "usually this set will be empty" — is a floating-point remark. $D_F$ and $D_B$ are reals; asking for exact equality on a discrete grid almost never fires. In practice you find where the sign of $D_F - D_B$ flips between neighbouring pixels and interpolate to get a sub-pixel curve.

The band — a formula that looks scarier than it is

$$ B_\rho(\Delta) = \bigcup_{x \in \Delta} B_\rho(x) $$

Take every point $x$ on the boundary curve. Stamp a disk of radius $\rho$ on it. Union of all those disks = a ribbon of width $2\rho$ following the silhouette. The second form, "$x \in B_\rho(\Delta) \iff \exists y \in \Delta: \|x-y\| \le \rho$", says the identical thing backwards: a pixel is in the band iff some boundary point is within $\rho$ of it.

Now stamp the trimap: inside $V_F$ and outside the band $\Rightarrow \alpha_T = 1$. Inside $V_B$ and outside the band $\Rightarrow \alpha_T = 0$. Anywhere in the band $\Rightarrow \alpha_T = \phi$. Done — Stage 1 complete.

Choosing $\rho$

$\rho$ too small: the band is one pixel wide, there's no room to represent a soft hair edge, and you're back to a binary mask. $\rho$ too big: half the image is "undecided" and Stage 2 — which is the expensive stage — has to work everywhere, plus the "nearly 1-D" assumption it relies on collapses. 5–15 px. We use 10.

Where this reappears Note the lecture says "distance maps similar to video colorization." That's the point: the exact same geodesic machinery from Chapter 9 is reused here, with only the cost field swapped — luminance gradient there, foreground-probability gradient here. Learn it once, use it twice.

6   Stage 2 — Opacity map

Original text (PDF pages 71–72)

Let's draw only the area that is interesting for us:

At this stage, we now have a much richer set of data. Previously, we only had a few scribbles indicating foreground and background, which were the sole sources of information for the entire image, despite its large color variability. Now, our focus is on a specific region with much more limited color variation. We have a lot more samples of the foreground and background on the boundary.

Thus, we will estimate again the distribution functions for refinement. And since we have a curve, we can put a coordinate system and measure the length of the curve. We will denote that as $\tau$. Every point we can denote as $(\tau, c(x))$. Since the boundary in very narrow, this problem is nearly 1-D. Now we can develop the equations:

$$ \forall x \in B_\rho(\Delta): \quad \alpha() = \frac{w_F(x)}{x_F(x) + w_B(x)} $$
$$ w_F(x) = \tilde{D}_F(x)^{-r} \tilde{P}_F(x), \qquad r \in (0, 2] $$

Here $\tilde{P}_F(x)$ is computed based on previous step. The equation of $w_F(x)$ was chosen intuitively in the paper.

From zero — why redo the KDE you already did

This paragraph confuses everyone, because it looks like the lecture forgot it already built colour models. It didn't. The point is that the first KDE was global and therefore mediocre, and now you can afford a much better one.

Think about it. A global foreground model has to describe the person's whole body: dark hair, pale skin, blue jeans, white shoes. That's a wide, multi-humped, low-confidence distribution. Ditto the background: bright window, dark door, beige wall. The two overlap heavily and $P(F|c)$ is mush.

But now zoom into a 20-pixel-tall patch of band next to the person's elbow. Within that patch the foreground is only ever "sleeve blue" and the background is only ever "wall beige." Two tight, well-separated bumps. A KDE fitted only to these pixels is dramatically sharper than the global one. That's the "much more limited color variation" sentence, and the tilde in $\tilde P_F$ is the lecture's way of writing "the good local version."

The $\tau$ business is the same observation made formal: parameterise the band by arclength along the boundary curve, and at each $\tau$ the colour problem is one-dimensional (just "how far across the ribbon are you?"). A hard 3-D segmentation problem has become a stack of easy 1-D ones.

Now read the $\alpha$ formula (with the typo fixed)

$$ \alpha(x) = \frac{w_F(x)}{w_F(x) + w_B(x)}, \qquad w_F(x) = \tilde{D}_F(x)^{-r}\,\tilde{P}_F(x), \qquad w_B(x) = \tilde{D}_B(x)^{-r}\,\tilde{P}_B(x) $$

You have seen this shape before — it's the Bayes formula from §5b, "foreground's share of the total vote." The only change is what makes a vote loud:

$$ \underbrace{w_F(x)}_{\substack{\text{how loudly foreground} \\ \text{claims this pixel}}} = \underbrace{\tilde{D}_F(x)^{-r}}_{\substack{\text{proximity:} \\ \text{closer} \Rightarrow \text{louder}}} \times \underbrace{\tilde{P}_F(x)}_{\substack{\text{colour evidence:} \\ \text{better match} \Rightarrow \text{louder}}} $$

Two independent kinds of evidence, multiplied. Multiplication means both must agree: if either the colour says "definitely not foreground" ($\tilde P_F \approx 0$) or the pixel is miles from any confident foreground ($\tilde D_F$ huge, so $\tilde D_F^{-r} \approx 0$), the foreground's vote dies. And because $\alpha$ is a normalised share, it lands in $[0,1]$ with no clamping.

Why the negative exponent, and why $r \le 2$

$D^{-r} = 1/D^r$ — so small distance means a big number. Nearness = loudness. The exponent tunes how brutally geometry overrules colour:

  • $r \to 0$: $D^{-r} \to 1$, distance is ignored, $\alpha$ becomes pure colour classification.
  • $r = 2$: distance dominates hard; a pixel twice as far has a quarter of the vote.
  • $r > 2$ (excluded): geometry annihilates colour completely, and $\alpha$ degenerates into a plain distance ratio across the band — a linear ramp that ignores the image. That's why the paper caps it at 2.

The beautiful part: the formula glues itself to the trimap

Walk to the inner edge of the band, right against the confident foreground. There $\tilde D_F(x) = 0$, so $w_F = 0^{-r} = \infty$, and

$$ \alpha = \frac{\infty}{\infty + w_B} = 1 $$

Walk to the outer edge: $\tilde D_B = 0$, $w_B = \infty$, $\alpha = 0$. So the opacity map automatically agrees with the trimap at both edges of the band, and varies smoothly in between. No seams. This is what the lecture means by "chosen intuitively in the paper" — it wasn't derived from a probabilistic model, it was designed to have exactly these boundary conditions. Knowing that, you could have invented it yourself.

Worked example — three band pixels, $r = 2$

Pixel A, hugging the person: $\tilde D_F = 2$ px, $\tilde D_B = 8$ px, colour is ambiguous ($\tilde P_F = 0.6$).

$$ w_F = 2^{-2}(0.6) = 0.150, \quad w_B = 8^{-2}(0.4) = 0.00625, \quad \alpha = \frac{0.150}{0.15625} = \mathbf{0.96} $$

Geometry wins: almost fully opaque, as it should be. Colour barely mattered.

Pixel B, dead centre of the band: $\tilde D_F = \tilde D_B = 5$ px, same $\tilde P_F = 0.6$.

$$ w_F = 5^{-2}(0.6) = 0.024, \quad w_B = 5^{-2}(0.4) = 0.016, \quad \alpha = \frac{0.024}{0.040} = \mathbf{0.60} $$

Distances cancel exactly, so $\alpha = \tilde P_F$. When geometry is neutral, colour decides — all of it. That's the design working as intended.

Pixel C, centre of the band but colour clearly says wall ($\tilde P_F = 0.1$).

$$ w_F = 5^{-2}(0.1) = 0.004, \quad w_B = 5^{-2}(0.9) = 0.036, \quad \alpha = \frac{0.004}{0.040} = \mathbf{0.10} $$

Mostly transparent. Two pixels at identical positions got $\alpha = 0.60$ and $\alpha = 0.10$ purely from colour. This is how wisps of hair survive: they sit in the middle of the band geometrically, but their colour votes foreground.

How the path integral becomes a graph

Along one step from pixel $u$ to a neighbour $v$, the lecture's integrand $|\langle \nabla P, \dot p\rangle|\,dl$ is just the change in posterior across the step, $|P(v) - P(u)|$. Summing steps gives the path cost; minimising over paths is shortest-path; shortest-path with non-negative costs is Dijkstra (multi-source: seed all rim pixels at distance 0, grow one wavefront). The tiny $\text{GEO\_EPS}\cdot dl$ regularizer handles flat regions, where the lecture's cost would be 0 for every path: there the distance gracefully falls back to scaled Euclidean. The graph only spans band ∪ rims — exact, since any path to the confident interior crosses the rim first — which is what keeps Dijkstra fast enough to run twice per frame.

Why $d^{-2}$ needs no guard, and why there is no blur or clamp

The lecture's boundary condition — $\tilde D_F \to 0 \Rightarrow w_F \to \infty \Rightarrow \alpha = 1$, seamlessly joining the trimap — used to require fudges when the distances could be exactly 0 inside the map. Here the sources are the rims, not band pixels, so every band distance is at least one step: $d \ge \text{GEO\_EPS} > 0$, always finite, yet spanning ~6 orders of magnitude of $d^{-2}$ across the band. α snaps to 1 against the core and 0 against the background on its own. The old de-speckling blur and the two boundary clamps became dead code and were removed: the geodesic α is smooth because the distance field itself is smooth, and the confident regions are never written over ($\alpha_{roi}$ starts as fg_core and only band pixels are assigned).

Why Lab colour space

The kernel $K(c,c') \propto e^{-\|c-c'\|^2/2\sigma^2}$ measures colour difference with a Euclidean norm and one $\sigma$ for all directions. That's only sane if equal distances look equally different to a human eye. In BGR they don't — a distance of 6 is a big change in the dark and imperceptible in the bright. CIE-Lab is designed to be roughly perceptually uniform, so a single $\sigma$ means the same thing everywhere. Bonus: it splits lightness (L) from chroma (a, b), which is exactly the split the background-subtraction stage used to kill shadows.

The ROI crop

All of the above runs inside bbox_of_mask(mask) padded by $\text{SAMPLE\_RADIUS} + \rho + 5$ pixels, not on the full frame. The band lives near the person; there is no reason to run distance transforms over a wall. This is the single biggest speedup in the file, and it's free — the alpha outside the box is 0 by construction.

7   Stage 3 — Matting, and the halo

Original text (PDF pages 72–73)

Now we are given:
B - new background
$\alpha$ - opacity map
c - original image.
As said earlier, we could do: $J(x) = \alpha c(x) + (1 - \alpha(x)) B(x)$.

This will be done on the foreground and background, but not on the boundary. That is because the colors will mix and we even may get a halo around the foreground. We will do something else then. Suppose we have the point x:

We can write down the value of the pixel as:

$$ c(x) = \alpha f(x) + (1 - \alpha(x)) b(x) $$

Where in this case f(x) and b(x) are some unkown foreground and background values respectively. Our goal would be to estimate them. This is an ill-problem (one equation with 2 unknowns), but we have a lot of colors around the pixel that we know they are foreground and background. We will open a window around the pixel, and claim that there are two pixels in the window sutisfying the equation above.

On a very small area this assumption most of the time works. What we will do is each time take two pixels in the window, compute the value according to the equasion earlier, substract c(x), calculate the norm and find those which minimize the result:

$$ (x_F^*, x_B^*) = \arg\min_{\substack{x_F \in N(x) \cap \{x:\alpha(x)=1\} \\ x_B \in N(x) \cap \{x:\alpha(x)=0\}}} \|\alpha(x) c(x_F) + (1 - \alpha(x)) c(x_B) - c(x)\|^2 $$

With that we can take the color in $x_F^*$ as the value of the foreground object than c(x):

$$ J(x) = \alpha c(x_F^*) + (1 - \alpha(x)) B(x) $$
From zero — you already derived this in §2

The compositing equation reappears, and now the lecture calls it what it is: one equation, two unknown colours. (Really 3 equations and 7 unknowns as we counted, but $\alpha$ is now known from Stage 2, leaving 3 equations and 6 unknowns. Still hopeless on its own.)

The trick is a locality prior. You don't know $f(x)$, but you're standing one or two pixels from a mob of pixels that are confidently pure foreground. The person's sleeve doesn't change colour over two pixels. So: whatever $f(x)$ is, it's probably the colour of one of those neighbours. Same for $b(x)$ and the confident background neighbours. That converts an impossible continuous estimation into a finite search over pairs you can actually enumerate.

Reading the $\arg\min$ out loud

Open a window $N(x)$ around the pixel. Inside it:

  • List the confident-foreground pixels: $N(x) \cap \{\alpha = 1\}$. Say 30 of them.
  • List the confident-background pixels: $N(x) \cap \{\alpha = 0\}$. Say 30 of them.
  • For each of the $30 \times 30 = 900$ pairs $(x_F, x_B)$, pretend those are the true pure colours and predict what the camera would have recorded: $\hat c = \alpha\, c(x_F) + (1-\alpha)\, c(x_B)$.
  • Compare with the colour you actually recorded: $\|\hat c - c(x)\|^2$.
  • Keep the pair with the smallest error. Call them $x_F^*, x_B^*$.

You have found the pair of real, pure, nearby colours that best explains the muddy pixel you observed. That's the whole idea, and $\arg\min$ just means "the arguments that minimise", i.e. return the winning pair rather than the winning error value.

The payoff line

$$ J(x) = \alpha\, c(x_F^*) + (1-\alpha)\, B(x) $$

Compare it to the naive blend, $J = \alpha\,\mathbf{c(x)} + (1-\alpha)B$. One symbol changed. Instead of the contaminated pixel $c(x)$, you composite the pure recovered foreground colour $c(x_F^*)$. And from §2 you know exactly what that buys you: the naive version carried an error term $\alpha(1-\alpha)(b - f)$, the old background bleeding through, peaking at $\alpha = 0.5$ along a ring around the person. That ring is the halo. Composite the pure colour and the term vanishes identically.

"Light hair on a red background": $c(x)$ at a hair pixel is pinkish. Paste pinkish onto green grass and the hair looks sunburnt. Search the window, find a pure-white hair pixel and a pure-red wall pixel that together explain the pinkish value, throw away the red, and composite the pure white onto the grass.

What our code does — the window search, implemented

This is _pure_composite() in Code/matting.py, run on the band pixels of every frame (confident regions don't need it: there $J = c$ or $J = B$ exactly):

band_yx = np.column_stack(np.nonzero(band))
idx_f = cKDTree(np.column_stack(np.nonzero(rim_f))).query(band_yx, k=kf)[1]   # N(x) ∩ {α=1}
idx_b = cKDTree(np.column_stack(np.nonzero(rim_b))).query(band_yx, k=kb)[1]   # N(x) ∩ {α=0}
cand_f = frame_f[rim_f][idx_f].reshape(n, kf, 3)
cand_b = frame_f[rim_b][idx_b].reshape(n, kb, 3)

recon = a * cand_f[:, :, None, :] + (1.0 - a) * cand_b[:, None, :, :]
err = ((recon - frame_f[band][:, None, None, :]) ** 2).sum(-1)
best_f = err.reshape(n, -1).argmin(1) // kb          # x_F* — the argmin's FG member
pure_f = cand_f[np.arange(n), best_f]                # c(x_F*) — the recovered pure colour
return a1 * pure_f + (1.0 - a1) * bg_roi[band]       # J = α·c(x_F*) + (1−α)·B_new

Reading it against the lecture: the k-d tree query is "open a window around the pixel" — the nearest confident FG/BG pixels, 7 of each, for every band pixel at once. The broadcast builds all $7 \times 7 = 49$ candidate reconstructions per pixel, the argmin picks the pair that best explains the observed colour, and the composite uses the recovered pure $c(x_F^*)$ — so the halo term $\alpha(1-\alpha)(b_{\text{old}} - f)$ from §2 vanishes identically instead of being argued away.

Two implementation notes. The search and the blend run in BGR, not Lab: the compositing equation $c = \alpha f + (1-\alpha)b$ is a physical statement about how light mixes, true in the sensor's (linear-ish) colour space and false after Lab's non-linear warp. And $x_B^*$ is found but never used — the lecture composites onto the new background, so the best background candidate's only job was to help explain the mixture.

If you are asked this in the exam Say: "Naive blending leaves $\alpha(1-\alpha)(b-f)$ of the old background in the result — a halo, maximal at $\alpha = 0.5$. Fix it by searching a local window for the pair of confident FG/BG pixels that best explains the observed mixed colour, then compositing the recovered pure foreground colour $c(x_F^*)$ instead of the observed $c(x)$." — which is exactly what the code does.

8   Making KDE fast (low rank)

Original text (PDF pages 73–74)

Now we are only left to calculate KDE efficiently. We want to calculate $\forall x \in \Omega$:

$$ f(x) = \sum_{y \in \Omega'} k(x, y) w(y) $$

The computation efficiency here is $O(|\Omega| \cdot |\Omega'|)$. We assume the kernel is low-rank. To understand this, lets assume we have:

$$ f_{n \times 1} = K_{n \times m} w_{m \times 1} $$

Low rank means: $\text{rank}(K) = p \ll m, n$.

The computation efficiency here is O(mn). We can write this as a sum of matrices with rank 1:

$$ K = \sum_{i=1}^p \phi_i \psi_i^T = \Phi_{n \times p} \Psi_{m \times p}^T $$

The rank of this matrix is maximum p. Now we will insert this to previous equation: $f = \Phi \Psi^T w$.

The computation time of $\Psi^T w$ is $O(pm)$, where p is very small. If we denote $a = \Psi^T w$, then $f = \Phi a$ will take $O(pn)$. In total we will have $O(p(m+n))$. Insead of quadratic complexity, we have linear complexity. If we say that our kernel $K(x,y)$ is low rank, then: $p \ll |\Omega|, |\Omega'|$.

we can write it as:

$$ K(x, y) = \sum_{i=1}^p \phi_i(x) \psi_i^T(y), \qquad a_i = \sum_{y \in \Omega'} \Psi_i(y) w(y), \qquad f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^p a_i \phi_i(x) $$

The calculation of $a_i$ takes $O(|\Omega'|p)$, the calculation of the last equation also takes $O(|\Omega|p)$ - in total $O((|\Omega| + |\Omega'|)p)$. Thus there is a lot of consideration when choosing the kernel and to pick one which is low rank. Gaussian kernel is for example infinite-rank, but if we take its singular values we will see they decay to 0. We will not discuss this in the course, but the methods for factorizing kernels is called Fast multiple method.

From zero — this whole section is one matrix trick

Forget KDE for a second. The sum $f(x) = \sum_{y} k(x,y) w(y)$ is literally a matrix–vector product. Stack the $n$ output values into a vector $f$, the $m$ sample weights into $w$, and put $k(x_i, y_j)$ in row $i$, column $j$ of a matrix $K$. Then $f = Kw$. And a dense $n \times m$ matrix–vector product costs $O(nm)$ multiplications. With $n$ = a million pixels and $m$ = ten thousand samples, that's $10^{10}$. Too slow.

What "low rank" means, concretely

A rank-1 matrix is one you can write as a column times a row: $\phi\psi^T$. It has $n \times m$ entries but only $n + m$ numbers of actual content — every column is a rescaling of the same $\phi$. A rank-$p$ matrix is a sum of $p$ of those. "Low rank" means $p \ll n, m$: a huge matrix with very little real information in it.

Why that makes it fast — the associativity trick

Suppose $K = \Phi \Psi^T$ with $\Phi$ being $n \times p$ and $\Psi$ being $m \times p$. Then

$$ f = Kw = \underbrace{\Phi\,\big(\Psi^T w\big)}_{\text{cheap}} \quad\text{instead of}\quad \underbrace{\big(\Phi \Psi^T\big)\, w}_{\text{expensive}} $$

Same answer — matrix multiplication is associative. Utterly different cost:

  • Compute $a = \Psi^T w$ first. That's $(p \times m)(m \times 1)$: costs $O(pm)$. The result $a$ is only $p$ numbers long.
  • Then $f = \Phi a$. That's $(n \times p)(p \times 1)$: costs $O(pn)$.
  • Total $O(p(n+m))$ instead of $O(nm)$. With $p = 5$, $n = 10^6$, $m = 10^4$: five million operations instead of ten billion. Two thousand times faster.

The physical reading: $a_i = \sum_y \psi_i(y) w(y)$ is a summary of all the samples — $p$ numbers that capture everything the samples have to say. Then each output pixel consults the summary instead of the raw samples. Summarise once, reuse a million times.

The catch, and the punchline

Is the Gaussian kernel low rank? Strictly, no — as a continuous operator it has infinite rank. But its singular values decay to zero fast, so truncating after $p$ of them gives a near-perfect approximation. That's the lecture's last sentence. The family of algorithms that finds such factorisations on the fly is the Fast Multipole Method (the PDF's "Fast multiple method" is a typo), one of the top-10 algorithms of the 20th century, and safely out of scope.

The general lesson

Whenever a computation looks like "every output touches every input," check whether the coupling matrix has low effective rank. If it does, $O(nm) \to O((n+m)p)$. The same trick shows up in low-rank texture models, PCA, separable filters (a 2-D Gaussian blur is rank-1, which is why every library does it as two 1-D passes), and the attention mechanism in transformers.

What our code does — this exact scheme, with bins as the basis

We implement the lecture's factorisation, choosing the $\psi_i$ basis pragmatically: colour is already quantised. Bin the Lab cube into $64^3$ voxels and let $\psi_i$ be the indicator of voxel $i$. Then $a_i = \sum_y \psi_i(y)$ is just the 3-D histogram of the samples, and the second half, $f = \sum_i a_i\,\phi_i$ with $\phi_i(c) = K(c, \text{bin}_i)$, is a convolution of that histogram with the Gaussian bell:

def _kde_3d(lab_samples):
    """Joint 3-D KDE over Lab from uint8 samples (N, 3), evaluated with the
    binned low-rank scheme of lecture 10.4. Returns a KDE_BINS^3 density."""
    q = (lab_samples >> 2).astype(np.int32)                     # 256 levels → 64 bins/channel
    idx = (q[:, 0] * KDE_BINS + q[:, 1]) * KDE_BINS + q[:, 2]
    hist = np.bincount(idx, minlength=KDE_BINS ** 3).astype(np.float32)   # aᵢ = Ψᵀw  — O(m)
    density = gaussian_filter(hist.reshape(KDE_BINS, KDE_BINS, KDE_BINS), KDE_SIGMA)  # Φa — O(bins)
    density /= density.sum() + EPS
    return density + EPS         # never return 0 — we divide by (f_F + f_B) later

And here the low-rank story closes on itself: the reason scipy.ndimage.gaussian_filter can process a $64^3$ volume in milliseconds is that the 3-D Gaussian is itself rank-1 along each axis — $e^{-\|d\|^2/2\sigma^2} = e^{-d_L^2/2\sigma^2}\,e^{-d_a^2/2\sigma^2}\,e^{-d_b^2/2\sigma^2}$ — so it runs as three 1-D passes. The separable-filter trick the lecture cites as an example of low-rank structure is the very thing that makes our evaluation cheap. Total cost per class per frame: $O(|\Omega'| + \text{bins})$, the lecture's promised linear complexity.

Per pixel, evaluation is one index

q  = lab_roi >> 2                             # same quantisation as the grid
pf = f_f[q[..., 0], q[..., 1], q[..., 2]]     # fancy-index: whole image in one lookup
pb = f_b[q[..., 0], q[..., 1], q[..., 2]]
return pf / (pf + pb)                         # Bayes, uniform priors

Because the density is joint, there is no per-channel product, hence no underflow to fight and no log-sum-exp machinery — the ratio of two floored densities is numerically safe as written. (An earlier version of this code used three 1-D KDEs multiplied together — naive Bayes. That silently assumes the Lab channels are independent given the class, an assumption the lecture never makes, and it needed a page of log-space stabilisation to work. The joint density is both more faithful and simpler.)

Rules of thumb (from the lecture)
  • Scribble inside each colour region of FG and BG. One per region is the minimum; more is safer. A stroke straddling two regions is worse than useless.
  • Boundary band radius $\rho \approx 5$–$15$ px. Smaller = sharper but misses hair; larger = smoother but expensive, and breaks the near-1-D assumption.
  • Use BOTH local KDE refinement and distance for the opacity map. Colour alone fails on brown-on-brown; distance alone fails on hair. Multiplied, they cover for each other.
  • Don't blend the original pixel directly at the boundary — recover the pure foreground colour first, or accept a halo of size $\alpha(1-\alpha)\|b-f\|$.
  • Add optical flow as an extra channel in video. It separates a moving person from a static background even when their colours coincide.
  • Quantised data kills the complexity problem. If your kernel's input has 256 possible values, precompute all 256 answers. The low-rank story is for continuous domains.

Likely questions, and what to say

"Why can't you just use a binary mask?"

Because a pixel on the silhouette physically is a mixture: a sensor well averages all light landing in it, and at an edge it collects both foreground and background. Forcing a binary answer either glues a fringe of old background onto the subject or shaves pixels off it. $\alpha$ is the mixing fraction, and matting is the problem of estimating it.

"Why is matting ill-posed?"

The compositing equation $c = \alpha f + (1-\alpha)b$ gives 3 equations per pixel (one per colour channel) and has 7 unknowns ($f$: 3, $b$: 3, $\alpha$: 1). Under-determined at every pixel. Every stage of the algorithm is a prior that supplies missing information: the trimap fixes $\alpha$ where it's obvious, the colour models constrain $f$ and $b$, the distance maps add spatial evidence, the window search supplies $f$ from neighbours.

"What is KDE and why not a histogram?"

KDE estimates a density by placing a Gaussian bell on each sample and summing: $f(c|F) \propto \sum_{x\in\Omega_F} K(c, c(x))$. A histogram has hard bin edges and, in 3-D, ~$10^3$ mostly-empty bins, so it assigns probability exactly zero to unseen colours — fatal, since we later divide by it. KDE is smooth and strictly positive everywhere. Implementation note: summing bells on samples is identical to convolving the sample histogram with the bell, so "histogram + Gaussian blur" computes KDE exactly.

"Why do you need distance maps if you have $P(F|c)$?"

Colour distributions overlap. A brown jacket in front of a brown sofa gives $P(F|c) \approx 0.5$ everywhere — the colour model has no information. Distance supplies independent, spatial evidence. The geodesic distance specifically measures "cheapest walk," where crossing a foreground-probability cliff is expensive, so it routes around object boundaries rather than through them.

"Explain $w_F = \tilde D_F^{-r}\tilde P_F$ and why $r \in (0,2]$."

It's a vote. Loudness = colour evidence × proximity. The negative exponent makes near pixels loud. $\alpha$ is the foreground's share of the total vote, so it's automatically in $[0,1]$. At the band's inner edge $\tilde D_F = 0$ so $w_F = \infty$ and $\alpha = 1$; at the outer edge $\alpha = 0$ — so the opacity map joins the trimap continuously, which is why this particular form was chosen. As $r \to 0$ distance is ignored and $\alpha$ becomes pure colour classification; beyond $r = 2$ geometry annihilates colour and $\alpha$ degenerates into a linear distance ramp that ignores the image.

"Why the low-rank trick?"

$f = Kw$ with a dense $n \times m$ kernel matrix costs $O(nm)$. If $K = \Phi\Psi^T$ with rank $p \ll n,m$, reassociate: compute $a = \Psi^T w$ once in $O(pm)$ — a $p$-number summary of all the samples — then $f = \Phi a$ in $O(pn)$. Total $O(p(n+m))$: linear, not quadratic. The Gaussian is formally infinite-rank but its singular values decay, so a small $p$ suffices. The algorithms that do this are Fast Multipole Methods.

"Why does the naive blend produce a halo? Show it."

Substitute $c = \alpha f + (1-\alpha) b$ into $J = \alpha c + (1-\alpha)B$ and compare with the correct $\alpha f + (1-\alpha)B$. The difference is $\alpha(1-\alpha)(b-f)$: the old background survives, weighted by $\alpha(1-\alpha)$, which is zero in the confident regions and maximal ($0.25$) at $\alpha = 0.5$ — a ring around the subject. Fix: recover pure $f$ via the window search and composite that instead.
